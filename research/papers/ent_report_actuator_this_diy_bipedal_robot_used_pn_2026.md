## 概述
Shadow Walker是Shadow Group的核心项目，由Richard Greenhill发起，David Buckley基于医学教科书设计。机器人采用枫木骨架，下肢简化至单骨和宽趾，踝关节双轴设计提供2自由度，膝关节无髌骨。28个气动肌肉（仿McKibben肌肉）通过压缩空气驱动8个关节（髋、膝、踝、趾），共12自由度。无头躯干容纳控制阀、电子设备和计算机接口。团队成功让机器人稳定站立并抵抗轻微推力，但行走挑战巨大。Rich Walker加入后尝试用神经网络解决平衡问题，但受限于传感器和阀门不可靠及整体脆弱性。1990年，Shadow Walker参加首届国际机器人奥林匹克，因未能行走而落败，现藏于伦敦科学博物馆。

## 核心内容
### 背景与团队
- 1987年，英国摄影师Richard Greenhill（无机器人学背景）在初创公司Intergalactic Robots工作期间，决定自建能执行实用任务（如搬运行李）的真人大小人形机器人。
- 他组织每周三晚的聚会，妻子Sally提供意大利面，团队从旧打印机和垃圾场收集零件，自称“Shadow Group”。
- 核心成员David Buckley（机器人及动画电子专家）基于医学教科书设计人体骨骼与肌肉结构。

### 设计与架构
- **骨架**：枫木材质，下肢极度简化——每腿仅一根骨骼，每脚仅一个宽趾。
- **关节**：踝关节双轴设计（2自由度），膝关节无髌骨；共8个关节（髋、膝、踝、趾），总计12自由度。
- **驱动系统**：28个“空气肌肉”（仿1950年代McKibben肌肉），通过压缩空气伸缩控制运动，完全替代电机。
- **物理参数**：高168厘米，宽46厘米，重约38公斤；无头躯干容纳控制阀、电子设备和计算机接口。

### 实验与挑战
- **站立与平衡**：团队成功实现可靠站立和自我平衡，轻微推力下能恢复重心。
- **行走失败**：行走是主要难题。Rich Walker（青少年时加入）编写软件，尝试用神经网络解决平衡，但受限于传感器和阀门不可靠、整体脆弱性。
- **软件进展**：Walker和团队开发了标准控制例程库，1999年撰写详细描述（现存于David Buckley网站）。

### 历史背景与竞赛
- 同期工业人形机器人进展：Honda自1986年开发E系列和P系列，1996年推出P2（高183厘米，重210公斤），首个实现稳定自主行走的人形机器人，后发展为ASIMO。
- 1990年9月27-28日，首届国际机器人奥林匹克在格拉斯哥举行，由Turing Institute和University of Strathclyde主办，Peter Mowforth组织。
- 竞赛开放报名，无预设类别，50多台机器人参赛（来自加拿大、法国、印度、日本、墨西哥、苏联、美国、英国、南斯拉夫等）。
- Shadow Walker在双足赛跑中未能迈出一步，冠军由Cardiff University Biped获得；整体冠军为日本筑波大学的Yamabico。

### 遗产与影响
- Shadow Group于1997年注册为Shadow Robot公司（英国最老机器人公司），Rich Walker后任技术总监及董事。
- 公司转向耐用机械手（而非行走机器人），经典模型含20个电机、24自由度，放弃气动肌肉。
- Shadow Walker现藏于伦敦科学博物馆，其行走困难凸显了机器人掌握低级技能的固有难度。
- 2025年8月北京世界人形机器人运动会中，机器人已能参与体操、足球、酒店清洁等任务，但实用人形机器人仍远未成熟。

## Overview

In 1987, Richard Greenhill , a British photographer who was fascinated by (but had no actual training in) robotics, decided he wanted to build a life-size humanoid that could do useful things, like carrying luggage. He was working at a startup called Intergalactic Robots, but he couldn’t convince anyone there to build such a machine, so he set about building one himself, in his attic. To help with his project, he organized a weekly get-together of a dozen or so like-minded folks. Every Wednesday night, his wife, Sally, would make a big pot of spaghetti, and the group would tinker with components scavenged from old printers and picked up from junkyards. They called themselves the Shadow Group. They eventually constructed several different robots, but their main project was the two-legged Shadow Walker. In 1987, photographer Richard Greenhill organized a weekly gathering of DIY enthusiasts to work on projects in his attic, including the Shadow Walker. Richard Greenhill and David Buckley Greenhill’s friend David Buckley , a robotics and animatronics expert he’d met at Intergalactic, sketched out a rough design based on medical textbooks of human bone structure and muscle movement. The robot’s skeleton, made of maple, was greatly simplified—only one bone in the lower leg and a single wide toe on each foot. The ankle’s double-axis design allowed for two degrees of movement. The knee had no complicating kneecap. Greenhill didn’t want the robot to use motors, so its movement was controlled using compressed air to extend and contract 28 “air-muscles”—his version of a McKibben muscle, invented in the 1950s to mimic musculature with pneumatics. The muscles were connected to the bones across eight joints (hips, knees, ankles, toes), which provided 12 degrees of freedom. The group managed to get the robot to stand up reliably and balance itself; it could even regain its center if pushed a little. But walking turned out to be more of a challenge. The Legacy of Shadow Walker In 1997, a paying customer in search of a robotic leg compelled the Shadow Group to get serious and become a registered company. Shadow Robot is now Britain’s oldest robotics company. Rich Walker, who had left the Shadow Group to earn a B.A. in mathematics and a diploma in computer science at the University of Cambridge, joined Shadow Robot in 1999 as technical director. Today he’s the director of the company. Shadow Robot specializes in durable robot hands rather than walking robots. But the focus on hands is also a legacy of the Shadow Group. Walker remembers that the Shadow Group’s first humanoid hand in the late 1990s was impressive simply for being able to pick up a pint of beer (a smooth-sided, thin-walled glass). Today, Shadow Robot’s hands are testbeds for dexterity . Gone are the pneumatic muscles, replaced by actuators that move each finger with precision. The classic model contains 20 motors, allowing for abductive and adductive movement with 24 degrees of freedom. Shadow Walker’s operator wore a data suit that captured his movements and allowed the robot to copy them. Richard Greenhill In a recent blog post , Sejal Parsotomo, senior marketing executive at Shadow Robot, wrote that while humanoid robots are great for public relations, specialized dexterity is key for success: A robot that can walk into your factory may be impressive, but a robot that can reliably manipulate objects is transformative. In its struggles to take more than a few steps, the Shadow Walker showed the inherent difficulty that robots had in mastering even low-level skills. In August 2025, Beijing hosted the World Humanoid Robot Games . Competing in sports such as gymnastics, soccer, and track events, as well as more “useful” tasks like hotel cleaning and sorting medicine, these robots could literally have run circles around the competitors in the first Robot Olympics 35 years earlier. And yet, there is still so much work needed in order for robots to navigate the human-built environment. Despite the astonishing progress, we’re still not all that close to actually useful humanoid robots. ## Content
In 1987, Richard Greenhill, a British photographer who was fascinated by (but had no actual training in) robotics, decided he wanted to build a life-size humanoid that could do useful things, like carrying luggage. He was working at a startup called Intergalactic Robots, but he couldn’t convince anyone there to build such a machine, so he set about building one himself, in his attic. To help with his project, he organized a weekly get-together of a dozen or so like-minded folks. Every Wednesday night, his wife, Sally, would make a big pot of spaghetti, and the group would tinker with components scavenged from old printers and picked up from junkyards. They called themselves the Shadow Group. They eventually constructed several different robots, but their main project was the two-legged Shadow Walker. In 1987, photographer Richard Greenhill organized a weekly gathering of DIY enthusiasts to work on projects in his attic, including the Shadow Walker. Richard Greenhill and David Buckley Greenhill’s friend David Buckley, a robotics and animatronics expert he’d met at Intergalactic, sketched out a rough design based on medical textbooks of human bone structure and muscle movement. The robot’s skeleton, made of maple, was greatly simplified—only one bone in the lower leg and a single wide toe on each foot. The ankle’s double-axis design allowed for two degrees of movement. The knee had no complicating kneecap. Greenhill didn’t want the robot to use motors, so its movement was controlled using compressed air to extend and contract 28 “air-muscles”—his version of a McKibben muscle, invented in the 1950s to mimic musculature with pneumatics. The muscles were connected to the bones across eight joints (hips, knees, ankles, toes), which provided 12 degrees of freedom. It stood 168 centimeters tall and 46 cm wide and weighed about 38 kilograms. The group managed to get the robot to stand up reliably and balance itself; it could even regain its center if pushed a little. But walking turned out to be more of a challenge. Rich Walker joined the group as a teenager and began writing software to get the robot to stand. He was particularly interested in using neural networks to solve balancing problems, although he ran into a number of hardware obstacles, including the unreliability of the sensors and the valves, and the robot’s overall fragility. Over time, Walker and the team developed a standard library of routines to control the robot. Walker wrote a detailed description of the Shadow Walker in 1999, which is available on David Buckley’s website. The 1st International Robot Olympics By the time the Shadow Group began developing Shadow Walker, engineers in academia and industry had been working on robotics for several decades. The world’s first industrial robot, the Unimate, debuted in 1961, and in 1967 Donald Michie and others began building a series of Freddy robots to investigate machine intelligence. The IEEE created its first dedicated robotics organization in 1984 when it established the IEEE Robotics and Automation Council, which became the IEEE Robotics and Automation Society in 1987. Also in 1987, the nonprofit International Federation of Robotics was established to promote research, development, use, and cooperation in the field of robotics. As Shadow Walker pushed the limits for a DIY humanoid robot, industrial humanoids were also gaining ground. In 1986, Honda began working on its experimental (E-series) and later the prototype (P-series) humanoid robots, finally unveiling the P2 in 1996. The P2 stood 183 cm tall and weighed 210 kg. It was the first humanoid capable of stable, autonomous walking. This work eventually led to the development of the groundbreaking ASIMO. ## 개요
Shadow Walker는 Shadow Group의 핵심 프로젝트로, Richard Greenhill이 시작하고 David Buckley가 의학 교과서를 기반으로 설계했다. 로봇은 단풍나무 골격을 사용하며, 하지는 단일 뼈와 넓은 발가락으로 단순화되었고, 발목 관절은 이중 축 설계로 2자유도를 제공하며, 무릎 관절에는 슬개골이 없다. 28개의 공압 근육(McKibben 근육 모방)이 압축 공기를 통해 8개 관절(고관절, 무릎, 발목, 발가락)을 구동하여 총 12자유도를 구현한다. 머리 없는 몸통에는 제어 밸브, 전자 장치 및 컴퓨터 인터페이스가 들어 있다. 팀은 로봇이 안정적으로 서서 가벼운 충격에도 저항하도록 만드는 데 성공했지만, 보행은 큰 도전 과제였다. Rich Walker가 합류한 후 신경망으로 균형 문제를 해결하려 했지만, 센서와 밸브의 불안정성 및 전체적인 취약성에 제약을 받았다. 1990년, Shadow Walker는 제1회 국제 로봇 올림픽에 참가했지만 보행에 실패하여 패배했고, 현재 런던 과학 박물관에 소장되어 있다.

## 핵심 내용
### 배경과 팀
- 1987년, 영국 사진작가 Richard Greenhill(로봇공학 배경 없음)은 스타트업 Intergalactic Robots에서 근무하던 중, 짐 운반 같은 실용적인 작업을 수행할 수 있는 실물 크기 인간형 로봇을 직접 만들기로 결심했다.
- 그는 매주 수요일 저녁 모임을 조직했고, 아내 Sally가 파스타를 제공했으며, 팀은 오래된 프린터와 폐차장에서 부품을 수집해 스스로를 "Shadow Group"이라 불렀다.
- 핵심 멤버 David Buckley(로봇 및 애니메트로닉스 전자 전문가)는 의학 교과서를 기반으로 인체 골격과 근육 구조를 설계했다.

### 설계와 아키텍처
- **골격**: 단풍나무 재질, 하지는 극도로 단순화됨—각 다리는 단일 뼈, 각 발은 넓은 발가락 하나만 존재.
- **관절**: 발목 관절은 이중 축 설계(2자유도), 무릎 관절에는 슬개골 없음; 총 8개 관절(고관절, 무릎, 발목, 발가락), 총 12자유도.
- **구동 시스템**: 28개의 "공기 근육"(1950년대 McKibben 근육 모방)이 압축 공기의 신축으로 운동을 제어하며, 전동기를 완전히 대체.
- **물리적 사양**: 높이 168cm, 너비 46cm, 무게 약 38kg; 머리 없는 몸통에는 제어 밸브, 전자 장치 및 컴퓨터 인터페이스가 내장.

### 실험과 도전
- **서기와 균형**: 팀은 안정적인 서기와 자가 균형을 성공적으로 구현했으며, 가벼운 충격에도 무게 중심을 회복할 수 있었다.
- **보행 실패**: 보행이 주요 난제였다. Rich Walker(청소년기에 합류)는 소프트웨어를 작성하고 신경망으로 균형을 해결하려 했지만, 센서와 밸브의 불안정성 및 전체적인 취약성에 제약을 받았다.
- **소프트웨어 진전**: Walker와 팀은 표준 제어 루틴 라이브러리를 개발했고, 1999년에 상세 설명을 작성했다(현재 David Buckley 웹사이트에 보존).

### 역사적 배경과 대회
- 같은 시기 산업용 인간형 로봇의 진전: Honda는 1986년부터 E시리즈와 P시리즈를 개발했고, 1996년에 P2(높이 183cm, 무게 210kg)를 출시—최초로 안정적인 자율 보행을 구현한 인간형 로봇으로, 이후 ASIMO로 발전.
- 1990년 9월 27-28일, 제1회 국제 로봇 올림픽이 글래스고에서 열렸으며, Turing Institute와 University of Strathclyde가 주최하고 Peter Mowforth가 조직.
- 대회는 사전 카테고리 없이 개방 등록으로 진행되었고, 50여 대의 로봇이 참가(캐나다, 프랑스, 인도, 일본, 멕시코, 소련, 미국, 영국, 유고슬라비아 등).
- Shadow Walker는 이족 보행 경주에서 한 걸음도 내딛지 못했고, 우승은 Cardiff University Biped가 차지; 전체 우승은 일본 츠쿠바 대학의 Yamabico.

### 유산과 영향
- Shadow Group은 1997년에 Shadow Robot 회사(영국에서 가장 오래된 로봇 회사)로 등록되었고, Rich Walker는 이후 기술 이사 및 이사로 활동.
- 회사는 보행 로봇 대신 내구성 있는 로봇 손으로 방향을 전환—클래식 모델은 20개 모터, 24자유도를 포함하며 공압 근육을 포기.
- Shadow Walker는 현재 런던 과학 박물관에 소장되어 있으며, 그 보행 어려움은 로봇이 저수준 기술을 습득하는 데 내재된 어려움을 부각.
- 2025년 8월 베이징 세계 인간형 로봇 운동회에서 로봇은 체조, 축구, 호텔 청소 등 작업에 참여할 수 있었지만, 실용적인 인간형 로봇은 여전히 성숙과는 거리가 멀다.
