Overview

The Humanoid Foundation Model Benchmark is an independent, free, and publicly accessible benchmark platform launched by humanoid.guide on April 14, 2026, designed to rate and compare AI foundation models for humanoid robots. It employs a five-point scoring system, evaluating models across ten capability dimensions: locomotion, whole-body control, bimanual coordination, dexterous manipulation, navigation, reasoning, simulation-to-real transfer, cross-embodiment generalization, real-time inference, and long-horizon planning. It covers three model categories: VLA, world models, and reward models.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Humanoid Foundation Model Benchmark is an independent evaluation and comparison system for AI foundation models in humanoid robotics. Initiated by the humanoid.guide team based in STAVANGER, Norway, its core objective is to provide the first standardized, horizontal comparison tool for the rapidly evolving "robot brain" market.

The benchmark evaluates 40 foundation models from institutions including NVIDIA, Google DeepMind, Tesla, Physical Intelligence, AgiBot, Skild AI, Figure AI, Meta AI, Sanctuary AI, Agility Robotics, 1X Technologies, Unitree, and BeingBeyond. These models are divided into three categories: Vision-Language-Action models (VLA), World Models, and Reward Models.

Each model is independently scored across ten capability dimensions using a five-point scale. The platform offers an interactive filtering interface, allowing users to filter models by type, open-weight status, humanoid-specific or omni-body application, and on-device inference support. Each model's profile page includes specifications, test observations, and capability breakdowns.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

As the humanoid robotics industry accelerates toward commercial deployment, "which AI model should drive a robot" has become one of the most critical yet opaque decisions in the sector. Investors allocating capital need to determine which teams have truly solved dexterous manipulation rather than merely locomotion; manufacturers selecting software stacks need to compare the merits of VLA approaches versus world model approaches; researchers tracking progress need a structured comparative framework. Before this benchmark, these stakeholders lacked a unified resource to examine the entire model landscape in a structured, comparable manner.

Humanoid.guide founder Christian Rokseth highlighted this core contradiction in the launch statement: "The humanoid robotics industry is producing exceptional foundation models at an accelerating pace, but there has been no standardized, independent way to compare them." This statement precisely captures the benchmark's historical positioning—it fills not a technological gap, but an evaluation and trust gap. What it truly changes is not model training methods, but the information infrastructure for industry decision-making.

From a timeline perspective, 2023–2026 marked a boom period for funding and R&D in humanoid robot foundation models, with a surge in model count but a lack of comparability. The benchmark's launch signals the industry's transition from "self-proclamation" to "third-party independent verification," similar to the role played by nuScenes in autonomous driving or MMLU in large language models.

Mechanism Breakdown

① Logic Behind Evaluation Dimension Selection: From "Can Walk" to "Can Work"

The benchmark's ten capability dimensions are not arbitrarily listed but correspond to the capability stack required for humanoid robots in real-world deployment:

Level Dimension What It Assesses
Locomotion Foundation Locomotion Stable bipedal walking and running
Whole-body Coordination Whole-body control Coordinated waist, leg, and arm movements
Manipulation Capability Bimanual coordination, Dexterous manipulation Two-hand cooperation and fine finger movements
Environmental Interaction Navigation, Sim-to-real transfer Spatial understanding and virtual-to-real transfer
Cognitive Capability Reasoning, Long-horizon planning High-level decision-making and task decomposition
Engineering Constraints Real-time inference, Cross-embodiment generalization Deployment feasibility and versatility

These ten dimensions cover the complete spectrum from low-level motion control to high-level cognitive planning, with each dimension directly corresponding to key performance indicators in real-world deployment.

② Scoring Mechanism: Five-Point Scale and Independent Evaluation

Each model is independently scored across ten dimensions using a five-point scale. Scoring is conducted independently by the benchmark team rather than relying on self-reported data from model developers. This "independent evaluation" design aims to eliminate bias from self-claims—in the absence of third-party verification, model developers tend to exaggerate their capabilities, while an independent benchmark ensures comparability through unified testing protocols and observation records.

③ Classification System: Differentiated Comparison Across Three Model Types

The benchmark categorizes models into VLA, world models, and reward models. This classification reflects current technical route divergences in humanoid robot AI:

  • VLA Models: Directly map visual and language inputs to action outputs, representing the current mainstream approach. Examples include NVIDIA GR00T N1 and Physical Intelligence's π₀.
  • World Models: Predictive models that learn environment dynamics for planning and simulation. Examples include NVIDIA Cosmos and 1X World Model.
  • Reward Models: Provide reward signals for reinforcement learning. An example is IRIS.

The three model types address problems at different levels, and the benchmark avoids "apples vs. oranges" confusion through categorized comparison.

Compared to academic benchmarks (such as ManiSkill and LIBERO), the Humanoid Foundation Model Benchmark differs in that it focuses on foundation models rather than specific policies, covers ten dimensions rather than a single task family, and is operated by an independent third party rather than an academic team. Its positioning is closer to an "industry rating agency" than an "academic competition."

Who Uses It · Application Cases

① Investors: Capital Allocation Decisions

During the 2023–2026 humanoid robot funding boom, investors faced a core question: which teams have truly solved technical challenges, and which are merely hyping concepts? The benchmark's ten-dimension scoring allows investors to distinguish teams that excel at "locomotion" from those that excel at "dexterous manipulation," enabling more precise capital allocation decisions.

② Manufacturers: Software Stack Selection

OEMs selecting AI software stacks need to compare how different VLA models perform on their own hardware. The benchmark's cross-embodiment generalization dimension directly addresses this need—a model that performs consistently across multiple robots holds greater deployment value than one optimized only for a single platform.

③ Model Developers: Self-Positioning and Improvement

Model developers can use the benchmark to understand their model's relative position across ten dimensions and identify capability gaps. For example, a VLA model scoring high on locomotion and navigation but low on dexterous manipulation would prompt its developers to invest targeted resources in data collection and training.

④ Researchers: Progress Tracking

Academic researchers view the benchmark as a "thermometer" for industry progress, quantifying the pace of advancement by tracking changes in the highest scores across each dimension.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Subjectivity of Evaluation Standards

The five-point scoring relies on the evaluation team's subjective judgment, lacking a unified automated testing protocol. Different evaluators may interpret "dexterous manipulation" differently, raising concerns about scoring standard consistency. The benchmark page mentions "test observations" but does not publicly disclose specific test tasks and scoring rubrics.

② Timeliness of Model Versions

Foundation models iterate extremely rapidly. The 40 models evaluated at the benchmark's launch on April 14, 2026, may be superseded by new versions within months. The benchmark's update frequency is not specified in public materials, posing a risk of "evaluation lagging behind development."

③ Coverage Boundaries

The benchmark covers 40 models, but the humanoid foundation model field extends far beyond this number. Some undisclosed or unsubmitted models (such as certain closed-source commercial models) may not be included, resulting in incomplete comparisons.

④ Inability to Evaluate Hardware-Coupled Performance

The benchmark evaluates AI models themselves, not the complete "model + robot" system. A model performing excellently in simulation may underperform on specific hardware due to physical factors such as actuator latency and friction. The benchmark's "sim-to-real transfer" dimension partially addresses this issue but cannot fully replace real-world deployment testing.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A high-scoring model is the best model" — Incorrect. The benchmark's ten dimensions are equally weighted, but different deployment scenarios require different dimensional weights. A model deployed in warehouse settings may prioritize locomotion and navigation over dexterous manipulation. A high-scoring model is not necessarily the best fit for a specific scenario.

  2. "Independent evaluation equals objective truth" — Not entirely. Independent evaluation eliminates bias from self-claims, but the evaluation itself still relies on human judgment and test design. The benchmark's scores represent "engineering judgment" rather than "physical measurement," carrying inherent subjectivity.

  3. "VLA models are always superior to world models" — Incorrect. The three model types solve different problems: VLA models directly output actions, world models predict environment dynamics, and reward models provide learning signals. In complex tasks requiring long-horizon planning, world models may hold advantages over VLA models. The benchmark's classification design specifically aims to avoid this "cross-category comparison" pitfall.

  4. "The benchmark covers all important models" — Inaccurate. The benchmark covers 40 models, but the field contains far more. Unsubmitted or undisclosed models may not be included, so the comparison results cannot be viewed as a complete market picture.
