Overview

Power Distribution and DC-DC is a critical process node in the electronic, electrical, and energy systems (P13) of humanoid robots. It is responsible for efficiently and safely distributing electrical energy from batteries or external power sources to motor buses, logic power, and various functional modules. What it truly changes is not merely "connecting wires properly," but rather achieving a verifiable engineering balance among electrical isolation, fail-safe operation, and energy efficiency for the robot's power and computing systems. This process uses bus voltage selection, DC-DC modules, filtering, OR-ing, and redundancy as core methods, with power distribution diagrams, DC-DC component selection, and efficiency/thermal testing as completion criteria.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition of Power Distribution and DC-DC

Power Distribution and DC-DC is the energy transmission and conversion stage within the overall electrical architecture of a humanoid robot. The core problem it solves is: how to convert a single energy input (e.g., a battery pack) into multiple voltage levels and power supply rails with varying power requirements, while ensuring system safety during faults.

In humanoid robots, the Power Distribution Network (PDN) typically includes two main buses: the motor bus (high voltage, e.g., 48V or higher, directly driving joint motors) and the logic power bus (low voltage, e.g., 12V/5V/3.3V, powering computing platforms, sensors, and communication interfaces). DC-DC converters handle voltage conversion and isolation between the two buses and among various sub-supply rails.

Inputs to this process include: power budgets defined by the upstream system architecture, peak motor power requirements, computing platform voltage requirements, and safety standard requirements. Outputs include: the Power Distribution Diagram, DC-DC component selection report, and efficiency/thermal test report. These outputs serve as direct inputs for downstream PCB design, wiring harness design, overall system thermal design, and functional safety certification.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the early stages of humanoid robot development, power system design was often simplified to "pick a power supply, connect a few wires," leading to three categories of systemic pain points:

First, motor bus contamination of logic power. Joint motors generate massive current spikes and voltage dips during startup, emergency stops, and stall conditions. If logic power shares a common rail with the motor bus, computing platforms may reset or crash due to voltage transients—which, in walking control, means falling. Isolation is the only reliable solution.

Second, lack of fault propagation boundaries. A single short circuit or overcurrent event, without fuse/current-limiting protection, can propagate through the power network and destroy an entire control board. The Power Distribution and DC-DC process uses OR-ing (diode or ideal diode paralleling) and redundancy design to confine faults to the smallest possible area.

Third, disconnect between efficiency and thermal design. DC-DC conversion efficiency directly determines overall system heat dissipation. If components are poorly selected, every few percentage points of efficiency loss create localized hot spots within the robot's compact torso, affecting electronic component lifespan and structural integrity.

In WBS V3, this process is historically positioned as: a connecting node within P13 electronic, electrical, and energy systems—receiving power budget and safety partitioning requirements from the system architecture upstream, and providing clear electrical interfaces and protection strategies for PCB design, wiring harness design, and system integration downstream.

Principle Breakdown

① Bus Voltage Selection: Trade-off Between Efficiency and Safety

The higher the motor bus voltage, the lower the current for the same power, and the lower the losses in wiring and connectors (P_loss = I²R). However, higher voltage also raises requirements for insulation, creepage distances, and safety standards. In engineering practice, the choice is typically made based on motor peak power and driver voltage ratings, with typical values ranging from 24V to 96V (specific model parameters need to be confirmed). Logic power follows the standard voltage rails of computing platforms and sensors, obtained by stepping down from the bus via DC-DC converters.

② DC-DC Conversion: Isolated vs. Non-Isolated

Non-isolated types (Buck/Boost) are used for voltage conversion within a bus, offering high efficiency and small size; isolated types (Flyback/Push-Pull) are used for electrical isolation between the motor bus and logic power, breaking ground loops and noise propagation paths. The isolation voltage rating of isolated DC-DC converters must meet safety standards (e.g., relevant requirements of IEC 62368-1; specific values need to be confirmed).

③ Filtering and EMI Suppression

Motor drivers are strong sources of electromagnetic interference (switching frequencies typically above 20kHz; specific frequencies need to be confirmed). Common-mode/differential-mode filters are required at power inputs, and decoupling capacitors at outputs, to suppress conducted and radiated emissions, ensuring signal integrity for computing platforms and communication buses.

④ OR-ing and Redundancy

OR-ing circuits (diode or ideal diode) are used for automatic switching and reverse-current prevention when multiple power supplies are paralleled. Redundancy design (e.g., dual batteries, dual DC-DC modules) ensures that critical functions remain operational even with a single point of failure—for humanoid robots, this means the fall-protection circuit remains powered even after power loss to the drive system.

⑤ Safe Power-Down During Faults

The power distribution network must implement staged power-down logic: during an emergency stop, first cut the motor bus (power), retain logic power (safety control), and finally cut logic power as needed. This sequence is implemented jointly by hardware circuits and software control and is key evidence for functional safety certification.

Horizontal Comparison

In humanoid robot power architectures, two mainstream design approaches exist:

Centralized Power Architecture: All DC-DC conversion is concentrated on a single power board, outputting all voltage rails uniformly. Advantages: simple management, low cost, centralized EMI filtering; disadvantages: large fault impact area, long wiring, significant voltage drops.

Distributed Power Architecture: Small DC-DC converters are placed locally near each functional module (e.g., head, torso, legs), with a unified bus voltage transmitted and stepped down at the endpoints. Advantages: small voltage drops, good fault isolation, high modularity; disadvantages: higher cost, dispersed EMI filtering, complex management.

Humanoid robots typically adopt a hybrid architecture: centralized for the motor bus (high power, high voltage) and distributed for logic power (local step-down, low noise). This choice is directly related to the safety partitioning design in the P13 phase—the distributed placement of logic power ensures each computing node receives clean power, while faults affect only local areas.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Practitioners of the Power Distribution and DC-DC process include:

  • System Electrical Engineers: Responsible for drawing the power distribution diagram, DC-DC component selection, and protection strategy design.
  • PCB Design Engineers: Complete layout and routing of power boards and functional boards based on the power distribution diagram.
  • Functional Safety Engineers: Review power-down logic, redundancy design, and fault propagation boundaries.
  • Thermal Design Engineers: Validate overall system thermal models based on efficiency/thermal test data.

Typical application scenario: In the torso of a bipedal humanoid robot, the battery pack output passes through a main fuse into the power distribution board. One path directly drives the joint motor drivers for the legs and arms (motor bus); another path steps down via an isolated DC-DC converter to 12V logic power, then further steps down via non-isolated Buck converters to 5V/3.3V for the computing platform and sensors. During an emergency stop, safety relays first cut the motor bus, while logic power remains energized to complete safe posture control.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundaries of the Power Distribution and DC-DC process are:

  • Does not cover motor driver internal design: Drivers belong to other P13 sub-tasks (e.g., motor drive and power stage); this process only handles supply rail distribution and conversion.
  • Does not cover battery cell design: Battery selection and BMS are upstream or parallel tasks; this process only defines battery interfaces and protection requirements.
  • Does not cover overall system thermal design: This process outputs efficiency/thermal test data, but the overall heat dissipation solution is the responsibility of the thermal design team.
  • Does not cover functional safety certification: This process provides the implementation of power-down logic and redundancy design, but certification is completed by a dedicated safety team.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Higher DC-DC efficiency is always better"—Higher efficiency typically means higher cost and larger size. In engineering, the focus should be on the efficiency curve across the full load range, not just full-load efficiency. Humanoid robot joint motors often operate under light load or standby conditions, where light-load efficiency is often more critical (engineering judgment).

  2. "Isolation just means adding a transformer"—The parasitic capacitance of isolated DC-DC converters creates high-frequency noise paths; isolation effectiveness depends on layout and shielding. Isolation does not equal interference immunity; filtering and grounding design are equally important.

  3. "Redundancy just means paralleling two modules"—Simple paralleling can lead to one module overloading due to uneven current sharing. OR-ing and current-sharing control are key to redundancy design; otherwise, redundancy can actually reduce reliability.

  4. "Voltage drop can be calculated later"—Wiring voltage drop is proportional to the square of current (P_loss = I²R). Under peak motor current, voltage drop can reach several volts, directly affecting motor output torque. Voltage drop budgeting must be completed at the power distribution diagram stage.
