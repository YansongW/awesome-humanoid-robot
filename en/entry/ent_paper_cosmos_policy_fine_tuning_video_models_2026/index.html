Summary

Recent video generation models demonstrate remarkable ability to capture complex physical interactions and scene evolution over time. To leverage their spatiotemporal priors, robotics works have adapted video models for policy learning but introduce complexity by requiring multiple stages of post-training and new architectural components for action generation.

Overview

The NVIDIA team introduces Cosmos Policy, which fine-tunes the pretrained video generation model Cosmos-Predict2-2B directly into a unified robot policy via a "latent frame injection" mechanism, enabling simultaneous output of actions, future states, and value functions without modifying the architecture. The method achieves an average success rate of 98.5% on LIBERO, 67.1% on RoboCasa, and 93.6% on real ALOHA, and supports model-based best-of-N sampling planning, providing an additional 12.5 percentage point improvement on challenging tasks.

What It Changes

Robot policy learning has long faced an architectural gap between "video priors" and "action output." Previous mainstream approaches either trained action diffusion models from scratch, like Diffusion Policy, wasting the physical and spatiotemporal priors already learned by video generation models, or designed custom unified architectures like UVA/UWM, which could not directly load pretrained weights. More troublesome, leveraging world models for planning typically required additional training of inverse dynamics models or separate value networks, forming complex multi-stage, multi-component pipelines.

What Cosmos Policy truly changes is compressing the two modules that previously needed to be stitched together—"video generation" and "policy learning"—into the same denoising process of the same diffusion transformer. It demonstrates that the latent space of a pretrained video model can naturally accommodate non-image modalities such as actions, proprioception, and value functions, and that modality injection can be achieved through simple latent frame replacement without any new architectural components. This makes possible a new paradigm of "video foundation models as policies"—downstream robotics teams no longer need to design dedicated action heads or value heads, but can simply reuse the generative capabilities of video models.

Method Breakdown

Core Mechanism: Latent Frame Injection

  • Non-image modalities such as robot proprioception, action chunks, and state values are encoded as new latent frames and directly inserted into the video model's latent diffusion sequence without modifying the network architecture.
  • Each modality is normalized to [−1, +1] and replicated to fill a volume identical to a single latent frame's H′×W′×C′.
  • Multiple camera views are inserted directly at the image sequence level as additional camera images.

Sequence Design and Conditioning Scheme

  • The latent sequence order is (s, a, s′, V(s′)), supporting left-to-right autoregressive decoding.
  • With two static third-person cameras plus one wrist camera as an example, the sequence contains 11 latent frames: blank placeholder, proprioception, wrist image, two third-person images, action chunk, future proprioception, future wrist image, future two third-person images, future state value.
  • During training, a conditioning mask determines which frames serve as conditions (clean) and which as generation targets (noisy), enabling switching among policy/world model/value function training modes within the same architecture.

Joint Training Objective

  • Training data is 50% from demonstrations (training policy p(a, s′, V(s′)|s)) and 50% from rollback data (half training the world model p(s′, V(s′)|s, a), half training the value function p(V(s′)|s, a, s′)).
  • Both the policy and world model include auxiliary objectives: the policy jointly predicts future states and values rather than only actions; the world model jointly predicts future states and values rather than only future states.

Inference and Planning

  • Direct policy evaluation: parallel decoding, extracting only actions.
  • Model-based planning: best-of-N sampling—the policy generates N action proposals, the world model predicts future states for each proposal, the value function evaluates them, and the action with the highest predicted value is executed.
  • Ensemble prediction: each action queries the world model 3 times and the value function 5 times, yielding 15 value predictions aggregated via "majority mean."

Noise Distribution Adjustment

  • The base model's σ sampling uses a log-normal distribution (P_mean=1.39, P_std=1.2), which underweights high-noise regions, causing poor denoising in early generation.
  • Changed to a mixed distribution: 0.7 probability from the original log-normal sampling, 0.3 probability from uniform sampling over [1.0, 85.0], extending the high-σ tail.
  • At test time, σ_min is raised from 0.002 to 4, empirically improving prediction accuracy for actions, future states, and values.

Key Innovations

  1. Latent frame injection replaces architectural modification: This is the most critical innovation. Previously, introducing non-image modalities into video models required modifying attention mechanisms or adding dedicated encoders. Cosmos Policy only needs to pad modality data into latent frames and overwrite placeholders, fully reusing the pretrained model's VAE and DiT. This greatly reduces the engineering complexity of adapting video foundation models into policies while preserving the spatiotemporal priors in pretrained weights.

  2. Single-stage joint training of policy, world model, and value function: Training objectives are switched via conditioning masks, with the same model learning all three capabilities simultaneously under a 50/25/25 batch ratio. Ablation experiments show that removing auxiliary objectives (policy predicting only actions) decreases average success rate by 1.5 percentage points; completely removing future state and value prediction drops performance from 62.5% to 44.4%, proving that joint future state prediction is the core mechanism for policy effectiveness, not a simple architectural benefit.

  3. Refining the world model with policy rollback data: The authors found that world models trained solely on demonstration data struggle to generalize out-of-distribution, so they deployed the policy to collect rollback data (648 episodes), using 90% of batches to refine the world model and value function. The dual-deployment strategy (original checkpoint as the policy model, refined checkpoint as the planning model) ensures the planning model trains on on-policy data, a design that yields an additional 12.5 percentage point improvement in model-based planning on challenging tasks.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Model-based planning inference latency is significant (approximately 5 seconds/action chunk with 8 H100 GPUs in parallel), limiting applicability to dynamic tasks and mobile robots.
  • Effective planning requires substantial rollback data (648 episodes); learning from fewer rollbacks remains an open problem.
  • No large-scale action-annotated robot data pretraining (unlike VLA models); no input history is used, predicting only observations at times t and t+K.
  • No additional demonstrations generated by MimicGen are used on RoboCasa (only 50 human demonstrations).
  • True states are not fully observable; the world model actually predicts future observations (proprioception and camera images), with the paper using "state" for notational simplicity.
  • The paper does not specify: exact training steps, learning rate, batch size (except Appendix A.2), GPU model and count (except planning parallelization), and specific inference frequency values (except ALOHA 25 Hz).

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, first verify the latent frame injection padding logic—each modality needs normalization to [−1, +1] and replication to fill the H′×W′×C′ volume; action chunk extraction requires averaging all copies before denormalization, and this step is the most error-prone and directly impacts action accuracy. Second, noise distribution adjustment is not optional: the default log-normal distribution underweights high-σ regions, causing poor early denoising and cascading errors; be sure to use the 0.7/0.3 mixed sampling and set σ_min to 4.

During training, pay attention to data batch ratios: 50% demonstrations + 50% rollback (with rollback split evenly between world model and value function); rollback data is critical for planning capability, as world models trained only on demonstrations clearly fail in out-of-distribution generalization. For deployment, if low latency is prioritized, 1 denoising step (0.16 seconds/action chunk) sacrifices only 0.5% success rate and is a more realistic choice for real robot control; if maximum success rate is desired, accept inference latencies of 0.61 seconds (simulation) or 0.95 seconds (ALOHA). Planning mode requires 8 GPUs in parallel with a total time of approximately 5 seconds, suitable only for non-real-time tasks.
