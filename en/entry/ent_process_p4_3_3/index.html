Overview

Joint Module Integration and Bench Test is a third-level subtask in the joint module and drive system design phase (P4) of humanoid robot development. It is responsible for integrating subcomponents such as motors, reducers, encoders, and drivers into a complete joint module, and verifying through bench testing whether key indicators such as peak torque, continuous torque, stiffness, efficiency, and temperature rise meet the design requirements. What it truly changes is not the qualitative judgment of "whether it can rotate," but the quantitative evidence chain of "whether design specifications are actually fulfilled"—before full-machine assembly, it uses a controlled bench environment to measure the physical limits and performance boundaries of each joint, providing credible inputs for downstream body design, control algorithms, and safety strategies.

Content

What It Is: A Verification Closed Loop from Components to System

Joint module integration and bench testing is essentially an engineering process that integrates upstream selected and designed subcomponents—motors, reducers, encoders, drivers, and structural parts—into an independently operable joint unit according to interface specifications, and quantitatively verifies its mechanical, thermal, electrical, and control performance on a dedicated test bench. It sits in the mid-to-late stage of the P4 phase, building on the input review, interface design, and prototype fabrication from P4.3.3.1 to P4.3.3.3, and provides data support for the documentation deliverables of P4.3.3.5.

The core output of this process is the Joint Test Report, containing key data such as torque-current curves, efficiency maps, temperature rise curves, and bandwidth. These data serve not only as acceptance criteria but also as foundational inputs for downstream full-machine integration, control parameter tuning, and safety protection threshold setting. What it truly changes is not the superficial confirmation of "whether it can move," but the deep verification of "whether each joint's physical characteristics match the design model"—every data point measured on the bench answers the question: "Do the parameters in simulation still hold on real hardware?"

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robot joint design has long faced a fundamental contradiction: no matter how accurate the simulation model is during the design phase, it cannot fully predict the combined effects of assembly tolerances, material tolerances, thermal effects, and friction nonlinearities in real hardware. If bench testing is skipped and the system goes directly to full-machine integration, troubleshooting becomes extremely difficult once issues such as insufficient torque, excessive temperature rise, or stiffness deviation arise—was the motor selection wrong, the reducer assembly misaligned, or the control parameters poorly tuned? The value of bench testing lies precisely in isolating variables one by one in a controlled environment, allowing each problem to surface at its source.

From a historical perspective, bench testing is the mandatory gateway connecting "design intent" with "physical performance." The industrial robot field has long established mature bench testing standards, but humanoid robot joints—due to their special requirements for high dynamic response, lightweight design, and high transparency—cannot simply copy those standards. What it truly changes is not the choice of "whether to test," but the engineering trade-off of "testing depth and coverage"—it must cover key indicators such as peak torque, continuous torque, stiffness, efficiency, and temperature rise, while finding a reasonable testing granularity under project schedule and cost constraints.

Principle Breakdown

① Torque-Current Characteristics: Bench Verification of the Motor Model

The core output of a joint module is torque, and torque has a deterministic relationship with motor current. The primary task of bench testing is to measure output torque at different currents using a torque sensor, plot the torque-current curve, and verify whether its linearity and measured torque constant match the design values. This curve is the basis for torque estimation in subsequent control algorithms; excessive deviation directly compromises force control accuracy.

② Efficiency and Temperature Rise: Thermal Characterization of Energy Loss

During joint module operation, motor copper losses, iron losses, and reducer friction losses convert into heat, causing temperature rise. Bench testing uses a power analyzer to measure input electrical power and output mechanical power to calculate efficiency; simultaneously, temperature rise tests record the temperature variation curves of the joint under different load conditions. Temperature rise data directly determines the joint's continuous torque capability—continuous torque is essentially "the torque that can be sustained within the thermal equilibrium limit," not the motor's peak capability.

③ Stiffness and Bandwidth: Frequency-Domain Characterization of Dynamic Performance

Stiffness reflects the joint's ability to resist deformation under external forces, while bandwidth reflects the upper frequency limit at which the joint can track command signals. Bench testing calculates stiffness by applying a known torque and measuring angular displacement, and determines bandwidth through swept-frequency excitation. These two indicators jointly determine the joint's dynamic response quality and are key inputs for control parameter tuning and full-machine dynamic performance prediction.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Parameter Test Tool Acceptance Focus
Peak torque Torque sensor Whether design peak is achieved, overload margin
Continuous torque Torque sensor + temperature rise monitoring Sustainable output capability under thermal equilibrium
Stiffness Torque sensor + angle encoder Whether deformation under load is within allowable range
Efficiency Power analyzer Energy conversion efficiency under rated conditions
Temperature rise Temperature sensor Whether steady-state temperature after sustained operation exceeds limits
Bandwidth Signal generator + response acquisition Whether amplitude and phase characteristics meet control requirements

All the above parameters must be presented as curves or data tables in the test report, serving as the basis for acceptance and delivery.

Horizontal Comparison

In the humanoid robot development process, bench testing, full-machine testing, and simulation verification form a three-layer complementary relationship:

Verification Layer Verification Object Advantages Limitations
Simulation verification Design model Low cost, fast iteration, can cover extreme conditions Model accuracy depends on assumptions, cannot fully reflect physical hardware
Bench testing Joint module Controlled environment, isolatable variables, high data credibility Cannot cover full-machine coupling effects and real loads
Full-machine testing Complete robot Closest to real operating conditions, verifies system-level performance Difficult problem localization, high cost, long cycle

The unique value of bench testing lies in the fact that it is closer to reality than simulation, yet more controllable than full-machine testing. What it truly changes is not "the choice of verification methods," but "the timing of problem exposure"—resolving joint-level issues before full-machine assembly is far more time- and cost-efficient than troubleshooting on the complete robot.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The direct executors of this process are joint module design engineers and test engineers, and its outputs serve multiple downstream roles:

  • Control algorithm engineers: Use torque-current curves and bandwidth data to tune torque control parameters, design feedforward compensation, and filtering strategies;
  • Structural design engineers: Use stiffness data to verify structural design margins and confirm whether the connection stiffness between joints and links meets full-machine dynamic requirements;
  • System integration engineers: Use temperature rise data to design thermal management solutions and efficiency data to evaluate full-machine power budgets;
  • Safety and reliability engineers: Use peak torque and continuous torque data to set overload protection thresholds and develop derating strategies.

In a typical humanoid robot development project, bench testing starts immediately after joint module prototypes are completed, running in parallel with structural part machining and control algorithm development. Its output is a prerequisite for initiating full-machine integration.

Limitations and Boundaries

Although bench testing provides high-credibility joint-level data, it has clear boundaries:

  • Cannot cover full-machine coupling effects: Bench testing is single-joint independent verification and cannot reflect dynamic coupling, structural resonance, or load interactions during multi-joint coordinated motion;
  • Simplified load conditions: Bench loading methods differ from the complex dynamic loads encountered in actual walking and manipulation, so test results cannot be directly equated with full-machine performance;
  • Test fixture rigidity effects: Insufficient rigidity in the test fixture introduces additional compliance, causing stiffness and bandwidth test results to deviate from true values (engineering judgment);
  • Measurement accuracy limitations: The precision of torque sensors, encoders, and other measurement equipment directly affects data credibility, requiring calibration and error analysis before testing.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Passing bench testing means the full machine will be fine"—Wrong. Bench testing only verifies joint-level performance; after full-machine integration, new issues such as cable routing, thermal conditions, and structural resonance will emerge and must be further validated through full-machine testing.
  2. "Meeting peak torque is sufficient"—Wrong. Humanoid robots in scenarios such as walking and jumping require sustained, controllable torque output; continuous torque and temperature rise characteristics are often more critical than peak torque.
  3. "The more test data, the better"—Wrong. Too many test items lengthen the schedule and increase costs; test items and depth should be reasonably selected based on the joint's key failure modes and downstream requirements (engineering judgment).
  4. "The test report is just a formality"—Wrong. The Joint Test Report is the basis for downstream control parameter tuning, safety protection settings, design iteration, and supply chain acceptance; its data quality directly affects full-machine development efficiency.
