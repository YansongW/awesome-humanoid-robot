Summary

Safety-critical control is essential for humanoid robots operating in complex human-centered environments, where physical safety constraints such as joint limits, self-collision avoidance, obstacle avoidance, and workspace boundaries must be satisfied during real-robot operation. Overview

This paper proposes a hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework for humanoid robots, embedding Input-to-State Safety Control Barrier Functions (ISSf-CBF) between kinematic-level and dynamic-level whole-body controllers. It simultaneously guarantees kinematic safety constraints, contact stability, and task performance under unknown model perturbations. The authors validate the method's robustness advantages over standard CBF and eCBF baselines in both simulation (MuJoCo) and on a real TOCABI robot (33 degrees of freedom).

What It Changes

Existing safety control methods for humanoid robots provide safety guarantees under ideal model assumptions, but when faced with inherent model mismatches in real hardware (such as link mass errors, tracking errors, and external disturbances), kinematic-level safety barriers (e.g., CBF) fail due to inaccurate low-level dynamic tracking—this is the core pain point the authors aim to address. Previous eCBF methods elevate safety constraints to the acceleration level, but their effectiveness heavily depends on high-fidelity dynamic models and precise tracking by low-level controllers, and safety guarantees degrade under model uncertainty. What this paper truly changes is the "level of responsibility" for safety guarantees: instead of letting the dynamic layer bear the full burden of safe execution, it uses ISSf-CBF at the kinematic layer to generate safety velocity references robust to perturbations, which are then passed to the dynamic layer for tracking. This shifts safety guarantees from "depending on model accuracy" to "depending on bounded perturbations." This transition makes safety control feasible in real hardware rather than only in idealized simulation environments.

Method Breakdown

Hierarchical Architecture

The framework consists of three layers, with signals flowing top-down: 1. KinWBC (Kinematic-level Whole-Body Control): Iteratively solves joint velocity commands via prioritized pseudo-inverse in the projected null space: ˙qdes_i = ˙qdes_{i−1} + J†{i|pre}(xdes_i − x_i − J_i ˙qdes) where J_{i|pre} = J_i N_{i−1}, with small singular values zeroed to avoid singularities. 2. ISSf-CBF Safety Filter: Solves a QP on the whole-body velocity kinematic model (y = q, v = ˙q_safe, ˙y = v + d): ˙q_safe = argmin ‖˙q − ˙q_des‖² Constraints include four categories: joint limits, self-collision avoidance, object collision avoidance, and workspace boundaries. Each constraint adopts the ISSf-CBF form: L_f h(y) + L_g h(y)v ≥ −α(h(y)) + (1/ε)‖∇h(y)‖² where ε > 0 controls the size of the disturbance tolerance region, and α is a linear class-K function (α(h) = αh). 3. DynWBC (Dynamic-level Whole-Body Control): Converts the safety-filtered kinematic reference into desired acceleration via PD feedback: ¨qsafe = Kdyn_p(qsafe − q) + Kdyn_d(˙qsafe − ˙q) Then solves a QP with an objective function containing four weighted terms (acceleration tracking error w_¨q, contact force deviation w_c, torque smoothing w_τ, acceleration energy w_M), with constraints including the linearized 6D contact force cone (UF_c ≤ 0) and the whole-body equation of motion (M(q)¨q + h(q, ˙q) = S^T τ + J_c(q)^T F_c). The final torque command: τ_cmd = τ_opt + K_p(q_safe − q) + K_d(˙q_safe − ˙q)

Key Design Decisions

  • Disturbance Modeling: The full-order FOM dynamics are projected onto a ROM (here, the whole-body velocity kinematic model), and the disturbance d is defined as the difference between the projected closed-loop FOM and the ideal ROM. The (1/ε)‖∇h‖² term in the ISSf-CBF condition explicitly compensates for this bounded disturbance.
  • Parameter Tuning: Following practical guidelines, α and ε are initialized from small values and gradually increased until performance is sufficient, avoiding excessive values that cause jitter in safety velocity commands.
  • Parallel Computation: The ISSf-CBF QP is solved on the main thread while the DynWBC QP runs on a parallel thread, satisfying the 2 kHz control cycle.

Key Innovations

  1. First extension of ISSf-CBF to full-order humanoid robots: Previously, ISSf-CBF was only combined with reduced-order models. This paper embeds it between kinematic-level and dynamic-level whole-body control, achieving consistent transfer from kinematic safety references to full-order dynamics, filling the gap in this theory for legged robots.
  2. Decoupling safety guarantees from model fidelity: By explicitly compensating for ROM-FOM disturbances via ISSf-CBF at the kinematic layer, safety guarantees no longer depend on high-fidelity dynamic models or precise tracking by low-level controllers, but rather on the bounded-disturbance assumption—a direct response to the fundamental flaw of eCBF-type methods.
  3. Hierarchical safety responsibility allocation: Instead of placing all safety execution burden on the dynamic layer, the kinematic layer generates robust safety references while the dynamic layer focuses on contact force tracking and balance. This division allows contact stability (via contact force cone constraints) and kinematic safety (via ISSf-CBF) to be tuned independently, making engineering implementation more practical.

Experiments and Results

Simulation Comparison (MuJoCo, link mass +20% mismatch)

Baseline Collision rate/penetration depth Remarks
w/o-CBF Left hand penetrates torso >5 cm No safety filter
CBF (standard) Collision rate ~50% (full α range) Severe joint velocity jitter at α≥30
eCBF Average collision rate ~50% α≤20 excluded (excessive motion deviation)
ISSf-CBF (ε=10) No collisions for α≤30 Collision rate increases at α≥35 due to jitter
ISSf-CBF (ε increased) Collision rate increases with ε Parameters insufficient to compensate ROM-FOM disturbances

Walking scenario: w/o-CBF has both feet penetrating each other >3 cm, while ISSf-CBF maintains the signed distance barrier value strictly positive.

Real Robot (TOCABI, 33 DOF, ~6% mass mismatch)

  • Single-leg balance TAICHI: CoM shifts toward the left foot center, hands move 30 cm and 60 cm respectively, with measured ZMP remaining within the support polygon.
  • Obstacle avoidance teleoperation (ε_OC_AO=30.0): No collisions across three repeated motions, with barrier value h_AO approaching zero but remaining non-negative.
  • Obstacle avoidance teleoperation (ε_OC_AO=50.0): Barrier values fall below zero in all three trials, with maximum penetration depth of 8 cm.
  • Ablation validation: The robot falls when using only PD feedback (without τ_opt), demonstrating the critical role of contact force tracking and force cone constraints in DynWBC for balance.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • When multiple CBF constraints conflict causing QP infeasibility, this was not observed in this paper (due to sufficient redundancy in the upper limbs), but may fail on platforms with fewer DOF; the authors mention slack variables as a remedy, though this sacrifices safety guarantees (Remark 1).
  • Dynamic obstacle scenarios are not addressed (obstacle velocity in object collision is estimated via Kalman filtering, but no explicit dynamic obstacle experiments are reported).
  • Real experiments only include single-leg balance and teleoperation; no real walking experiments were conducted.
  • No quantitative comparison with other whole-body safety control methods (e.g., MPC-based approaches).
  • Contact stability relies on contact force cone constraints, which may still fail under uncertain terrain height or friction conditions.

Engineering Insights

For reproduction or engineering application, it is recommended to verify the following in order: 1. Prioritize parameter tuning: The initialization and step size of α and ε directly affect safety and task performance—start from small values and gradually increase. If safety velocity command jitter occurs (α too large) or collisions occur (ε too small), rebalance the two. 2. Validate disturbance modeling: The robustness of ISSf-CBF relies on the bounded-disturbance assumption for ROM-FOM. Before deployment, measure or estimate the maximum disturbance magnitude and select ε accordingly; excessively large ε causes safety barrier failure (e.g., 8 cm penetration at ε=50 in experiments). 3. QP solver configuration: Two QPs (ISSf-CBF and DynWBC) must be solved in parallel within the 2 kHz control cycle. It is recommended to use qpOASES and ensure timing synchronization between the main thread and parallel thread to avoid delays causing safety constraint violations. 4. Contact force cone constraints are indispensable: Relying solely on joint PD feedback (without τ_opt) leads to falls. The desired contact force tracking and force cone constraints in DynWBC are necessary conditions for balance stability and must not be removed for simplification. 5. Collision geometry modeling accuracy: The sphere/capsule approximation radii of obstacles and robot links directly affect barrier value computation. In experiments, the obstacle sphere radius is 11.5 cm and the hand is 6 cm; for actual deployment, calibration based on real geometric dimensions is required.
