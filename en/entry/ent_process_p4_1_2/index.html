Overview

Selection of actuator topology is a critical process node in the joint module and drive system design phase (P4). It is responsible for determining the optimal solution for each joint among four mainstream topologies: QDD, harmonic drive, planetary + frameless motor, and direct drive. What it truly changes is not the selection of a specific motor, but the physical ceiling of the entire robot's dynamic performance and energy efficiency—because once the topology is frozen, subsequent controller design, structural layout, and cost structure are all locked in.

Content

What It Is: The Decision Process from Joint Requirements to Topology Solutions

Actuator topology selection is essentially mapping the joint-level requirements (peak torque, speed, dynamic response bandwidth) output from upstream dynamics analysis into a specific actuator structural form. The question it answers is: Which "motor + reducer" combination should this joint use?

Each of the four candidate topologies has its own physical essence:

  • Direct Drive: The motor rotor directly drives the load, with no reducer. Torque density is low, but backdrivability transparency is the highest, making it suitable for low-load, high-dynamics scenarios.
  • QDD (Quasi-Direct Drive): The motor drives the load through a small reduction ratio (typically 3–10x). While retaining relatively high backdrivability transparency, it significantly improves torque density.
  • Harmonic Drive: Utilizes the elastic deformation of the flexspline to achieve a large reduction ratio (typically 50–160x). It is compact, lightweight, and offers high torque density, but backdrivability transparency is poor.
  • Planetary + Frameless Motor: A planetary reducer paired with a frameless motor (a motor body without housing or bearings), balancing torque density and structural integration at moderate reduction ratios.

In WBS V3, this process is located in the middle of the P4 phase, receiving joint requirement analysis from P4.1.1 and providing topology constraints for motor selection in P4.1.3 and reducer selection in P4.1.4.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robot joint design has long faced a fundamental contradiction: high dynamic response and high torque density cannot be achieved simultaneously. Traditional industrial robot joints use large-ratio harmonic reducers, offering high torque density, but the motor inertia is amplified by \(G^2\), making the joint "unaware" of external forces and unable to achieve compliant control. Pure direct-drive motors, on the other hand, offer excellent backdrivability transparency but insufficient torque density to drive high-torque joints such as the hip and knee of a humanoid robot.

This contradiction is amplified in bipedal walking scenarios: the hip joint needs to output high torque during the stance phase to maintain balance, yet requires extremely low impedance during the swing phase to respond quickly to disturbances. If the topology is chosen poorly, the joint is either "stiff," leading to unnatural gait, or "weak," failing to support body weight.

Actuator topology selection exists precisely as the decision node to resolve this contradiction. By establishing a quantitative evaluation matrix, it weighs five key constraint dimensions—backdrivability transparency, stiffness, backlash, efficiency, and noise—to find a topology solution for each joint that is "sufficient without waste."

Principle Breakdown

① Physical Relationship Between Reduction Ratio and Backdrivability Transparency

The core difference among actuator topologies lies in the choice of reduction ratio \(G\). The motor-side inertia \(J_m\) reflected to the load side becomes \(G^2 J_m\), so the larger the reduction ratio, the greater the equivalent inertia on the load side, and the worse the backdrivability transparency. Additionally, friction and backlash in the reducer itself add to the backdrive torque:

\[ \tau_{\text{backdrive}} = \tau_{\text{friction}} + \frac{\tau_{\text{load}}}{G} + G \, J_m \, \ddot{\theta}_m \]

Here, the first term is reducer friction, the second term is the backdrive component of the load torque reduced by the reduction ratio, and the third term is the inertial resistance from the amplified motor inertia. QDD can balance torque and transparency precisely because it selects a sufficiently small \(G\) (3–10x), keeping the second and third terms within a controllable range.

② Decision Logic for Topology Selection

According to the design reasoning in the corpus, topology selection follows two empirical rules:

  • High-dynamics joints (hip/ankle) favor QDD: The hip and ankle joints need to respond to external force disturbances at high frequency during walking. QDD's low inertia and high transparency allow it to quickly sense ground reaction forces and actively comply.
  • High-torque, compact joints (shoulder/wrist) favor harmonic drives: Shoulder and wrist joints require high torque output but have limited space. The harmonic reducer's high reduction ratio (50–160x) achieves large torque amplification in a compact volume.

③ Quantitative Method of the Evaluation Matrix

Once candidate solutions are established, a quantitative evaluation is performed using the "QDD, harmonic drive, planetary + frameless motor, direct drive comparison table." Evaluation dimensions include cost, performance, supply chain, and maintainability, each scored by weight, ultimately forming the "Actuator Topology Decision Matrix."

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key constraint dimensions of actuator topology selection and their engineering implications are as follows:

Constraint Dimension Physical Meaning Impact on Topology Selection
Backdrivability transparency Ease with which external forces drive the joint in reverse QDD and direct drive are best; harmonic is worst
Stiffness Joint's resistance to deformation Harmonic is best; direct drive is limited by motor stiffness itself
Backlash Empty travel during forward/reverse rotation of the reducer Harmonic has minimal backlash; planetary reducers have larger backlash
Efficiency Energy transfer efficiency from motor to load Direct drive is highest (no reduction losses); harmonic is lower
Noise Acoustic performance during operation Harmonic is noticeably noisy; direct drive and QDD are quieter

These dimensions are mutually constraining: for example, increasing the reduction ratio improves torque density and stiffness but sacrifices backdrivability transparency and efficiency. The essence of topology selection is finding the Pareto-optimal solution among these conflicting dimensions.

Horizontal Comparison

Qualitative comparison of the four topologies across key dimensions (based on the methods/tools described in the corpus):

Topology Type Reduction Ratio Range Backdrivability Transparency Torque Density Volume/Weight Typical Application Joints
Direct Drive 1:1 Extremely high Low Large Fingers, wrist
QDD 3–10:1 High Medium Medium Hip, ankle
Harmonic Drive 50–160:1 Low High Small Shoulder, wrist
Planetary + Frameless Motor 10–50:1 Medium Medium-high Medium Elbow, knee

Engineering judgment: QDD and harmonic drives are the two most mainstream topologies for current humanoid robots. Direct drive is used only for end-effector joints requiring extremely high transparency but very low loads, while planetary + frameless motors are commonly used for joints requiring moderate reduction ratios and cost sensitivity.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Actuator topology selection is the prerequisite decision for all joint designs in the P4 phase. Its output, the "Actuator Topology Decision Matrix," directly constrains subsequent motor selection, reducer selection, and structural design. Specific application scenarios include:

  • P4.1.2.1 Input Organization and Target Quantification: Organize joint requirements from upstream dynamics analysis, convert completion criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and clarify Owners and milestones.
  • P4.1.2.2 Candidate Solution Development and Evaluation: Form at least 2 candidate solutions, establish an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize reviews, and freeze the solution.
  • P4.1.2.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulations or prototype validation, and document anomalies and deviations.
  • P4.1.2.4 Validation and Issue Closure: Develop test/review plans and pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output issue lists and improvement measures.
  • P4.1.2.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Output final reports/drawings/specifications, update ICD/BOM/SOP/requirement traceability chains, and complete formal delivery to downstream processes.

Limitations and Boundaries

Actuator topology selection has the following inherent limitations:

  1. Topology decisions do not solve motor body selection: After the topology is determined, specific motor models, rated power, peak torque, and other parameters still need to be selected in P4.1.3.
  2. Weights in the evaluation matrix are subjective: The weight allocation across cost, performance, supply chain, and maintainability depends on team experience. Different product positioning (e.g., research platforms vs. consumer-grade) leads to different weights.
  3. Topology is difficult to revisit once frozen: Once the solution is frozen and enters the prototyping phase, changing the topology means redesigning structural components, controllers, and wiring harnesses, at extremely high cost.
  4. The corpus does not provide specific values: Parameters such as reduction ratio ranges and torque density need to be confirmed through simulation and testing during the implementation phase.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "QDD is direct drive"—Wrong. QDD has a 3–10x reducer; without it, the motor torque would be insufficient. The point is "low enough to just preserve transparency."
  2. "Harmonic reducers have zero backlash"—Not entirely true. Harmonic reducers have extremely small backlash (typically less than 1 arcmin), but the elastic deformation of the flexspline introduces torsional compliance, which still produces position errors under high loads.
  3. "Topology selection only needs to consider torque density"—Wrong. Backdrivability transparency, stiffness, efficiency, and noise are equally critical, especially for joints requiring force control, where transparency often matters more than torque density.
  4. "Direct drive is always the best"—Wrong. Although direct drive offers the highest transparency, its low torque density and large volume make it suitable only for end-effector joints with very low loads.
