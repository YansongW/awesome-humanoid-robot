Overview

The Thermal Network/CFD Model is the core methodology work package in the thermal management simulation and iteration phase (P9) of humanoid robot development, used for temperature rise simulation of enclosed cavities, joint interiors, and computing compartments. It converts key constraints such as convective boundaries, material thermal conductivity, and contact thermal resistance into quantifiable temperature rise predictions through lumped-parameter thermal networks or computational fluid dynamics, thereby locking in thermal boundary conditions at the early design stage. What it truly changes is not a single-point action like "adding a fan to the robot," but rather shifting thermal issues from "firefighting after the fact" to "design constraints upfront"—locking in thermal boundary conditions during the early concept phase of joints, torso, and computing compartments.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition of the Thermal Network/CFD Model

The Thermal Network/CFD Model comprises two complementary methods used for temperature rise prediction and heat dissipation validation in the thermal management simulation and iteration phase (P9). The Thermal Network model adopts a lumped-parameter approach, discretizing the continuous temperature field into a finite number of nodes, with nodes connected via thermal resistances and capacitances to form an equivalent circuit for solving steady-state and transient temperatures. The CFD model (Computational Fluid Dynamics) solves the Navier-Stokes equations and the energy equation to obtain spatial distribution details of the flow field and temperature field. In work package P9.1.2, both methods jointly serve the temperature rise simulation of enclosed cavities, joint interiors, and computing compartments, delivering thermal simulation models, boundary conditions, and mesh/node lists as completion criteria.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Thermal management in humanoid robots is more challenging than in industrial robots due to the superposition of three "high densities." High power density—joint motors experience both electrical current losses and frictional heat under continuous operating conditions; high integration density—multiple high-power circuit boards are packed into enclosed cavities within the computing compartment, leaving extremely limited space for natural convection; high mass constraints—the added weight of heat sinks, fans, and heat pipes directly penalizes walking energy consumption and dynamic performance. These three constraints make the traditional path of "build first, measure temperature later" extremely costly: a single thermal runaway event can destroy joint encoders or the battery management system, with rework costs far exceeding simulation investment.

The historical positioning of the Thermal Network/CFD Model lies precisely here—it serves as the bridge in phase P9 from "heat source power consumption statistics" to "heat dissipation solution design." Upstream inputs (heat source inventory, structural CAD models, material lists) are transformed into computable models here, while downstream outputs (temperature rise predictions, hotspot locations, heat dissipation requirements) directly constrain heat dissipation solution selection. Without this step, thermal design would rely solely on empirical estimation and prototype trial-and-error, unable to form a closed loop before design freeze.

Principle Breakdown

① Thermal Network: Circuit Analogy of Lumped Parameters

The core of the thermal network model is discretizing the continuous temperature field into nodes, where each node represents an isothermal region. Nodes are connected by thermal resistances \(R\), and each node connects to ground via thermal capacitance \(C\). In steady state, node temperatures are determined by the heat flow balance equation:

\[ \sum_j \frac{T_i - T_j}{R_{ij}} + Q_i = 0 \]

where \(T_i\) is the temperature of node \(i\), \(R_{ij}\) is the thermal resistance between nodes \(i\) and \(j\), and \(Q_i\) is the injected heat flow at node \(i\) (from motor copper losses, iron losses, electronic component power dissipation, etc.). Transient analysis requires introducing the thermal capacitance term \(C_i \frac{dT_i}{dt}\) to evaluate temperature rise rates and thermal time constants. The advantage of the thermal network lies in its low computational cost and clear physical meaning of parameters, making it suitable for rapid system-level iteration.

② CFD: Numerical Solution of Continuous Fields

The CFD model solves mass conservation, momentum conservation, and energy conservation equations to perform spatially resolved numerical simulations of natural and forced convection within enclosed cavities. Its core value lies in capturing details that thermal networks cannot express—local vortices causing hotspots, flow distribution in narrow air ducts, and radiative heat transfer between stacked circuit boards. The cost is significantly increased mesh generation and solution time, and uncertainties in boundary conditions such as convective heat transfer coefficients and contact thermal resistance propagate directly into the results.

③ Synergy Strategy of the Two Methods

Engineering practice typically adopts a two-tier strategy of "thermal network coarse screening + CFD refined calculation": first, use the thermal network model to cover the entire system (all joints, cavities, boards) and identify risk areas where temperature limits may be exceeded; then, build CFD models for those risk areas to obtain precise temperature distributions and flow field details, guiding heat dissipation structure optimization. This synergy controls computational cost while ensuring accuracy in critical regions.

Horizontal Comparison

The thermal network and CFD models complement each other within work package P9.1.2, with their comparison as follows:

Dimension Lumped-Parameter Thermal Network CFD Model
Spatial resolution Node-level (discrete isothermal regions) Continuous field (mesh-level)
Computational cost Low, solved in seconds High, solved in hours
Applicable scenarios System-level rapid iteration, multi-condition sweeping Local hotspot refined calculation, flow field detail analysis
Boundary condition sensitivity Sensitive to thermal resistance assignment Sensitive to convection coefficients, mesh quality
Outputs Node temperatures, heat flow distribution Temperature field, flow field, pressure field

Engineering judgment: The thermal network is suitable for trend assessment and parameter sensitivity analysis during the concept phase, while CFD is suitable for quantitative verification during the detailed design phase. Only by combining both can the complete closed loop from input organization to result validation in P9.1.2 be covered.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Work package P9.1.2 is decomposed into five third-level subtasks in WBS V3, each corresponding to a clear application scenario:

  • P9.1.2.1 Input Organization and Target Quantification: Organize the upstream input list and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task owners, timelines, and risk registers. This step ensures thermal simulation has a solid basis, avoiding "garbage in, garbage out."
  • P9.1.2.2 Solution/Method Design: Develop no fewer than 2 candidate solutions (e.g., thermal network-first vs. CFD-first), establish an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize reviews, and freeze the solution. This step determines the technical route and resource investment for subsequent simulation.
  • P9.1.2.3 Modeling/Simulation and Prototype Implementation: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulations or prototype validation, and document anomalies and deviations. This step is the core execution phase of the work package.
  • P9.1.2.4 Simulation Result Validation and Optimization: Validate the consistency of simulation results against theoretical/experimental data, identify weak points, and iterate for optimization. This step ensures model credibility.
  • P9.1.2.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Write documentation per templates with references to raw data, complete internal reviews and version control, and publish with notification to downstream dependents. This step ensures thermal design constraints are effectively transmitted to heat dissipation solution design.

Limitations and Boundaries

The limitations of the Thermal Network/CFD Model are closely tied to boundary conditions. Convective boundary assignment relies heavily on empirical correlations or experimental calibration, and in enclosed cavities dominated by natural convection, errors can reach 20%–30% (engineering judgment); material thermal conductivity exhibits batch variability in scenarios involving composite materials and interface pads; contact thermal resistance is sensitive to assembly pressure and surface roughness, making it one of the largest sources of deviation between simulation and experiment. Additionally, CFD models demand high mesh quality, and insufficient boundary layer meshing can lead to underestimation of convective heat transfer coefficients. These uncertainties mean simulation results must be benchmarked against experimental data through the validation step in P9.1.2.4 before being used as design basis.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Thermal networks are low-precision, CFD is high-precision, so only use CFD"—Wrong. CFD accuracy depends on accurate boundary conditions and material parameters, which are often unknown at the early design stage; the computational efficiency of thermal networks makes them better suited for multi-condition sweeping and parameter sensitivity analysis. The two-tier synergy is the correct engineering approach.
  2. "Simulation results are the real temperatures"—Wrong. Simulation results are affected by multiple uncertainties including boundary conditions, material parameters, and mesh quality, and must be benchmarked against experimental data through the validation step in P9.1.2.4 to identify deviations and iterate.
  3. "Contact thermal resistance can be ignored"—Wrong. Contact thermal resistance is one of the key constraints explicitly listed in P9.1.2; between joint motors and reducer housings, and between computing compartment boards and heat sinks, contact thermal resistance can dominate overall temperature rise.
  4. "Thermal simulation is a one-time task"—Wrong. The five subtasks of P9.1.2 form a closed loop: input organization → solution design → modeling/simulation → result validation → documentation delivery, and validation results feed back into the modeling step for iterative optimization.
