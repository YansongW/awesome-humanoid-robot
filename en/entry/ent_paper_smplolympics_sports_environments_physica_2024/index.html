Summary

We present SMPLOlympics, a collection of physically simulated environments that allow humanoids to compete in a variety of Olympic sports. Sports simulation offers a rich and standardized testing ground for evaluating and improving the capabilities of learning algorithms due to the diversity and physically demanding nature of athletic activities. Institutions per source list: CMU + NVIDIA.

Overview

SMPLOlympics is a collection of Olympic sports environments for physically simulated humanoid robots, proposed by the research team. It aims to address the fragmentation of models, simulators, and algorithms in existing sports simulation research by unifying the SMPL/SMPL-X humanoid model and a standardized training pipeline. The core contribution lies in providing complete state-reward designs for 10 sports (including individual and competitive tasks), and systematically comparing the performance of PPO, AMP, PULSE, and their combinations in generating human-like behaviors, demonstrating the critical role of motion priors and curriculum learning in solving complex motor tasks.

What It Changes

The real change of this work is pushing "motor skill learning" from fragmented locomotion benchmarks toward structured, diverse sports competition scenarios. Previous benchmarks like dm_control or OpenAI Gym only covered basic movements such as walking and running, which could not induce behaviors requiring whole-body coordination and strategic decision-making, such as jumping, throwing, and hitting; meanwhile, specially crafted humanoid models struggled to obtain compatible motion data, leading to fragmented research efforts. SMPLOlympics, by unifying the SMPL ecosystem, directly opens the data pipeline of "internet videos → 3D pose estimation → physics simulation," allowing human demonstrations (MoCap or video) to be injected into training at low cost. This is a substantial breakthrough over existing benchmark paradigms—it enables "human-like behavior" to emerge from data rather than relying on hand-crafted rewards.

Method Breakdown

Unified Humanoid and State Space

  • Uses SMPL (24 joints, 23 actuated, action space ℝ⁶⁹) or SMPL-X (52 joints, 51 actuated, including fingers, action space ℝ¹⁵³) humanoids; all tasks share the same embodiment, avoiding cross-study model incompatibility issues.
  • Full-body pose 𝒒_t = (𝜽_t, 𝒑_t), with 𝜽_t ∈ ℝ^{J×6} (6 DoF rotation) and 𝒑_t ∈ ℝ^{J×3}; velocity 𝒒̇_t = (𝝎_t, 𝒗_t). State 𝒔_t = (𝒔^p_t, 𝒔^g_t), proprioception 𝒔^p_t = (𝒒_t, 𝒒̇_t), and goal state 𝒔^g_t customized per task; all quantities are normalized by yaw.

Task-Specific Reward Design

  • High Jump: Segmented rewards (position + height), horizontal bar height curriculum 0.5/1/1.5/2m, target point located 2m behind the bar.
  • Long Jump: 20m runway, reward = position + 0.01×speed + 0.1×height + 30×jump length; feet must not cross the take-off line.
  • Hurdles: 110m finish line, 10 hurdles (height 1.067m, first hurdle 13.72m from start, spacing 9.14m); reward only includes distance term; hurdle height randomly sampled 0–1.167m in curriculum.
  • Golf: 1.14m club, clubhead 0.05×0.025×0.02m, wavy terrain amplitude 0.5m; reward includes contact, target distance, and predicted landing point (based on projectile equations).
  • Javelin: SMPL-X humanoid, 2.7m javelin; three-stage reward (grasp → throw → flight), default pose tilted upward 30°.
  • Tennis/Table Tennis: Racket replaces right hand; reward divided into pre-contact (racket close to ball) and post-contact (predicted landing point close to target) stages; table tennis additionally includes N_hit to encourage consecutive hits.
  • Fencing/Boxing: 1v1 adversarial; reward includes orientation, speed, striking (sword tip/hand close to target body part), and scoring (contact force ≥ 50 Nm and distance ≤ 0.1m); trained using alternating-optimization self-play.
  • Soccer/Basketball: Penalty/free-throw tasks use staged rewards (player to ball, ball to goal, ball speed to goal, predicted landing point); team matches add one-time goal rewards; basketball uses SMPL-X and prohibits foot-ball contact.

Motion Priors and Training

  • Baselines: PPO (no prior), AMP (discriminator style reward, weight 0.5), PULSE (AMASS-pretrained 32-dimensional motion representation, frozen prior), PULSE+AMP (combination of both).
  • Human demonstrations: TRAM estimates SMPL parameters from video, PHC physics tracker refines them, serving as positive samples for AMP; ablations show significant performance degradation without PHC.
  • Unified hyperparameters: Batch 1024, LR 5×10⁻⁴, γ 0.99, ϵ 0.2, MLP [2048,1024,512], sample count ≈10⁹; σ is 0.05 for PPO/AMP and 0.3 for PULSE series.

Key Innovations

  1. Integration of SMPL Ecosystem and Video Data Pipeline: This is the first work to integrate SMPL/SMPL-X humanoids, internet video pose estimation (TRAM), and physics tracking (PHC) into a sports simulation benchmark. It makes "learning motion from video" a reproducible standard pipeline rather than a private solution with custom datasets—directly lowering the barrier to acquiring human-like behaviors.
  2. Curriculum Learning as a Necessary Component for Complex Motor Tasks: Ablations show that without curriculum, success rates for high jump (1.5m) and hurdles are both 0%, while with curriculum they reach 100%. This finding reveals the mechanism by which policies fall into local minima under sparse rewards, providing clear guidance for designing subsequent complex motor tasks.
  3. Validation of Complementarity of Motion Priors: PULSE (action-space prior) and AMP (style reward) are not redundant—PULSE provides a learnable human-like action space, while AMP provides task-specific style guidance. Their combination outperforms either alone in table tennis (Avg Hits 1.83 vs PULSE 0.74) and free throws (Suc Rate 89.8% vs PULSE 85.6%), demonstrating the effectiveness of the "prior + style" dual-channel design.

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly list systematic limitations, but they can be inferred from factual points: 1) The team basketball task is "extremely challenging," and the initial reward design cannot produce interesting behaviors, indicating that complex team collaboration scenarios remain unsolved; 2) AMP is highly sensitive to demonstration quality—non-task-specific demonstrations (such as running/dribbling in penalty kicks) cause the discriminator reward to overwhelm the task reward, and models struggle to utilize reference motions of poor quality (noisy, with abrupt speed changes); 3) Competitive tasks (2v2 soccer, basketball) results "do not reach SOTA achieved by more complex systems," indicating that self-play mechanisms remain rudimentary; 4) Some sports (swimming, shooting, ice hockey, 11-a-side soccer, equestrian) are omitted due to simulation constraints or complexity, limiting framework coverage.

Engineering Insights

For reproduction or downstream use, prioritize checking the following: First, curriculum learning is a hard prerequisite for high jump and hurdles—without it, success rates drop to zero; be sure to implement the paper's 4-level bar heights and 0–1.167m hurdle height sampling, otherwise policies will fall into local minima. Second, AMP demonstration data must be task-specific (e.g., tennis only includes swing motions) and must undergo PHC physical refinement—directly using raw TRAM estimates significantly degrades performance (table tennis Avg Hits drops from 1.83 to 0.91). Third, the SMPL-X version of PULSE requires expanding the latent space from 32 to 48 dimensions to accommodate finger degrees of freedom, and σ must be set to 0.3 (rather than PPO's 0.05), otherwise exploration behavior may mismatch. Fourth, all tasks share unified hyperparameters (Batch 1024, LR 5×10⁻⁴, MLP [2048,1024,512]), trainable in 1–3 days on a single RTX 3090, but requiring approximately 10⁹ samples—if resources are limited, prioritize PULSE over AMP as the baseline, as it is more robust in track and field and ball tasks. Fifth, the "initial contact" stage in contact-based tasks (golf, penalty kick, free throw) is the main difficulty, and only the PULSE series can reliably solve it; if downstream tasks involve object manipulation, directly adopt the PULSE+AMP combination rather than PPO alone.
