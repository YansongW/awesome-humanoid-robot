Overview

Neodymium-Iron-Boron Magnet is a rare-earth permanent magnet material primarily composed of neodymium, iron, and boron. It is currently the commercial permanent magnet with the highest magnetic energy product and the strongest overall magnetic performance. In the field of humanoid robots, it serves as the magnetic field source for core actuation components such as Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSM) and frameless torque motors, directly determining the torque density, efficiency, and temperature rise characteristics of joint motors. What it truly changes is not the motor itself, but the physical upper limit of torque output within a limited mass and volume—without neodymium-iron-boron magnets, the high power density requirements of compact humanoid robot joints would be unattainable.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Neodymium-iron-boron magnets belong to the material category, with a value chain tier of upstream (upstream) and a domain of raw materials (01_raw_materials). It is a high-performance rare-earth permanent magnet widely used in servo motors, electric vehicle motors, and high-precision actuators. Its core components are the rare-earth element neodymium (Nd), iron (Fe), and boron (B), manufactured through powder metallurgy or sintering processes, exhibiting extremely high remanence (\(B_r\)), coercivity (\(H_c\)), and maximum energy product (\((BH)_{\max}\)).

In humanoid robot systems, neodymium-iron-boron magnets are not directly visible components but functional materials embedded inside the motor rotor. Their presence allows the motor to establish an air-gap magnetic field without excitation windings, thereby significantly reducing copper losses, improving efficiency, and delivering higher torque at the same volume. Taking the PMSM as an example, the rotor uses neodymium-iron-boron magnets to generate the excitation field, while the stator is fed with AC current to create a rotating field; the two rotate synchronously—this is the most mainstream topology for humanoid robot joint motors.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before the advent of neodymium-iron-boron magnets, motor design was long constrained by the performance bottlenecks of permanent magnet materials. Ferrite magnets, though inexpensive and highly resistant to demagnetization, have low energy products (typically about \(30\text{–}50\,\text{kJ/m}^3\)), resulting in large motor sizes and low torque density; AlNiCo magnets have high remanence but low coercivity, are prone to demagnetization, and contain the strategic metal cobalt. Neither material could simultaneously satisfy the engineering requirements of "high flux density + strong demagnetization resistance + miniaturization."

In 1983, neodymium-iron-boron magnets were invented almost simultaneously by Sumitomo Special Metals of Japan (now Hitachi Metals) and General Motors of the United States, pushing the maximum energy product above \(200\,\text{kJ/m}^3\) (with laboratory values exceeding \(400\,\text{kJ/m}^3\)), making it the strongest commercial permanent magnet material. This breakthrough allowed motor designers to significantly reduce motor size at equivalent torque, or substantially increase output torque at the same volume—this is precisely the physical foundation for the dual requirements of "compactness + high torque density" in humanoid robot joints.

What it truly changed is not the performance parameters of permanent magnet materials, but the design freedom of motors: with neodymium-iron-boron, engineers can fit motors capable of supporting whole-body jumping and running within joint spaces of 50–100 mm in diameter.

Principle Breakdown

① Magnetostatics Fundamentals: How Permanent Magnets Establish the Air-Gap Field

At motor operating frequencies far below the speed of light, displacement current is negligible, and the magnetic field satisfies the magnetostatic equations:

\[ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}, \qquad \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \mu \mathbf{H} \]

where \(\mathbf{H}\) is the magnetic field strength, \(\mathbf{B}\) is the magnetic flux density, and \(\mu\) is the permeability. In the magnetic circuit, a permanent magnet can be equivalently modeled as a constant magnetomotive force source \(F_m = H_c l_m\) (where \(H_c\) is the coercivity and \(l_m\) is the magnet thickness), driving flux along the closed path formed by the stator teeth, air gap, and rotor yoke. The air-gap flux density \(B_g\) directly determines the motor's electromagnetic torque and back-EMF.

② Physical Mechanism of Torque Generation

In permanent magnet motors, the air-gap field is established by neodymium-iron-boron magnets. When current flows through the armature windings, the current-carrying conductors experience the Lorentz force in the magnetic field, generating torque at the rotor radius. The torque constant is \(k_t = N B l r\) (where \(N\) is the number of turns, \(B\) is the magnetic flux density, \(l\) is the effective conductor length, and \(r\) is the radius). With a fixed magnetic field, the motor's output torque is proportional to the armature current. The higher the \(B\) provided by neodymium-iron-boron magnets, the greater the torque produced by the same current—this is the physical chain by which "magnetic performance directly translates into torque density."

③ Demagnetization Characteristics and Safe Operating Point

The demagnetization curve of neodymium-iron-boron magnets (the second-quadrant \(B\!-\!H\) relationship) exhibits a knee point: when the operating point is above the knee, the magnet operates in the linear reversible region; once the knee is crossed, even if external conditions are restored, the magnetic performance cannot fully recover, resulting in irreversible demagnetization. The intrinsic coercivity \(H_{ci}\) is the core indicator of demagnetization resistance—it represents the reverse field required to reduce the internal magnetization to zero. The \(H_{ci}\) of neodymium-iron-boron magnets decreases with rising temperature, so high-temperature applications require higher grades (such as \(H\), \(SH\), and \(UH\) series) to ensure sufficient operating point margin.

④ Eddy Current Losses and Suppression

Neodymium-iron-boron magnets are electrically conductive. During high-speed motor operation, harmonic components in the air-gap field induce eddy currents within the magnet, generating losses and causing temperature rise. The temperature rise further reduces \(H_{ci}\), creating a vicious cycle. In engineering practice, magnets are typically segmented along the axial direction (with insulation between segments) to interrupt eddy current paths and reduce losses.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Tesla Optimus Rotary Joints

The rotary joints of Tesla Optimus reportedly adopt an integrated solution combining frameless torque motors with harmonic reducers. The torque density of a frameless torque motor can be expressed as:

\[ \tau = 2 \pi r^2 l B_{\text{gap}} J_s \]

where \(r\) is the air-gap radius, \(l\) is the axial length, \(B_{\text{gap}}\) is the air-gap flux density, and \(J_s\) is the electric loading. Increasing \(r\) improves torque quadratically, so large-diameter frameless motors can achieve higher torque at the same mass—and high \(B_{\text{gap}}\) is a direct contribution of neodymium-iron-boron magnets.

② Whole-Body Actuator Distribution in Humanoid Robots

Taking a 60–80 kg humanoid robot as an example, leg actuators account for 30–45% of total actuator mass and 35–50% of power, representing the highest torque density requirements. The 6 DoF × 2 joint configuration in the legs requires motors to deliver high instantaneous torque within extremely compact volumes, making neodymium-iron-boron magnets a necessary material to meet this demand.

③ Industrial Servo Motors

High-end servo motors represented by the Yaskawa Sigma-7 series use rare-earth permanent magnet materials (neodymium-iron-boron) in their rotors, paired with high-resolution encoders to achieve millisecond-level dynamic response. This is the most mature large-scale application scenario for neodymium-iron-boron magnets beyond humanoid robots.

Limitations and Boundaries

① High-Temperature Demagnetization Risk

The intrinsic coercivity of neodymium-iron-boron magnets decreases with rising temperature; once the operating point crosses the knee point, irreversible demagnetization occurs. In humanoid robot joints under heavy load and highly dynamic conditions, motor temperature rise is significant; if thermal design is insufficient, magnet performance will permanently degrade. This is the most critical engineering constraint for neodymium-iron-boron in humanoid robot applications.

② Brittleness and Processing Difficulty

Neodymium-iron-boron magnets are hard and brittle with low tensile strength, unable to withstand impact or bending loads. During high-speed rotor rotation, magnets must be secured via adhesive bonding, clips, or external stainless steel sleeves, adding process complexity.

③ Rare-Earth Supply Chain Risk

Neodymium, the core raw material of neodymium-iron-boron, is a rare-earth element. Global rare-earth supply is highly concentrated, and prices are volatile. This directly impacts the material cost and supply chain stability of humanoid robot systems.

④ Eddy Current Losses

The magnets themselves are conductive; at high speeds, eddy current losses cause additional temperature rise, requiring suppression through segmentation and insulation, which adds manufacturing steps.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Stronger neodymium-iron-boron magnets are always better"—Wrong. Higher energy products typically come with a poorer balance between coercivity and temperature tolerance. In high-temperature joint scenarios, selecting excessively high grades may lead to soaring costs with limited benefits; the correct approach is to select the appropriate grade based on operating temperature and demagnetization risk (engineering judgment).

  2. "The demagnetization curve is linear"—Wrong. The demagnetization curve of neodymium-iron-boron bends sharply below the knee point; once the operating point crosses the knee, irreversible demagnetization occurs. Design must ensure that the operating point remains above the knee under the worst-case conditions (maximum temperature + maximum demagnetizing current).

  3. "Neodymium-iron-boron and ferrite are interchangeable"—Wrong. Their energy products differ by severalfold; direct substitution would result in severely insufficient motor torque or a substantial increase in size. The compact space of humanoid robot joints makes neodymium-iron-boron almost the only choice.

  4. "Magnets only affect torque magnitude"—Wrong. Magnet performance also affects back-EMF, efficiency, temperature rise, and control bandwidth. Insufficient flux density makes field-weakening control difficult in the high-speed region, limiting the speed range.
