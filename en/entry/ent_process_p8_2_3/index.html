Overview

Lightweight and topology optimization is a critical process node in the structural strength simulation and iteration phase (P8) of humanoid robot development. Its core task is to reduce structural weight through topology optimization, size optimization, rib/ stiffener placement, and composite material substitution—while maintaining sufficient stress margins—thereby reducing overall inertia and energy consumption. What it truly changes is not just the single metric of "how many grams of material are removed," but rather establishing a closed-loop iterative relationship among "design—simulation—manufacturing" with weight as the constraint, strength as the baseline, and manufacturability as the boundary.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Lightweight and topology optimization is a systematic class of methods in structural design. Its goal is to find the optimal spatial distribution of material through mathematical optimization algorithms or engineering heuristics, subject to performance constraints such as strength, stiffness, and fatigue life. In the humanoid robot development workflow, this process is located within the structural strength simulation and iteration phase (P8), numbered P8.2.3. It receives structural strength simulation results from upstream and delivers optimized CAD models and weight-reduction reports downstream.

This process encompasses five core methods: topology optimization (finding the optimal material distribution within a given design space), size optimization (adjusting parameters such as wall thickness and cross-sectional dimensions), rib/stiffener placement (improving structural efficiency by adding local stiffness rather than increasing overall thickness), and composite material substitution (replacing metallic materials with lightweight, high-strength materials such as carbon fiber). What it truly changes is not just the single point of "whether the calculation is accurate," but rather establishing a traceable, iterative closed-loop relationship among "design—simulation—manufacturing."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the field of humanoid robots, uncontrolled weight is the number one enemy of structural design. In early development, structural design often relied on a "double safety net" of empirical formulas and safety factors—thicker materials, larger fillets, and denser stiffeners. The direct consequence of this approach is weight escalation, which for humanoid robots requiring single-leg support and dynamic walking translates into reduced battery life, degraded dynamic performance, and overloaded joint actuators.

Lightweight and topology optimization is a systematic solution specifically targeting this pain point. Its historical positioning can be summarized as: a paradigm shift from "experience-based weight reduction" to "algorithm-driven optimization." Traditional weight reduction relies on the engineer's personal experience, making results non-reproducible and non-quantifiable. Topology optimization, by contrast, delegates the decision of "where material should exist and where it should be hollowed out" to mathematical algorithms, which automatically generate material distribution schemes under given loads and constraints—producing results that are reproducible, verifiable, and traceable.

In the humanoid robot development workflow, the key constraints of this process are manufacturing process limitations and cost. Topology optimization often outputs complex organic shapes; if these cannot be manufactured using existing processes (such as CNC machining, casting, or 3D printing), the optimization results are meaningless. Therefore, this process must be tightly coupled with DFM/DFA reviews, explicitly incorporating manufacturing constraints into the optimization objectives.

Principle Breakdown

① Topology Optimization: The Mathematical Essence of the SIMP Method

The core idea of topology optimization is to find a material density distribution function ρ(x) ∈ [0,1] within the design domain Ω that minimizes structural compliance (i.e., maximizes stiffness) while satisfying a volume constraint. The most commonly used method in engineering is SIMP (Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization), whose basic approach treats material density as the design variable and uses a penalty factor p to drive intermediate densities toward 0 or 1:

\[ \min_{\rho} \; C(\rho) = \mathbf{U}^T \mathbf{K}(\rho) \mathbf{U} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \frac{V(\rho)}{V_0} \le f, \quad 0 \le \rho_e \le 1 \]

where C is the structural compliance, K is the global stiffness matrix, U is the displacement vector, V(ρ)/V₀ is the volume fraction constraint, and f is the target volume ratio. The penalty factor p is typically set to 3, causing intermediate-density elements to be "penalized" during optimization and driven toward 0 or 1, thereby yielding a clear topological configuration.

② Size Optimization: Parametric Optimization on the Topological Skeleton

Topology optimization provides the qualitative answer to "where material should exist," while size optimization addresses the quantitative question of "how thick it should be." On the skeleton obtained from topology optimization, wall thickness, cross-sectional dimensions, fillet radii, and other parameters serve as design variables, with stress, deformation, and modal frequency as constraints, and minimum weight as the objective. The computational cost of size optimization is far lower than that of topology optimization, making it suitable for fine-tuning after the topological scheme is established.

③ Rib/Stiffener Placement: Geometric Stiffness Instead of Material Accumulation

The essence of stiffeners is to replace "material stiffness" with "geometric stiffness"—improving bending stiffness by altering the cross-sectional moment of inertia rather than increasing material usage. For thin-walled structures subjected to bending loads, strategically placing stiffeners can significantly enhance stiffness with minimal added weight. Engineering judgment: for long rod-like structures in humanoid robots such as thighs, shins, and arms, reasonable longitudinal stiffener placement can typically improve bending stiffness by more than 30% while increasing weight by less than 5% (engineering judgment; specific values must be confirmed based on the actual cross-section and load cases).

④ Composite Material Substitution: Engineering Utilization of Anisotropy

Carbon fiber composites offer significantly higher specific strength (strength/density) and specific stiffness (elastic modulus/density) than metallic materials. Replacing aluminum alloy or steel structural components with carbon fiber composites can achieve weight reductions of 30%–50% at equivalent strength (engineering judgment; specific values depend on layup design and load direction). However, the anisotropic nature of composites requires designers to precisely understand load directions; otherwise, insufficient strength may occur in non-principal directions.

In the field of humanoid robot structural lightweighting, the four primary methods each have their applicable scenarios:

Method Advantages Limitations Applicable Scenarios
Topology optimization Automatic optimization, can discover counterintuitive configurations Results require manufacturability treatment, high computational cost Initial stage of new designs, complex load-bearing components
Size optimization Fast computation, clear parameters Depends on initial topology, cannot change configuration Fine-tuning of existing topological schemes
Rib/stiffener placement Simple manufacturing, low cost, minimal added weight Relies on experience, poor adaptability to complex loads Thin-walled shells, long rod-like structures
Composite material substitution Significant weight reduction, high specific strength High cost, complex anisotropic design Structural components extremely sensitive to weight

Who Uses It · Application Cases

In the humanoid robot development workflow, the direct users of this process are structural engineers and simulation engineers. Upstream, it depends on the stress contour plots and deformation results from P8.2.2 structural strength simulation; downstream, it delivers to the P8.2.4 structural optimization iteration confirmation step, with final outputs entering DFM/DFA review and mold design.

Typical application scenarios include:

  1. Thigh/shin structural components: Subject to bending and torsional loads, hollowed configurations are obtained through topology optimization, followed by stiffener placement to compensate for local stiffness deficiencies;
  2. Arm/forearm structural components: Sensitive to inertia, wall thickness is reduced through size optimization, combined with composite material substitution for further weight reduction;
  3. Complex castings such as pelvis/scapula: Optimal material distribution is obtained through topology optimization, followed by manufacturability treatment incorporating casting process constraints.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary conditions of lightweight and topology optimization must be clearly defined:

  1. Manufacturing process limitations: The organic shapes output by topology optimization are often difficult to realize with traditional CNC machining, requiring manufacturability treatment (such as adding draft angles and minimum wall thickness constraints);
  2. Cost constraints: Although composite material substitution offers significant weight reduction, material and manufacturing costs far exceed those of metals, requiring a trade-off between weight-reduction benefits and cost;
  3. Load uncertainty: Topology optimization relies on precise load definitions, yet the actual loads on humanoid robots during dynamic walking are complex and variable; the accuracy of the load spectrum directly affects the reliability of optimization results;
  4. Fatigue and impact: Topology optimization typically uses static strength as the constraint; fatigue life under cyclic loading and dynamic response under impact loading require separate verification.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Topology optimization results can be directly used for manufacturing"—Wrong. Topology optimization outputs conceptual configurations that must undergo geometric reconstruction and manufacturability treatment (adding draft angles, minimum wall thickness, fillets, etc.) before engineering use;
  2. "The more weight reduction, the better"—Wrong. Weight reduction must be performed with sufficient stress margins; excessive reduction leads to insufficient stiffness, degraded vibration modes, and even fatigue failure;
  3. "Topology optimization can replace strength simulation"—Wrong. Topology optimization and strength simulation are complementary: topology optimization is used to "find the configuration," while strength simulation is used to "verify strength"; the two must be performed iteratively;
  4. "Composite material substitution is just swapping materials"—Wrong. The anisotropy of composites requires redesigning ply orientations and layer counts; directly replacing metallic materials often results in substandard performance or uncontrolled costs.
