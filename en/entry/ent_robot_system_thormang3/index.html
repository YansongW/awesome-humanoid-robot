Overview

THORMANG3 (Tactical Hazardous Operations Robot 3) is a full-sized humanoid robot from South Korea's ROBOTIS, originating from the DARPA Robotics Challenge 2015 finals platform (Team ROBOTIS). The entire robot stands 137.5 cm tall, weighs 42 kg, and its 29 degrees of freedom are driven by 29 DYNAMIXEL-P series integrated servos. Two Intel NUCs handle motion control and perception respectively, equipped with ankle six-axis force/torque sensors and a complete ROS1 software stack. The full STP model is officially public (source: research archive thormang3.md, same below).

What it truly changes is not the label of "open-source humanoid robot"—but rather delivering a DRC-level full-sized bipedal platform to researchers through a combination of "commercial complete robot + open-source control software + public 3D model," giving whole-body control (WBC) and model predictive control (MPC) algorithm research a reproducible hardware platform for the first time.

What It Is: Precise Definition

THORMANG3 belongs to the robot_system type, positioned at the midstream value chain level, with an intelligence level of intelligence. It is essentially a full-sized humanoid robot research platform—not a consumer toy, nor industrial specialized equipment, but a programmable, modifiable bipedal robot system designed for university laboratories and research institutes.

From a product lineage perspective, THORMANG3 is the flagship model of ROBOTIS's humanoid robot product line, succeeding the DRC-HUBO+ (developed in collaboration with KAIST), and complementing the smaller ROBOTIS OP3 platform: the OP3, at approximately 40 cm tall, is used for algorithm validation, while THORMANG3 provides real-scale dynamics and perception challenges.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before THORMANG3, full-sized humanoid robot research faced a dilemma: Boston Dynamics Atlas and NASA Valkyrie offered top-tier performance but were extremely expensive and not commercially available; Honda's ASIMO was lease-only; and self-built platforms required simultaneous progress in mechanics, electronics, and control—a laboratory often needed 3–5 years to produce a prototype capable of stable walking.

THORMANG3's historical positioning is that of a "relatively affordable full-sized platform"—it packages DRC finals-level hardware capabilities (harmonic drives + high power-density servos) into a standard product, paired with an open-source ROS1 software stack and public STP models, allowing researchers to skip mechanical design and low-level actuation and directly enter whole-body control, gait planning, and perception algorithm research. What it truly changes is not "whether humanoid robots can be built," but "how many people and how many years a laboratory needs before starting humanoid robot algorithm research."

Principle Breakdown

① Modular Joints: DYNAMIXEL-P Integrated Servos

All 29 degrees of freedom of THORMANG3 are driven by DYNAMIXEL-P series (formerly DYNAMIXEL PRO, renamed from June 2019) integrated servos. Each servo integrates the motor, harmonic reducer, encoder, driver, and communication interface into a single mechanical unit, networked via RS-485 serial bus. Three models are tiered by power:

Model Power Quantity Deployment Location
PH54-200-S500-R 200 W 10 Large leg joints
PH54-100-S500-R 100 W 11 Medium-load joints
PH42-020-S300-R 20 W 8 Small joints

This "harmonic drive + high power-density servo" approach is the typical architecture for full-sized platforms from the DRC era, providing ample torque but with expensive individual servos—the bulk of the total cost is concentrated here (engineering judgment).

② Dual-Computer Division: Motion Control and Perception Decoupling

The computing system uses 2 Intel NUCs (Core i5, 8GB DDR4, 128GB M.2 SSD) with clear role separation:

  • Motion PC (MPC): Runs real-time motion control and whole-body control algorithms;
  • Perception PC (PPC): Processes camera, LiDAR, and IMU data.

The two computers communicate via an onboard D-Link DIR-806A wireless router. This "control-perception separation" architecture prevents vision processing from preempting control cycles—a classic design pattern for real-time robotic systems.

③ Force Sensing: Ankle Six-Axis Force/Torque Sensors

Each ankle is equipped with 1 ATI Mini58 six-axis force/torque sensor to measure ground reaction forces. This is critical feedback for bipedal stability control—without force sensing, the robot can only walk open-loop based on kinematic models, with extremely poor disturbance rejection. Combined with the MicroStrain 3DM-GX4-25 IMU providing attitude reference, this forms a complete "force + inertial" state estimation input.

THORMANG3's core differentiator lies in the dual openness of "open-source hardware + open-source software"—the officially public STP 3D model means researchers can modify structural components themselves or even 3D-print replacement parts, which is extremely rare among full-sized platforms.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

THORMANG3's primary users are university robotics laboratories and research institutes, with typical application scenarios including:

  1. Whole-Body Control (WBC) Algorithm Validation: 29 degrees of freedom plus ankle force sensors make it an ideal WBC testbed, allowing researchers to validate QP solvers and task-priority management on real hardware;
  2. Model Predictive Control (MPC) Gait Research: The dual Intel NUCs provide sufficient compute for real-time MPC solving, enabling dynamic walking and disturbance rejection experiments;
  3. Teleoperation and Autonomous Navigation: The ROS1 software stack plus optional LiDAR/camera supports SLAM and teleoperation research;
  4. DRC Task Reproduction: As the direct successor to the DRC finals platform, it can be used for algorithm research on disaster response tasks (valve operation, stair walking, etc.).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Price Barrier: No official public pricing; distributor pages state "quote required, 12-week lead time"—the total cost is primarily driven by the 29 DYNAMIXEL-P servos, with individual 200W-class servos being expensive (engineering judgment), requiring budget approval for actual procurement;
  2. Limited Computing Power: The Intel NUCs (Core i5, 8GB DDR4) are entry-level by the mid-to-late 2020s, struggling with modern vision foundation models or large-scale RL training;
  3. Aging ROS1 Ecosystem: The software stack is based on ROS1, while the community has largely migrated to ROS2—compatibility with newer algorithm libraries requires independent verification;
  4. No Dexterous Hands: End effectors must be configured by the user; multi-fingered dexterous hands are not included by default;
  5. Low Beginner-friendliness: Assessed at 1/5 in the research archive—requires foundational knowledge in ROS, C++, and control theory, making it unsuitable for beginners.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "THORMANG3 is an open-source robot"—inaccurate. The hardware is a commercial product; what is open-source is the ROS software packages and STP 3D model; the COMMON package license is marked as "Other/Unspecified," requiring caution for commercial use;
  2. "29 degrees of freedom means 29 ordinary servos"—wrong. DYNAMIXEL-P is an industrial-grade integrated servo with harmonic reducers and absolute encoders, with per-unit costs far exceeding hobby servos;
  3. "Having THORMANG3 means you can run DRC tasks"—not necessarily. The DRC finals platform underwent extensive customization and tuning; THORMANG3 is a research platform, not an out-of-the-box task robot;
  4. "The ROS1 software stack can be directly migrated to ROS2"—requires independent verification. THORMANG3's software packages are based on ROS1, and migration involves systematic changes to message types, node lifecycles, and more.
