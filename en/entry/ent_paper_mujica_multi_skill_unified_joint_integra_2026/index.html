Overview

MUJICA is a unified control architecture for wheel-legged robots proposed by the research team, aiming to integrate multiple skills with significantly different dynamics—such as omnidirectional locomotion, high-platform climbing, and fall recovery—into a single proprioceptive policy, with a high-level skill selector enabling automatic switching without external perception. Its core contribution lies in introducing precise DC motor constraint modeling and a safety-aware constrained reinforcement learning framework, which improves motor performance utilization while ensuring reliable sim-to-real transfer and safe real-world deployment.

What It Changes

The wheel-legged robot field has long faced a structural contradiction: the efficiency of wheeled driving and the flexibility of legged obstacle traversal are difficult to unify at the control level. Most existing frameworks are directly adapted from legged robot methods, inheriting their "insensitivity" to dynamic differences—when skills expand from flat-ground walking to sprinting, pacing, or climbing, these methods either handle only similar skills or rely on manual switching, failing to adaptively integrate them within a single policy. More critically, most learning methods model actuators with simplified drive constraints, completely ignoring the physical limits of DC motor torque varying with speed and joint position. Such simplifications may be harmless in simulation, but on real hardware they cause policies to frequently request torques beyond motor physical limits, leading to performance degradation at best, or triggering protection mechanisms and even hardware damage at worst.

What MUJICA truly changes is not "yet another multi-skill RL framework," but elevating "motor physical constraints" from post-training engineering patches to first-class citizens during training. It reformulates the problem as a constrained partially observable Markov decision process (C-POMDP), requiring the policy to satisfy k hard constraints while optimizing long-term rewards. This shift means the policy is no longer allowed to "fantasize" actions beyond motor capabilities, but must find optimal solutions within constraint boundaries. Meanwhile, by introducing a skill indicator variable to decouple observation spaces of different skills along a specific dimension, MUJICA enables a single policy network to accommodate skills with vastly different dynamics without training separate networks for each skill—providing architectural possibilities for autonomous continuous operation of wheel-legged robots in complex real-world environments.

Method Breakdown

MUJICA adopts a hierarchical architecture: the low level is a multi-skill unified policy, and the high level is a skill selector, both trained based on proprioception.

Problem Formulation and Observation/Action Spaces

  • The problem is formulated as a C-POMDP, aiming to maximize long-term rewards while satisfying k constraints (Eq. 1).
  • The observation vector contains only proprioceptive signals: o_t = [ω_t^b, g_t^b, cmd_t, q_t, q̇_t, a_t, ζ_t]^T (Eq. 2), where ζ_t is the skill indicator variable, with each skill associated with a unique indicator value, decoupling observation spaces of different skills along this dimension.
  • The privileged state s_t = [o_t, v_t, c_t, u_t, h_t]^T (Eq. 3) includes base linear velocity, collision probability, wheel-ground distance, and latent state, available only during training.
  • Action space: leg joints output angular offsets relative to default postures, wheel joints output desired motor speeds; converted via PD controllers: wheel joints τ = K_d(q̇ - a), other joints τ = K_p(q - a) - K_d·q̇ (Eq. 4).

State Estimator

  • Based on the past H=6 frames of observations, a GRU network processes temporal dependencies to predict latent states, base linear velocity, per-part collision probabilities, and wheel-ground distance (Eq. 5).
  • A reference encoder maps subsequent observations to latent states as supervision targets, using SwAV contrastive learning loss.
  • Total loss L_Estimate = L_Pred + L_SwAV(e_t, ê_t) (Eq. 6), where L_Pred includes velocity MSE, collision BCE, and wheel-ground distance MSE (Eq. 7).

Asymmetric Actor-Critic and Constrained Optimization

  • The policy network receives historical embeddings f_t from the online encoder and current observation o_t; the reward critic and constraint critics receive privileged observations.
  • The P3O framework handles constrained optimization, with loss L_P3O(θ) = L_R_CLIP(θ) + κ·Σ_i max{0, L_Ci_CLIP(θ)} (Eq. 8), where κ is the penalty factor.

DC Motor Constraint Modeling

  • Each actuator is subject to speed-dependent torque limits: maximum torque is constant at low speeds and decreases linearly with speed at high speeds.
  • Shank joint torque is affected by both joint speed and joint position; maximum torque exhibits cosine dependence on joint position at fixed speeds.
  • Numerical values are sourced from the Unitree official motor manual, ensuring constraints align with real hardware.

Skill Selector

  • The high-level selector receives the most recent H frames of observations (excluding ζ_t) and outputs a probability distribution over a predefined skill set.
  • Trained using an asymmetric actor-critic network on top of the frozen low-level skill policy (Training S2), learning through a unified velocity tracking reward.

Curriculum Learning

  • A 33×20 grid multi-task environment is constructed, with 4,096 robots assigned to different rows (corresponding to specific terrains and tasks), each column representing a curriculum difficulty level.
  • Omnidirectional locomotion and high-platform climbing advance the curriculum after successfully maintaining velocity tracking; fall recovery advances when the robot approaches a standard standing posture.

Key Innovations

  1. End-to-End Embedding of Precise DC Motor Constraints: Speed-dependent and position-dependent torque limits are directly modeled into the C-POMDP constraints rather than post-hoc clipping. This is a fundamental correction to existing RL control frameworks—the policy learns to act within motor physical boundaries during training. Ablation experiments show that without constraints, over 90% of samples fall outside the feasible motor region, while with constraints, the violation rate drops below 3.5%. This design directly determines the safety of sim-to-real transfer.

  2. Skill Indicator Variable-Driven Observation Space Decoupling: By separating observation spaces of different skills along a single dimension via ζ_t, a single policy network can accommodate skills with vastly different dynamics (e.g., fall recovery vs. sprinting) without training separate networks or manual switching. The scalability of this mechanism means future skills only require assigning new indicator values without redesigning the architecture.

  3. Pure Proprioceptive Automatic Switching via High-Level Skill Selector: The selector outputs skill probability distributions based solely on the most recent H frames of observations (excluding ζ_t), trained on top of the frozen low-level policy. This enables the robot to autonomously complete the continuous task chain of "fall → recover → climb → move" in real environments without external perception or human intervention. Real-world demonstrations successfully completed continuous tasks from random fall postures to climbing a 60 cm high platform.

Experiments and Results

Experiments were conducted on the IsaacLab simulation platform with 4,096 parallel environments on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, using the Unitree Go2-W robot platform. Low-level training ran for 30,000 iterations, high-level training for 10,000 iterations, with a control frequency of 50 Hz and physics simulation at 200 Hz. Test setups included 5 representative tasks, 10 increasing difficulty levels (level intervals doubled relative to training), and 4 random seeds.

Real-World Experiments

  • Stair recovery: Successfully recovered from an inverted state on a 30° staircase.
  • Stair climbing: Successfully climbed stairs with varying step heights and irregular edges.
  • High-platform climbing: Successfully climbed an 80 cm outdoor platform and a 1 m indoor platform (near mechanical and actuation limits).

Results demonstrate that the skill indicator variable is critical for multi-skill switching—all three tasks failed under shared rewards, high-platform climbing reached only 38% under separate rewards, while the full MUJICA improved success rates across all three tasks to over 90%. The DC motor constraint ablation validates the decisive role of constraint modeling in policy safety.

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly discuss limitations. Inferred boundaries from factual points include: real-world experiments only validated stair recovery, stair climbing, and high-platform climbing, without testing fall recovery in the real world; the skill selector was only evaluated at difficulty level 5/10, leaving switching robustness at higher difficulties unknown; only the Unitree Go2-W platform was validated, without generalization to other wheel-legged robots; no detailed analysis of computational resource requirements or inference latency was provided, with the real-world computing platform being NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX but without specifying resource utilization margins. Additionally, DC motor constraint values come from the Unitree official manual, requiring recalibration for other motor models.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing MUJICA, prioritize verifying the following aspects: First, DC motor constraint values must strictly match the target robot's official manual—this is key to reducing violation rates from 90% to 3.5% in ablations and is the foundation of sim-to-real safety; if motor manual data is unavailable, it is recommended to fit torque-speed-position curves through real measurements first. Second, the encoding of the skill indicator variable ζ_t directly affects multi-skill decoupling effectiveness; Table III shows complete failure under shared rewards and only 38% for high-platform climbing under separate rewards, so assigning unique indicator values per skill and correctly passing them in the observation vector is essential. Third, curriculum advancement conditions must be precisely reproduced: omnidirectional locomotion and high-platform climbing advance after successfully maintaining velocity tracking, while fall recovery advances when approaching a standard standing posture; overly loose or tight conditions will affect training stability. Fourth, the low-level policy must be frozen during high-level skill selector training, otherwise learned skills will be disrupted. The most error-prone area is the SwAV contrastive loss in the state estimator—incorrect synchronization between the reference encoder and online encoder updates can cause latent state drift, degrading collision probability and wheel-ground distance prediction accuracy, ultimately weakening constraint satisfaction. It is recommended to first reproduce the DC motor constraint ablation in simulation (without vs. with constraints) to confirm violation rate metrics before proceeding to full training.
