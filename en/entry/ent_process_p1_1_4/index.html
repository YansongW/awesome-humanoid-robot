Overview

Human Factors Engineering and Interaction Requirements (P1.1.4) is a level-3 subtask within the requirements definition and system concept phase (Concept / Pre-A). Its core mission is to transform the vague requirement that "the height, field of view, sound, and actions of robots in human-robot coexistence scenarios must be acceptable" into quantifiable, verifiable, and traceable engineering constraints. What it truly changes is not the aesthetic quality of the interaction interface, but the decision-making approach "from intuitive judgment to data-driven" — by leveraging four key tools: anthropometric databases, reachability analysis, startle response experiments, and HMI prototypes, it turns human physiological limits and psychological expectations into design inputs.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Human Factors Engineering and Interaction Requirements is a level-3 subtask under the requirements engineering work package in Phase P1, numbered P1.1.4. It focuses on whether the geometric dimensions, range of motion, sensory outputs (visual/auditory), and behavioral patterns of humanoid robots operating in real physical spaces alongside humans fall within an acceptable range for humans.

In WBS V3, this subtask is decomposed into five level-4 key actions: P1.1.4.1 Input Review and Target Quantification, P1.1.4.2 Approach/Method Design, P1.1.4.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication, P1.1.4.4 Verification and Issue Closure, and P1.1.4.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery. Each action has clear inputs, methods, outputs, and acceptance criteria, forming a complete closed loop.

Its completion criteria/deliverables include a three-part package: the Human Factors Requirements Report (documenting all quantified metrics and their rationale), the Key Interaction Posture Envelope (defining the allowable motion ranges of robot joints in interaction scenarios), and the HMI Principles (governing basic specifications for subsequent interface and interaction design).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early humanoid robot development, human factors engineering was often deferred until after prototype integration. The consequences were disastrous: mechanical engineers selected motors based on load and speed but overlooked that the robot's head height was exactly at children's eye level — the camera and speaker positions made children feel intimidated; software engineers defined rich voice interactions but failed to account for misoperations caused by startle responses in noisy environments; industrial design pursued a sci-fi aesthetic but left elderly users unable to understand the robot's intent.

The common root of these problems is: human factors constraints were not front-loaded into the requirements phase. By the time they were discovered, modifying the mechanical structure meant re-tooling molds, and changing interaction logic meant overhauling the software architecture — costs escalated exponentially. The purpose of P1.1.4 is to answer, with data at the concept stage, the three questions: "Can humans accept it, are they willing to accept it, and will they be frightened by it?"

Principle Breakdown

① Anthropometric Database: Statistical Foundation for Geometric Adaptation

The height, arm span, and operating surface height of a humanoid robot must cover the body dimension distribution of the target user group. Anthropometric databases (e.g., GB 10000, NHANES) provide static dimension data at various percentiles (P5, P50, P95). The design principle is: key interaction surfaces (e.g., touchscreens, pickup openings) should accommodate the P5 to P95 range — meaning shorter users can reach them, and taller users do not need to bend excessively. This principle directly determines the upper and lower bounds of the robot's torso height, arm length, and joint travel.

② Reachability Analysis: Intersection of Kinematics and Safety Boundaries

The Reachability Envelope is the spatial set that the robot's end effector can reach under joint limits. In human factors engineering, reachability analysis computes the intersection of the robot's kinematic reachable space with the human's "comfortable interaction zone":

\[ \mathcal{R}_{\text{interaction}} = \mathcal{R}_{\text{robot}} \cap \mathcal{R}_{\text{human, comfortable}} \]

where \(\mathcal{R}_{\text{robot}}\) is determined by joint angle limits \(\theta_{\min} \le \theta_i \le \theta_{\max}\), and \(\mathcal{R}_{\text{human, comfortable}}\) is the spatial range within which a human can operate comfortably in a natural standing/sitting posture without excessive stretching. Regions outside the intersection are either unreachable by the robot or uncomfortable for the human — both should be identified and eliminated during the design phase.

③ Startle Response Experiments: Quantitative Means for Psychological Safety

The Startle Response is a human's instinctive defensive reaction to sudden stimuli (loud noises, rapidly approaching objects), manifesting as muscle tension, retreat, and increased heart rate. In human-robot coexistence scenarios, sudden robot movements, rapid gestures, or abnormal sounds can trigger startle responses, potentially causing users to fall or misoperate. Startle response experiments measure participants' physiological signals (heart rate, EMG) and behavioral reactions (retreat distance, avoidance time) under controlled conditions to determine safe motion speed thresholds and minimum approach distances. These data are directly translated into speed limits and safety distance parameters in robot motion planning.

④ HMI Prototypes: Rapid Validation Carriers for Interaction Logic

HMI (Human-Machine Interface) prototypes are the vehicles that translate the quantified constraints above into perceivable interaction experiences. They can be software interface simulations, physical button panels, or simple robot heads with screens. The purpose of HMI prototypes is to validate whether interaction logic is clear, feedback is timely, and error recovery is natural before investing in expensive hardware. They allow designers and users to "experience" the final product at low cost, thereby identifying breaks in the interaction flow at the concept stage.

The key distinction is: P1.1.4 answers "what humans need," while P1.2 answers "how the system satisfies it." The former defines constraints; the latter implements solutions.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Case 1: Community Pharmacy Assistant Robot (Role-Play and Hierarchical Task Analysis)

In the development of a community pharmacy assistant robot, researchers employed a role-play method — pharmacists enacted the robot's role in a controlled environment, capturing real customer needs and behavioral patterns through improvised interactions. Subsequently, Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA) was used to decompose observed behaviors into hierarchical task structures, ensuring accurate modeling of each interaction step (e.g., inquiry, medication retrieval, usage guidance). This method directly serves the "approach/method design" phase of P1.1.4, systematically capturing expert tacit knowledge and mapping it to robot control code.

Case 2: Social Robot Facial Emotion Expression (Startle Response and Acceptance Experiments)

A study on facial emotion recognition in social robots showed that fully animated projected faces (whether human-like or mechanical in appearance) achieved emotion recognition rates not significantly different from human baselines; however, when only the eye region was displayed, recognition accuracy dropped across all conditions, with human-like appearance robots (accuracy ~65%) significantly outperforming mechanical appearance robots (accuracy ~52%). This finding directly guides HMI design: if the robot's face is partially occluded (e.g., wearing a hat or in low-light environments), a human-like appearance better maintains emotion communication effectiveness, thereby reducing user confusion and discomfort.

Case 3: Dynamic Personality Design (Psychological Safety in Long-Term Interaction)

Socially Assistive Robots (SARs) operating in highly sensitive environments impose high demands on safety and user communication. Research notes that existing studies predominantly focus on different communication modalities (e.g., voice, gestures, expressions) but lack longitudinal studies revealing how user communication needs evolve over time. Dynamic personality design advocates: adopting a transparent, outgoing "acquaintance" personality in the initial phase to build initial trust, then transitioning to a gentler, habituated personality to sustain long-term interaction. This approach directly addresses the key constraints of "psychological safety" and "user group diversity" in P1.1.4 — different users and different phases require different interaction styles.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary of P1.1.4 is that it defines requirements and constraints, not specific implementation solutions. It tells the design team "the robot's head height should fall within the target population's percentile range (example range 1400–1650mm, engineering recommendation, subject to verification against target market anthropometric data)," but does not specify "which lifting mechanism to use"; it requires "maximum approach speed not exceeding X m/s," but does not specify "which sensor to use for distance detection." These implementation details fall within the scope of P1.2 System Concept Design.

Another boundary is that human factors data exhibit cultural and group specificity. The P5–P95 body dimension range is based on statistical data from specific populations, with significant distribution differences across countries, age groups, and genders. The outputs of P1.1.4 must explicitly state data sources and applicable populations; otherwise, adaptation deviations will occur in cross-market deployment. Additionally, conclusions from startle response experiments are influenced by experimental environments and participant samples, requiring safety margins (engineering judgment) when extrapolating to real-world scenarios.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Human factors engineering is just about appearance design" — Incorrect. Appearance is only one part of HMI; the core of human factors engineering is quantified constraints: height, speed, distance, response time — each is a testable engineering parameter, not an aesthetic preference.

  2. "Having an anthropometric database is sufficient" — Not sufficient. Databases provide static dimensions, but interaction is a dynamic process: human joint range of motion, reaction time, and cognitive load are equally critical. P1.1.4 requires combining reachability analysis, startle response experiments, and HMI prototype validation to cover dynamic interaction scenarios.

  3. "Startle response experiments are only for children" — Incorrect. Different age groups and cultural backgrounds exhibit significantly different response thresholds to sudden stimuli. Experimental design must cover the diversity of the target user group; otherwise, safety parameters may be too loose or too strict for certain populations.

  4. "HMI prototype validation can be deferred to the prototype phase" — Incorrect. The very purpose of HMI prototypes is to identify interaction logic flaws before hardware investment. Deferring validation means leaving problems to be fixed at the most costly stage, contradicting the fundamental goal of "front-loaded constraints" in Phase P1.
