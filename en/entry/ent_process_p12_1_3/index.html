Overview

Human/object detection and tracking is a core process node in the perception system of humanoid robots, belonging to the VLA / WAM / AI algorithm integration (AI & Perception) work package. It provides real-time perceptual input for human-robot interaction, obstacle avoidance, and manipulation tasks through technologies such as 2D/3D detection, multi-object tracking, and human pose estimation. The key constraints of this process lie in occlusion, illumination variation, and real-time performance, with its completion criteria centered on quantitative metrics such as detection accuracy, tracking ID stability, and latency.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Human/object detection and tracking refers to the spatial localization, category recognition, and temporal continuous association of humans and objects within the field of view in a robot's perception system. It is not a single algorithm but a complete perception pipeline: detection answers "what is in the field of view and where" (2D bounding boxes or 3D bounding boxes), tracking answers "how these targets are associated over time" (ID maintenance and trajectory estimation), and pose estimation further answers "what is the spatial configuration of human joints" (2D keypoints or 3D skeletons).

In the full-process development of humanoid robots, P12.1.3 serves as the bridge between the perception layer and the decision layer—it outputs not raw pixels but structured, target-level information that is directly consumed by subsequent obstacle avoidance planning, grasping operations, and human-robot interaction strategies.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early robot systems, perception was often "an isolated island customized for a single task": obstacle avoidance systems only detected obstacles, grasping systems only recognized workpieces, and interaction systems only tracked faces. This fragmented design led to three long-standing pain points:

  1. Disconnect between perception and action: Detection results lacked temporal consistency, making it impossible for robots to determine whether an object was static or moving, let alone predict its trajectory.
  2. Redundant construction across tasks: Each subsystem trained its own detection model, multiplying data annotation costs, with no sharing between models.
  3. Failure in human-robot coexistence scenarios: In unstructured environments such as homes and hospitals, frequent occlusion and varying illumination sharply degrade single-frame detection accuracy, while tracking can compensate for single-frame perception deficiencies through temporal context.

The historical positioning of P12.1.3 is precisely to integrate "detection" and "tracking" from independent modules into a unified perception process, making it the perceptual foundation of the robot's "embodied intelligence." What it truly changes is not the fact of "seeing" itself, but "understanding a continuously changing world"—from single-frame snapshots to spatiotemporally consistent scene understanding.

Principle Breakdown

① Detection: From Sliding Windows to End-to-End Regression

Modern 2D detectors (e.g., YOLO series, DETR series) model object detection as a dense prediction problem. End-to-end methods represented by DETR directly output object sets through the attention mechanism of Transformers, without hand-designed anchor boxes or post-processing. 3D detection extends this by adding a depth estimation branch, outputting the target's center, dimensions, and orientation. For humanoid robots, 3D detection typically relies on RGB-D cameras or LiDAR, with the core formula:

\[ [x_c, y_c, z_c, w, h, l, \theta] = f_{\text{det}}(I, D) \]

where \(I\) is the RGB image, \(D\) is the depth map, and the output is the target's center coordinates, 3D dimensions, and orientation angle.

② Tracking: Data Association and State Estimation

The core of multi-object tracking (MOT) is data association—matching current-frame detections with existing trajectories. Classical methods use the Hungarian algorithm to solve the optimal matching, where the cost matrix is constructed from both motion models (e.g., Kalman filter predictions) and appearance features (e.g., Re-ID features). Tracking ID stability is a key metric for system quality: the fewer ID switches, the more robust the system is to occlusion and crossing scenarios.

③ Human Pose Estimation: From Keypoints to Skeletons

Human pose estimation is divided into two routes: top-down (detect the person first, then estimate keypoints) and bottom-up (detect all keypoints first, then cluster them into skeletons). For humanoid robots, pose estimation matters not only for understanding human action intent but also for providing reference data for imitation learning—by observing the joint angle trajectories of human operators, robots can learn similar motion strategies.

Horizontal Comparison

In the field of robot perception, multiple technical routes exist for human/object detection and tracking:

Technical Route Advantages Disadvantages Applicable Scenarios
2D detection + 2D tracking Low computational requirements, mature ecosystem Lacks depth information, cannot be directly used for grasping Human-robot interaction, simple obstacle avoidance
3D detection + 3D tracking Complete spatial information, directly interfaces with manipulation planning Relies on depth sensors, high computational overhead Grasping operations, navigation and obstacle avoidance
Monocular 3D reconstruction No depth sensor required, low cost Limited depth accuracy, prone to failure under occlusion Lightweight platforms, data collection
Multi-sensor fusion Strongest robustness, resistant to occlusion and illumination changes Complex calibration, high system cost Full-size humanoid robots

The design logic of P12.1.3 determines its technical selection: to provide perceptual input for human-robot interaction, obstacle avoidance, and manipulation, it typically adopts a combination of 2D/3D detection and multi-object tracking, with human pose estimation introduced as a supplement in key scenarios.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Case 1: Human-Robot Collaborative Assembly (Industrial Scenario) In factories, humanoid robots need to share workspace with workers. Through human detection and pose estimation, the robot perceives the worker's arm position in real time and dynamically adjusts its own motion speed and trajectory to ensure a safe distance. The tracking module maintains the worker's ID, enabling the robot to predict motion intent and avoid collisions in advance.

Case 2: Home Services (Unstructured Scenario) In home environments, robots need to recognize and track user-specified objects (e.g., "help me get the cup on the table"). 3D detection provides the cup's spatial position, and the tracking module continuously locks onto the target while the robot moves, maintaining ID stability even when the cup is partially occluded. Human pose estimation is used to understand the user's pointing gestures, enabling natural human-robot interaction.

Case 3: Imitation Learning Data Collection (R&D Scenario) Referring to the EmbodMocap work, dual RGB-D cameras can simultaneously reconstruct the human body and the scene, providing training data for robot motion control. The human pose estimation module in P12.1.3 outputs high-precision joint trajectories in this scenario, which, combined with sim-to-real reinforcement learning, enables humanoid robots to reproduce human motions from videos.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Physical limits of occlusion: When a target is fully occluded, any tracking algorithm can only rely on motion model extrapolation, with errors accumulating over time. This is a physical boundary of perception that cannot be completely eliminated by algorithms.
  2. Robustness to illumination changes: Severe illumination variations (e.g., backlighting, nighttime) significantly reduce detection accuracy. Multi-sensor fusion is a mitigation measure but increases system complexity and cost.
  3. Trade-off between real-time performance and accuracy: High-accuracy models (e.g., large Transformer-based models) often have high computational demands, making it difficult to meet real-time requirements. Engineering requires a compromise between accuracy and latency, with specific metrics to be confirmed.
  4. Data annotation costs: The annotation cost for 3D detection and pose estimation training data is far higher than for 2D detection, limiting model generalization in specific scenarios.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Accurate detection = stable tracking"—Wrong. Detection accuracy and tracking ID stability are two orthogonal metrics. Even with high single-frame detection mAP, ID switches will still occur frequently if the data association strategy is inadequate. Tracking quality depends on the robustness of the association algorithm, not merely detection precision.
  2. "3D detection is always superior to 2D detection"—Wrong. 3D detection relies on the quality of depth information; when depth sensors fail or calibration drifts, its performance may be inferior to mature 2D solutions. Technical selection should be based on specific task requirements.
  3. "Pose estimation is just an accessory to detection"—Wrong. The joint-level information output by human pose estimation is a key input for imitation learning and intent understanding, with value far beyond "detecting a person." What it truly changes is the robot's cognitive depth from "knowing someone is there" to "understanding what the person is doing."
