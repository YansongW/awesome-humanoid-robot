Overview

BridgeData V2 is a large-scale desktop manipulation dataset annotated with language instructions, collected by UC Berkeley and other institutions. It contains 60,096 trajectories covering 24 environments and 13 skills, and is widely used for imitation learning and cross-embodiment pretraining of Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs). What it truly changes is not the data volume itself, but rather providing a reproducible, comparable public benchmark for "language-conditioned policies."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

BridgeData V2 is a large-scale, diverse robot manipulation behavior dataset designed for scalable robot learning research. It is compatible with open-vocabulary, multi-task learning methods that condition on goal images or natural language instructions. Skills learned from this data can generalize to new objects, new environments, and transfer across institutions.

From a data engineering perspective, it is a key component of cross-embodiment pretraining data. In the data recipe for VLA training, it stands alongside Open X-Embodiment, DROID, and AgiBot World Colosseo, providing models with "manipulation physical intuition"—that is, foundational knowledge about how objects are manipulated, decoupled from specific robot embodiments.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before BridgeData V2, the robot learning community faced a structural dilemma: each lab collected its own data with different environments, camera viewpoints, and object setups, making policies difficult to reproduce and compare across institutions. More critically, early datasets mostly lacked language annotations, so policies could only condition on goal images and could not leverage natural language instructions as a flexible interface.

BridgeData V2's positioning is precisely to fill this gap: it collects large-scale, multi-environment, language-annotated manipulation data using a unified protocol, enabling researchers to train policies conditioned on natural language and verify their generalization capabilities on new objects and environments. It is also one of the "fuels" for the rise of the VLA paradigm—without such large-scale semantically annotated data, cross-embodiment pretraining of models like RT-2 and OpenVLA would be impossible.

Principle Breakdown

① Data Composition: Scale and Diversity

The core design principle of BridgeData V2 is "diversity supports generalization." To support broad generalization, data is collected across many environments for a large number of tasks, with variations introduced in objects, camera viewpoints, and workspace positions. Each trajectory is annotated with a natural language instruction corresponding to the task the robot is performing.

The dataset contains a total of 60,096 trajectories, of which 50,365 are teleoperated demonstrations and 9,731 come from rollouts of scripted pick-and-place policies. The data covers 24 environments and 13 skills.

② Environment and Skill Distribution

The 24 environments fall into 4 major categories. Most data comes from 7 different toy kitchens containing combinations of elements such as sinks, stoves, and microwaves; the remaining environments come from various sources, including various tabletops, standalone toy sinks, toy washing machines, and more.

In terms of skill distribution, most data comes from basic object manipulation tasks such as pick-and-place, pushing, and sweeping; there is also environment interaction data, including opening and closing doors and drawers; the rest comes from more complex tasks such as stacking blocks, folding cloth, and sweeping granular media. Some data segments contain mixtures of these categories.

③ Camera Viewpoint Configuration

The dataset includes multiple camera viewpoints: "Over-the-shoulder" refers to the primary fixed camera, "randomized" refers to two alternate camera viewpoints that data collectors randomized every 50 trajectories, "Depth" (when present) shares the same viewpoint as the primary fixed camera, and "Wrist" refers to a wide-angle wrist-mounted camera. Additional cameras were added in later collection rounds.

④ Relationship to VLA Training

In VLA pretraining, BridgeData V2 serves as one source of real robot demonstration data, forming a mixed data recipe together with human videos, simulation data, and internet images and text. Its language annotations enable models to learn "instruction-action" mappings, which purely visual data cannot provide.

Horizontal Comparison

Compared with similar datasets, BridgeData V2's differentiated positioning is clear:

  • vs. DROID: DROID emphasizes distributed collection across labs and real home environments, known for scene and viewpoint diversity; BridgeData V2 focuses on tabletop manipulation with toy kitchens as the primary environment, but with more systematic language annotations.
  • vs. Open X-Embodiment: The latter is an open dataset aggregated from over 20 institutions, covering 20+ robot embodiments and millions of episodes, serving as a larger public foundation; BridgeData V2 is an important component of it, but smaller in scale and more homogeneous in embodiment.
  • vs. AgiBot World Colosseo: The latter targets full-size humanoid robots, providing scarce high-quality real-robot data; BridgeData V2 focuses on tabletop gripper manipulation and does not involve whole-body control.

Engineering judgment: BridgeData V2's value lies not in being the "largest," but in being the "most standardized"—it provides a reproducible benchmark for imitation learning research through unified protocols and language annotations.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Imitation Learning Research

BridgeData V2 is a common benchmark dataset for imitation learning research. Researchers train policies on it, evaluate language-conditioned manipulation capabilities, and test generalization to new objects and environments.

② VLA Cross-Embodiment Pretraining

In VLA data recipes, BridgeData V2, together with Open X-Embodiment, DROID, and others, forms the pretraining data mixture. Models are first pretrained on the mixed data, then adapted to downstream tasks via LoRA or full fine-tuning.

③ Cross-Institution Generalization Validation

One of the dataset's design goals is "cross-institution generalization"—policies trained on data from one institution should work in another institution's environments and robots. This makes it a standard testbed for validating policy robustness.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Limited Task Scope: The data focuses on tabletop manipulation and does not cover mobile manipulation, whole-body manipulation, or the floating-base issues unique to humanoid robots. For tasks requiring manipulation during locomotion or whole-body coordination, additional data sources are needed.

② Homogeneous Embodiment: The data primarily comes from gripper manipulation on fixed-base or wheeled platforms, which differs from dexterous hand manipulation on humanoid robots. Engineering judgment: policies learned from BridgeData V2 require additional adaptation or fine-tuning when transferred to humanoid platforms.

③ Language Annotation Granularity: Each trajectory is annotated with one natural language instruction, but the semantic granularity of the instructions may be insufficient to cover sub-step decomposition in complex tasks.

④ Data Distribution Bias: Most data comes from toy kitchen environments, with limited coverage of real home or industrial scenarios.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "BridgeData V2 is the largest manipulation dataset"—Incorrect. Open X-Embodiment covers millions of episodes and is far larger; BridgeData V2's value lies in the standardization of its language annotations and the controllability of its environments.

  2. "With it, you can train general-purpose humanoid robot policies"—Incorrect. It does not contain humanoid robot data and does not involve whole-body control; it addresses the problem of language-conditioned policies for tabletop manipulation.

  3. "Language annotations are just auxiliary information"—Incorrect. Language annotations are central to the dataset design: they enable policies to condition on natural language, which is the foundation of open-vocabulary generalization.

  4. "Scripted policy data is low quality and should be discarded"—Incorrect. The 9,731 scripted rollouts provide diversity and scale that teleoperation struggles to cover, making them an important component of the data recipe.
