Overview

The Weighted Task-Error Objective is a form of QP objective function in whole-body control: it penalizes the weighted squared difference between the desired task acceleration and the predicted task acceleration, while adding a regularization term on the generalized acceleration. It reflects the relative importance of tasks through weights \(w_i\), serving as a bridge between task-priority theory and practical numerical solving.

Content

What it is: precise definition

The weighted task-error objective function belongs to the equation type, with the English name Weighted Task-Error Objective and the Korean name 가중 작업 오차 목적 함수. It falls within the AI models and algorithms domain, positioned in the intelligence layer of the value chain.

Its mathematical form can be summarized as follows: given a set of tasks (e.g., center-of-mass balancing, foot trajectory tracking, arm manipulation), each task \(i\) corresponds to a desired task acceleration \(\ddot{x}_i^*\) and a weight \(w_i\). The objective function penalizes the weighted squared difference between the predicted task acceleration and the desired value, while adding a regularization term on the generalized acceleration to improve numerical stability. The weight \(w_i\) reflects the relative importance of the task—the larger the weight, the higher the priority of that task in the optimization.

What it truly changes is not "how tasks are described," but "how multiple tasks can coexist within the same optimization framework"—shifting from rigid priority ordering to soft weighted compromise.

Why it exists: pain points and historical context

In the early development of whole-body control (WBC), multi-task coordination primarily relied on strict hierarchical methods: tasks were sorted by priority, and after higher-priority tasks were fully satisfied, lower-priority tasks could only be solved within their null space. The mathematical foundation of this approach is null-space projection—the matrix \(N = I - J^{\dagger}J\) projects any joint velocity onto the set of directions that do not alter the task quantity. Under strict priority, lower-priority tasks must not degrade the solution of higher-priority tasks.

However, strict hierarchy has two pain points: first, task priorities must be hard-coded in advance and cannot be dynamically adjusted based on real-time states; second, solving a QP or pseudo-inverse computation at each layer incurs computational cost that grows linearly with the number of tasks, limiting real-time performance.

The weighted task-error objective provides an alternative path: approximating strict hierarchy through large weight gaps while allowing all tasks to be optimized simultaneously in a single QP. What it truly changes is not "whether task priorities exist," but "how priorities are expressed"—from discrete hierarchical structures to continuous weight scalars.

Principle breakdown

① Weighted squared difference: a soft measure of task error

Let the Jacobian of task \(i\) be \(J_i\) and the generalized acceleration be \(\ddot{q}\). Then the predicted task acceleration is \(J_i \ddot{q}\), and the desired task acceleration is \(\ddot{x}_i^*\). The core term of the weighted task-error objective is:

\[ \min_{\ddot{q}} \sum_{i=1}^{p} w_i \left\| J_i \ddot{q} - \ddot{x}_i^* \right\|^2 + \lambda \|\ddot{q}\|^2 \]

where \(w_i\) is the weight of task \(i\) and \(\lambda\) is the regularization coefficient. The first term penalizes task tracking error, and the second term is a regularization term used to improve numerical stability or avoid trivial solutions.

② Weights and approximate hierarchy

The weight \(w_i\) reflects the relative importance of tasks. Strict hierarchy can be approximated through extremely large weight gaps, but exact implementation requires layer-by-layer null-space projection. This means: when the weight gap is sufficiently large, the behavior of the weighted method approaches that of strict hierarchy; however, if the weight gap is not large enough, lower-priority tasks will "contaminate" the solution of higher-priority tasks. Engineering judgment: weight ratios typically need to reach the order of \(10^3\)–\(10^6\) to approximate strict hierarchy, and the specific values must be calibrated based on task characteristics.

③ Role of the regularization term

Regularization involves adding a small \(\| \cdot \|\) penalty in the pseudo-inverse/QP to avoid gain explosion near singularities. What it truly changes is not "the optimization objective itself," but "the numerical stability of the solution"—without the regularization term, the solution tends toward infinity when the Jacobian approaches singularity.

Who uses it: application cases

The weighted task-error objective is widely applied in whole-body control of humanoid robots. A typical task stack includes: primary task of balance/center-of-mass (CoM) control, secondary task of foot trajectory tracking, tertiary task of arm manipulation, and lowest priority of posture optimization. In the Inverse-Dynamics QP Formulation, this objective is solved jointly with floating-base dynamics constraints and contact force constraints, outputting generalized acceleration, contact forces, and joint torques.

What it truly changes is not "the structure of the controller," but "the mathematical expression of multi-task coordination"—allowing researchers to flexibly adjust task weights within the same QP framework without rewriting the entire control architecture.

Limitations and boundaries

  1. Difficulty in weight calibration: The selection of weights \(w_i\) lacks a systematic method and typically relies on manual tuning or empirical formulas. Engineering judgment: weight calibration is one of the most time-consuming steps in practical deployment.
  2. Task compromise is unavoidable: The weighted method is inherently a soft constraint, and high-priority tasks cannot be absolutely satisfied. In safety-critical scenarios (e.g., fall prevention), this may be unacceptable.
  3. Sensitivity to model accuracy: The objective function depends on task Jacobians and the dynamics model; model errors directly degrade task tracking performance.
  4. Regularization introduces bias: Although regularization improves numerical stability, it introduces additional optimization bias, requiring a trade-off between stability and accuracy.

Common misconceptions

  1. "The larger the weight, the better"—incorrect. Excessively large weights can lead to numerical ill-conditioning and amplify the impact of model errors. The significance of weights lies in their relative relationships, not absolute magnitudes.
  2. "The weighted method can fully replace strict hierarchy"—inaccurate. The weighted method can only approximate strict hierarchy; exact implementation still requires layer-by-layer null-space projection. The source text explicitly states: "Strict hierarchy can be approximated through extremely large weight gaps, but exact implementation requires layer-by-layer null-space projection."
  3. "The smaller the regularization term, the better"—incorrect. The purpose of the regularization term is to avoid gain explosion near singularities; an excessively small regularization coefficient leads to numerical instability.
