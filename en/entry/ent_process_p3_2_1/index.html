Overview

Computing architecture and node distribution is the first third-level subtask in the electrical and computing architecture design (P3.2) of humanoid robots. Its core work is to map the intelligent functional requirements of the entire machine into specific computing node partitioning, compute budget allocation, and communication topology schemes. What it truly changes is not the number of circuit boards, but the architectural discipline of "where compute should reside, who is responsible for it, and how it is isolated"—completing compute requirement estimation and centralized/distributed comparative论证 before design freeze is a critical engineering milestone to avoid insufficient compute or loss of real-time control in later stages.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Computing architecture and node distribution is a systematic design activity conducted during the system architecture and electromechanical overall design phase, focusing on the physical deployment and logical division of responsibilities for the robot's "brain" and "cerebellum." It answers three fundamental questions: How much compute does the entire machine require? How should this compute be concentrated across several computing nodes? How should each node communicate and be isolated from the others?

Within the WBS V3 framework, this task belongs to the first third-level subtask of the P3.2 Electrical and Computing Architecture work package. Its upstream inputs come from the functional requirements and system-level concepts defined in the P1 requirements phase, while its downstream outputs directly support P3.3 Control Architecture Design and subsequent detailed electrical design. The task follows a unified five-step methodology: input review and target quantification (P3.2.1.1), solution/method design (P3.2.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P3.2.1.3), verification and issue closure (P3.2.1.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P3.2.1.5).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In early robot development, the determination of computing architecture often lagged behind mechanical and electrical design, sometimes even resulting in the inverted situation of "drawing the board first, then thinking about compute." This led to three typical problems:

First, coexistence of compute redundancy and insufficiency. Without a systematic method for estimating compute requirements, designers either stacked high-performance computing boards based on worst-case scenarios, leading to uncontrolled power consumption and heat dissipation, or relied on experience-based estimates, only discovering during system integration that GPU compute was insufficient to support concurrent visual SLAM and motion planning.

Second, interference between real-time and non-real-time tasks. The joint control cycle of humanoid robots is typically 1 kHz or higher, while AI inference tasks (such as visual perception and large language model dialogue) can have latencies of tens of milliseconds. If both run on the same computing node without priority scheduling and isolation mechanisms, jitter in the control loop will directly lead to gait instability.

Third, lack of an independent channel for safety monitoring. When the main computing node fails or experiences a software deadlock, without an independent safety monitoring node (typically implemented on an MCU) to execute emergency stop and joint brake logic, the entire machine loses its last line of defense.

The computing architecture and node distribution process was established precisely to systematically address these issues. It adopts "compute requirement estimation, centralized vs. distributed comparison, and safety partitioning" as core methods, forcing the design team to complete quantitative论证 before design freeze, thereby transforming architectural decisions from "experience-driven" to "data-driven."

Principle Breakdown

① Compute Requirement Estimation: From Functional List to TOPS Budget

The starting point for compute requirement estimation is the functional list of the entire machine. Typical functional modules of a humanoid robot include: visual perception (stereo cameras + depth estimation), LiDAR SLAM, motion planning (whole-body dynamics planning WBC), joint control (FOC and impedance control), voice interaction (ASR/TTS), and large language model inference. The compute requirement for each functional module can be preliminarily estimated using the following formula:

\[ P_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \cdot P_{\text{peak},i} + P_{\text{overhead}} \]

where \( P_{\text{peak},i} \) is the peak compute requirement (TOPS) of the \( i \)-th functional module, \( \alpha_i \) is the concurrency coefficient (accounting for the probability of multiple modules running simultaneously and the proportion of reduced-frequency operation), and \( P_{\text{overhead}} \) is the fixed overhead of the operating system, communication protocol stack, and debugging interfaces. Engineering judgment: for a full-size bipedal humanoid robot, visual perception and motion planning typically account for 60%–70% of the total compute requirement, while joint control, despite having the highest real-time requirements, has relatively limited per-cycle computational demand.

② Centralized vs. Distributed: Trade-offs in Architecture Selection

A centralized architecture concentrates all computing tasks (AI, control, safety monitoring) on one or two high-performance computing nodes. Its advantages are low communication latency, simple software deployment, and relatively controllable cost; its disadvantages are high single-point failure risk, concentrated heat dissipation pressure, and difficulty in fully isolating real-time from non-real-time tasks.

A distributed architecture disperses computing tasks across multiple heterogeneous nodes: GPUs handle AI inference, MCUs/FPGAs handle real-time control, and an independent safety MCU handles monitoring. Its advantages are good isolation, high reliability, and the ability to select each node according to its needs; its disadvantages are additional latency introduced by inter-node communication, increased software integration complexity, and increased overall power consumption and weight.

Engineering judgment: full-size bipedal humanoid robots typically adopt a hybrid architecture of "distributed as the primary approach with local concentration"—that is, AI tasks are separated from real-time control, but multiple joint controllers are centrally managed through a high-speed bus (such as EtherCAT). The core basis for this choice is the safety partitioning principle: safety monitoring must be independent of the AI and main control chain.

③ Safety Partitioning: Functional Safety and Degradation Strategies

Safety partitioning is the most easily overlooked yet most critical principle in computing architecture design. It divides computing nodes into three safety levels: non-safety-critical (AI inference, voice interaction), safety-critical (joint control, state estimation), and functional safety (emergency stop, brake, overcurrent protection). Tasks at different levels run on physically isolated nodes and are connected through independent power and communication links.

The core logic of safety partitioning is: when a higher-level task (such as AI inference) fails, lower-level tasks (such as joint control) must be able to operate in degraded mode (e.g., switching to position mode), while functional safety tasks must remain completely unaffected. This design philosophy directly determines the node distribution scheme—functional safety nodes typically use independent MCUs and do not rely on the main communication bus.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Parameter/Specification Typical Range (Engineering Judgment) Design Basis
AI compute requirement (vision + planning) 50–200 TOPS Determined by concurrency coefficients of visual perception and motion planning
Real-time control cycle 1 kHz–4 kHz Determined by joint control bandwidth and mechanical resonance frequency
Control node communication latency < 1 ms Typical value for real-time buses such as EtherCAT at the scale of hundreds of nodes
Safety monitoring response time < 10 ms Full-chain time from fault detection to emergency stop activation
Computing node power budget 20%–30% of total power Constrained by battery capacity and heat dissipation capability of the entire machine
Inter-node communication interface EtherCAT / CAN FD / PCIe Selected by level based on real-time and bandwidth requirements

The values in the table above are typical ranges given by engineering judgment; specific values must be confirmed based on the machine-level specification and upstream requirement documents. Key constraints include power consumption, heat dissipation, weight, and real-time performance—these four constraints are coupled. For example, increasing compute inevitably increases power consumption and heat dissipation burden, while a distributed architecture improves heat dissipation but increases weight.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Within the P3.2 Electrical and Computing Architecture work package, computing architecture and node distribution is the first third-level subtask to be initiated, and its output directly determines the input conditions for subsequent electrical schematic design, wiring harness design, and control architecture design. Specific application scenarios include:

  • P3.2.1.1 Input Review and Target Quantification: The design team first lists all upstream input items and confirms their versions, including functional requirement documents from the P1 phase, machine-level power budgets, and spatial constraints on computing nodes imposed by the mechanical structure. Acceptance criteria are then converted into quantifiable KPIs, such as "AI inference latency < 50 ms," "control cycle jitter < 10 μs," and "safety monitoring response < 10 ms." Finally, task owners, timelines, and risk registers are established.

  • P3.2.1.2 Solution/Method Design: Based on the input review results, no fewer than 2 candidate solutions are formed (e.g., "single GPU + single MCU" vs. "dual GPU + multiple MCUs + independent safety MCU"), an evaluation matrix is established with quantitative scoring, and evaluation dimensions include real-time performance, reliability, power consumption, cost, and development cycle. Reviews are organized and the solution is frozen.

  • P3.2.1.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication: Models/prototypes are established and key parameters are recorded, such as verifying EtherCAT bus communication latency in a simulation environment or measuring GPU power curves under different loads on actual hardware. Simulations or prototype verifications are executed, and anomalies and deviations are recorded.

  • P3.2.1.4 Verification and Issue Closure: Test/review plans and pass criteria are formulated, tests are executed with raw data recorded, and issue lists and improvement measures are output. Typical verification items include: heat dissipation testing under full compute load, bit error rate testing of the communication bus under electromagnetic interference, and fault injection testing of the safety monitoring node.

  • P3.2.1.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Documents are written according to templates with references to raw data, internal reviews and version control are completed, and publication and notification of downstream dependent parties are carried out. Deliverables include the computing architecture diagram, compute/power budgets for each node, and communication interface definitions.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary of the computing architecture and node distribution process is that it is only responsible for the "macro-level layout of compute and nodes," and does not involve specific circuit schematic design (belonging to subsequent subtasks of P3.2) or low-level driver development (belonging to P3.3 Control Architecture Design). Its limitations include:

  • Limited precision of compute estimation: The TOPS metric cannot fully reflect the actual operating efficiency of algorithms on specific hardware, and final calibration still requires prototype verification.
  • Physical limits of communication latency: The inter-node communication latency introduced by a distributed architecture cannot be completely eliminated. For tasks requiring high-frequency data exchange across nodes (such as the coupling between whole-body dynamics planning and joint control), node boundaries must be carefully defined.
  • Hard constraints of heat dissipation and weight: The selection of computing nodes must strike a balance among power consumption, heat dissipation, and weight, and this constraint may force the design team to reduce compute redundancy.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The more compute, the better"—Wrong. Increased compute brings simultaneous increases in power consumption, heat dissipation, and cost, and the portion exceeding requirements is almost never utilized during machine operation. The correct approach is to perform requirement estimation based on the functional list and retain only 20%–30% redundancy.

  2. "Centralized architecture is simpler"—Wrong. A centralized architecture is indeed simpler in terms of software deployment, but the difficulty of isolating real-time from non-real-time tasks is extremely high, and the single-point failure risk is unacceptable for legged robots. Engineering judgment: the increase in software complexity from a distributed approach is far smaller than the reliability risk introduced by a centralized approach.

  3. "Safety monitoring can reuse the main controller"—Wrong. Safety monitoring must be physically independent of the main computing chain; otherwise, when the main controller fails, safety functions will fail simultaneously. This is a fundamental requirement of functional safety standards and the core of the safety partitioning principle.

  4. "The fewer nodes, the better"—Wrong. The number of nodes depends on functional isolation requirements, not on minimizing the count. Over-concentration leads to heat dissipation and reliability issues, while over-dispersion increases communication latency and cost. The correct approach is to "partition by safety level and group by real-time requirements."
