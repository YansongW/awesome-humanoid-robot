Overview

ISO/TS 15066 is a technical specification published by the International Organization for Standardization, providing the first quantitative framework for safety requirements of collaborative robot systems in human-robot collaborative operations, particularly defining force and pressure limits for various body parts during contact. It fills the gap in industrial robot safety standards (such as ISO 10218) for "human-robot shared space" scenarios, shifting safety assessment from "avoiding contact" to "controlling the consequences of contact."

Content

What It Is: A Safety Paradigm Shift from "Isolation" to "Coexistence"

ISO/TS 15066 (full title Collaborative robots — Safety requirements) is a Technical Specification published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) in 2016, designated as ISO/TS 15066:2016. It is not an independent standard but a supplement to ISO 10218-1 and ISO 10218-2 (safety requirements for industrial robots), specifically providing detailed safety requirements for collaborative robots (cobots)—robots designed to interact directly with operators within a shared workspace.

What it truly changes is not the technical question of "how to make robots safer," but rather the very definition of "safety" itself: from the absolute safety of traditional industrial robots where "humans and robots must be physically isolated," to a relative safety where "contact is permitted, provided that the risk of injury is controlled within an acceptable range." To this end, ISO/TS 15066 is the first to systematically provide force and pressure limits for different body parts under quasi-static contact and transient contact with robots, offering designers quantifiable and verifiable engineering evidence.

Why It Exists: The "Blind Spot" in Industrial Robot Safety Standards

The safety strategy for traditional industrial robots (e.g., welding, painting robots) is isolation: separating humans and robots completely through fences, light curtains, safety doors, etc. The ISO 10218 series is based on this logic. However, the emergence of collaborative robots breaks this premise—they are designed to work in the same space as humans, even allowing intentional contact (e.g., hand-guiding, collaborative handling).

This creates a fundamental contradiction: if the "isolation" logic is maintained, collaborative robots lose their purpose; if contact is fully permitted, there is no safety criterion to rely on. ISO/TS 15066 exists precisely to resolve this contradiction: it defines four collaborative operation modes for collaborative robots (safety-rated monitored stop, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, and power and force limiting), and provides biomechanical tolerance limits for human body parts for the most critical mode—"power and force limiting." Without these limits, designers cannot prove their products are "safe enough," and regulators cannot conduct conformity assessments.

Principle Breakdown: How Biomechanical Limits Translate into Engineering Parameters

The core technical contribution of ISO/TS 15066 lies in transforming safety requirements from qualitative descriptions into quantitative limits. Its foundational principle is the tolerance of human tissue to mechanical loading, implemented through the following two levels:

① Dual Limits for Force and Pressure

The standard specifies that in both quasi-static contact (prolonged contact duration, e.g., being clamped) and transient contact (brief impact, e.g., collision), the force (in Newtons, N) and pressure (in Pascals, Pa) exerted by the robot on specific body parts must not exceed given values. These limits are based on research into human pain perception thresholds and tissue damage thresholds, accounting for sensitivity differences across body parts (e.g., head, neck, fingers, torso).

② Mapping from Limits to Design Parameters

Designers must translate the above biomechanical limits into design parameters for the robot system. This involves a key formula—the impulse-momentum theorem:

\[ F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{m \, \Delta v}{\Delta t} \]

where \(F_{\text{avg}}\) is the average impact force, \(m\) is the effective mass (sum of the robot's moving part mass and load mass), \(\Delta v\) is the velocity change during collision, and \(\Delta t\) is the contact duration. This formula shows that collision force is proportional to mass and velocity, and inversely proportional to contact time. Therefore, reducing robot mass and velocity, and increasing contact compliance (soft covers, elastic joints), are core principles of inherently safe design.

Key Difference: ISO/TS 15066 focuses on contact consequences (force and pressure), while ISO 13849/IEC 61508 focus on control system reliability (failure probability). The former answers "is contact safe," while the latter answers "is the safety function reliable." Both are indispensable in collaborative robot design.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

ISO/TS 15066 has become the benchmark document for safety design and verification among collaborative robot manufacturers, integrators, and end users. Typical application scenarios include:

  • Hand-guiding: Operators directly guide the robot's end effector movement; the robot must detect and limit contact forces in real time.
  • Collaborative handling: Humans and robots jointly handle large or heavy workpieces; the robot must actively reduce speed and torque upon contact.
  • Assembly and machine tending: In industries such as electronics and automotive components, collaborative robots work in close proximity to operators without fences, and their safety design must be verified against 15066 limits.
  • Humanoid robot R&D: For humanoid robots targeting home or public service scenarios, the design of arms, torso, and other parts must reference 15066 limits to support safe human-robot interaction.

Engineering Judgment: In practical applications, merely meeting the force/pressure limits of 15066 is insufficient to guarantee overall safety. Designers must also incorporate functional safety requirements from ISO 13849 (e.g., reliability of safety-rated monitored stop, speed monitoring) and conduct comprehensive risk assessments.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Technical specification, not an international standard: ISO/TS 15066 is a "Technical Specification" rather than a formal "International Standard." This means its guidance has lower maturity and consensus than formal standards and may be revised or upgraded in the future.
  2. Limits based on specific assumptions: The force/pressure limits in the appendix are derived from specific population statistics and contact conditions (e.g., contact area, skin/tissue properties). For non-typical populations (e.g., children) or special contact scenarios (e.g., sharp objects), applicability must be verified independently.
  3. Does not cover all hazards: 15066 primarily addresses mechanical contact injuries. Other hazards, such as noise, radiation, hot surfaces, and chemical substances, still require compliance with other relevant standards.
  4. Complexity of dynamic contact: The standard distinguishes between quasi-static and transient contact, but actual collision processes may be more complex (e.g., involving multi-joint linkage, nonlinear elastic deformation). The applicability of its limits under extreme conditions requires careful evaluation.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Meeting 15066 limits equals absolute safety"—Wrong. Limits are statistically based tolerance thresholds; individual differences, contact angles, surface shapes, and other factors can affect actual injury severity. Safety margins and risk assessments are essential.
  2. "15066 applies to all robots"—Wrong. It applies only to collaborative robots and their collaborative application scenarios. For non-collaborative industrial robots, the isolation principles of ISO 10218 still apply.
  3. "Force limiting is the only collaborative safety mode"—Wrong. ISO/TS 15066 defines four collaborative modes; power and force limiting is just one of them. Designers should select the most appropriate mode based on the application scenario, rather than always adopting force limiting.
  4. "Pressure limits are more important than force limits"—Wrong. Both are equally important. Pressure (force divided by contact area) determines local tissue damage risk, while force determines overall pushing or pinching risk. Both must be satisfied simultaneously in design.
