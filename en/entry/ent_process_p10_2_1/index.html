Overview

Standing and Anti-Interference Balance Control is a critical process node in the development workflow of humanoid robot motion control algorithms, serving as the first subtask of the P10.2 Balance, Gait, and WBC work package. It addresses the core safety functionality of maintaining balance when a bipedal robot is subjected to external push disturbances while standing still. Its methodological toolkit encompasses LQR, MPC, ZMP/Capture Point, and angular momentum control, with key constraints requiring real-time solving frequencies of no less than 100 Hz and compliance with joint limits. This process translates abstract control objectives into quantifiable, verifiable engineering deliverables through five third-level subtasks (input analysis, solution design, simulation validation, parameter tuning validation, and documentation delivery).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Standing and Anti-Interference Balance Control is the first process node in the humanoid robot motion control algorithm development workflow (P10.2), numbered P10.2.1. It refers to the ability, while the robot is in a bipedal standing state, to maintain the center of mass (CoM) stable within the support polygon through control algorithms, and to actively recover balance after being subjected to external pushes, impacts, or self-induced disturbances. This process is not the implementation of a single algorithm but a complete development loop: starting from input analysis and objective quantification, proceeding through solution design, simulation validation, and parameter tuning validation, and finally delivering outputs downstream in documentation form.

What it truly changes is not the outcome of "making the robot stand," but the engineering process of "systematically guaranteeing that the robot can stand"—transforming balance control from empirical parameter tuning into a standardized development workflow with quantitative metrics, review checkpoints, and traceability chains.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Bipedal standing balance is the most fundamental and demanding safety function of humanoid robots. Unlike wheeled robots, the support surface of a bipedal robot is discrete and finite; once the CoM exceeds the boundary of the support polygon, falling becomes inevitable. Early balance control relied on manually tuned PID parameters, which exhibited poor robustness across different payloads and ground conditions and could not explicitly handle hard constraints such as joint limits and torque saturation.

The historical positioning of this process lies in its role as the hub connecting upstream perception and dynamics modeling with downstream gait planning and whole-body control (WBC). Without reliable standing anti-interference capability, gait planning and WBC would lack a safe foundation. Therefore, P10.2.1 is designed as the first subtask of the P10.2 work package, and its completion quality directly determines the feasibility of subsequent gait and WBC processes.

Principle Breakdown

① Physical Foundation of Balance Control: The ZMP Criterion

The core criterion for standing balance is that the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) must lie within the support polygon. In a simplified model, assuming a constant CoM height \(z_c\), the ZMP coordinates are:

\[ x_{\text{ZMP}} = x_{\text{CoM}} - \frac{z_c}{g} \ddot{x}_{\text{CoM}} \]
\[ y_{\text{ZMP}} = y_{\text{CoM}} - \frac{z_c}{g} \ddot{y}_{\text{CoM}} \]

where \(g\) is the gravitational acceleration, and \(\ddot{x}_{\text{CoM}}\), \(\ddot{y}_{\text{CoM}}\) are the horizontal accelerations of the CoM. This equation indicates that the distance of the ZMP from the CoM projection point is proportional to the CoM acceleration—the essence of balance control is controlling CoM acceleration so that the ZMP remains within the support polygon.

② Capture Point: Geometrizing the Dynamic Problem

The Capture Point (CP) is a point on the ground such that if the robot could immediately step onto it and position the CoM directly above the support foot, it could stop without taking another step. In the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM), assuming a constant CoM height \(z_c\) and a point mass connected to the ground ZMP via a massless rod, the horizontal dynamics of the LIPM are:

\[ \ddot{x} = \frac{g}{z_c}(x - x_{\text{ZMP}}) \]

The existence of the Capture Point transforms the complex dynamic balance problem into a geometric question of "where to step next." In the standing anti-interference scenario, if the disturbance is small, the robot can pull the Capture Point back within the support polygon using ankle joint torques; if the disturbance is large, stepping becomes necessary—this is precisely the interface between standing anti-interference and gait planning.

③ Control Framework: Division of Labor Between LQR and MPC

LQR is optimal over an infinite horizon but cannot explicitly handle constraints—yet the core constraints of humanoid robots are precisely hard constraints: joint limits cannot be exceeded, torques are bounded, and the ZMP must remain within the support polygon. MPC, by contrast, employs a receding horizon mechanism, solving a finite-horizon open-loop optimal control problem at each control cycle but executing only the first control action, then re-solving with updated state estimates in the next cycle. The prediction model of MPC is "re-anchored" to the current state at each solve—this is the fundamental difference between MPC and offline trajectory optimization.

In the standing anti-interference scenario, MPC can uniformly handle constraints: incorporating ZMP support polygon constraints, joint torque limits, and joint position limits into the optimization problem while achieving foresight through receding-horizon optimization within the prediction horizon. LQR is commonly used for local linearized control under small disturbances or as an infinite-horizon approximation in the terminal cost function of MPC.

A real-time solving frequency of ≥ 100 Hz implies a control cycle of no more than 10 ms. For MPC, this requires solving a finite-horizon optimal control problem within 10 ms—imposing extremely high demands on the computational efficiency of the optimization solver, which is the fundamental reason why real-time performance must be evaluated during solution selection (engineering judgment).

Horizontal Comparison

Method Constraint Handling Capability Real-Time Performance Applicable Scenarios
LQR Weak (requires post-hoc saturation) Extremely fast (analytical solution) Small disturbances, linearized models
MPC Strong (explicit hard constraints) Slower (requires online solving) Large disturbances, constraint-dominated scenarios
ZMP/Capture Point Medium (geometric criterion) Fast (analytical computation) Balance criteria, foothold planning
Angular momentum control Medium (supplements CoM model) Fast Whole-body posture coordination

LQR and MPC are not mutually exclusive—in engineering practice, a common approach is to use MPC for constraint handling and foresight while employing LQR approximations within MPC or at its terminal (engineering judgment). Capture Point provides an intuitive geometric criterion that can serve as a reference trajectory generator or safety monitoring layer for MPC.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

This process is a component of the P10.2 Balance, Gait, and WBC work package in the full humanoid robot product development workflow (V3 version), applicable to any full-size humanoid robot product development project requiring bipedal standing and anti-interference capability. Its application scenarios include:

  • Industrial inspection: Robots standing on uneven ground, resisting disturbances from wind or cable pulling;
  • Logistics and material handling: Robots standing while holding heavy objects with both hands, maintaining balance when the CoM shifts;
  • Human-robot interaction: Robots remaining upright when pushed or patted by humans, a prerequisite for safe interaction.

The completion criteria of this process—physical standing anti-interference video and recovery capability meeting metrics—serve directly as product acceptance evidence, and its output documents update the ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chain, providing inputs for downstream gait planning and WBC processes.

Limitations and Boundaries

This process focuses on anti-interference balance in the standing state, with boundary conditions including:

  • Does not cover dynamic walking: Balance during walking is handled by subsequent gait planning processes; this process addresses only standing scenarios;
  • Does not cover step recovery from large disturbances: When the Capture Point exceeds the support polygon and cannot be pulled back via ankle torques, a stepping strategy is required—this falls at the interface between standing anti-interference and gait planning; the boundary of this process is "recovery capability meeting metrics," with specific metric values to be quantified in P10.2.1.1;
  • Model simplification assumptions: The derivations of ZMP and Capture Point are based on the LIPM assumption (constant CoM height); actual robot CoM height fluctuates and requires compensation via angular momentum control;
  • Real-time performance bottleneck: Online solving of MPC at frequencies ≥ 100 Hz imposes hard computational requirements; if hardware cannot meet them, model reduction or explicit MPC is needed (engineering judgment).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Standing balance is just a PID matter"—Wrong. PID cannot explicitly handle joint limits and ZMP support polygon constraints and lacks foresight under large disturbances. This process explicitly requires optimization-based control frameworks like MPC precisely to handle constraints uniformly.

  2. "Capture Point is only for walking"—Wrong. Capture Point is equally critical in standing anti-interference: it provides the criterion for "whether stepping is needed." If the Capture Point lies within the support polygon, ankle strategy suffices for recovery; if outside the boundary, stepping is mandatory.

  3. "Passing simulation validation means it's ready for the physical robot"—Wrong. P10.2.1.4 explicitly requires "executing tests and recording raw data" and "outputting issue lists and improvement measures," and the completion criterion is "physical standing anti-interference video"—discrepancies in model errors, latency, and noise exist between simulation and reality, necessitating physical parameter tuning validation.

  4. "Higher real-time frequency is always better"—Wrong. ≥ 100 Hz is a lower bound, not an upper limit. Excessively high frequencies may lead to insufficient MPC solving time and control cycle jitter; excessively low frequencies fail to capture disturbance dynamics. The appropriate frequency must be determined based on robot dynamic characteristics and hardware computational power (engineering judgment).
