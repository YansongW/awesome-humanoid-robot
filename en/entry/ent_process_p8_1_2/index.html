Overview

FEA model preparation is a critical process node in the structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA) phase, responsible for transforming CAD geometric models into high-quality analysis models suitable for finite element solving. What it truly changes is not just the single point of "how fine the mesh is drawn," but rather establishing a traceable, quantifiable engineering closed loop among "design intent—simulation assumptions—process constraints." This process encompasses five level-3 subtasks: input review, solution design, implementation and production, verification closed loop, and documentation delivery. The quality of its output directly determines the reliability of subsequent solution results and the effectiveness of engineering decisions.

Content

What it is: Engineering transformation from "geometric model" to "solvable model"

FEA model preparation is a pivotal process in the finite element analysis workflow, serving as the link between upstream and downstream. Upstream, it receives CAD geometric models and load case definitions from the structural design phase; downstream, it delivers to the solver for computation. Its core task is not "mesh generation" per se, but rather completing a series of engineering decisions: which features can be simplified, which regions must be refined, how material properties are assigned, and how contacts and boundary conditions are defined.

In WBS V3, this process is expanded under code P8.1.2 and includes five level-3 subtasks: input review and objective quantification (P8.1.2.1), solution/method design (P8.1.2.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P8.1.2.3), verification and issue closure (P8.1.2.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P8.1.2.5). Each subtask further contains several level-4 key actions, forming a complete engineering execution chain.

The methods/tools for this process include mainstream pre-processing and solving platforms such as HyperMesh / ANSA / Abaqus / ANSYS, as well as the core methodology of mesh convergence analysis. The design thinking logic revolves around "simplifying non-load-bearing features, rational mesh division, material properties, contact/bonding settings," while key constraints focus on the balance among mesh quality, computation time, and resource consumption.

Why it exists: Pain points and historical positioning

In finite element analysis practice, the "model preparation" phase has long been underestimated, yet it is precisely a major source of error. In early development, engineers often meshed complete CAD models directly, resulting in an explosive growth in mesh count due to small fillets, small holes, threads, and other detailed features. Computation time stretched to days, while solution accuracy did not improve significantly. More seriously, unsimplified models often produced spurious stress concentrations at detailed features, misleading design decisions.

In the humanoid robotics field, this issue is particularly pronounced. Robot structural components contain numerous topologically optimized complex surfaces, thin-walled stiffening ribs, and lightweight hollowed-out features, making direct meshing almost impossible to achieve a usable computational scale. FEA model preparation thus becomes the essential bridge connecting "design geometry" and "computable model"—what it truly changes is not just the single point of "whether the calculation is accurate," but rather establishing a traceable, iterative closed-loop relationship among "design—simulation—process."

The historical positioning of this process lies in the fact that it is the first execution-oriented process in the P8 structural strength simulation and iteration phase, and its output directly determines the efficiency and credibility of subsequent solving, post-processing, and design iteration. Unlike P8.1.1 task definition, P8.1.2 answers the question "how does this structure become a computable model"—and this question must be answered clearly before the solver starts; otherwise, all calculations will be built on an unreliable foundation.

Principle breakdown

① Geometric simplification: The first trade-off from "complete" to "computable"

The first step in FEA model preparation is geometric cleanup and simplification. The core principle is "simplify non-load-bearing features": small fillets, small holes, threads, chamfers, and other features should be removed or filled if they are not in the region of interest. The engineering basis for this step lies in Saint-Venant's Principle, which states that local details have a negligible effect on stress distribution far from that region. Therefore, simplifying detailed features outside the region of interest does not significantly affect overall solution accuracy, yet it can substantially reduce mesh size.

② Mesh generation: The quality of discretization determines solution accuracy

Mesh generation is the process of discretizing continuous geometry into finite elements. Mesh quality directly determines solution accuracy and convergence. Key parameters include element type (tetrahedral/hexahedral), element size, and mesh density gradient. Mesh convergence analysis is the standard method for verifying mesh independence: progressively refine the mesh and observe whether the stress/displacement response at key locations tends toward a stable value. When the change in results between two successive refinements is less than the engineering acceptable threshold (typically within 5%, engineering judgment), the mesh is considered converged.

\[ \sigma_{\text{conv}} = \lim_{h \to 0} \sigma_h \quad \text{satisfying} \quad \left| \frac{\sigma_{h_2} - \sigma_{h_1}}{\sigma_{h_1}} \right| < \varepsilon \]

where \(h\) is the characteristic mesh size and \(\varepsilon\) is the convergence criterion (engineering judgment). This formula embodies the essence of mesh convergence analysis: the solution result should approach a mesh-independent exact solution.

③ Material properties and boundary conditions: Carriers of physical realism

Material property assignment includes elastic modulus, Poisson's ratio, density, yield strength, etc., and must match the material state achievable by subsequent processes. Boundary condition definition includes constraint types (fixed/simply supported/symmetric), load application (force/pressure/displacement/temperature), and contact definition (bonded/frictional contact). The key in this step is that boundary conditions must reflect real operating conditions rather than idealized assumptions made "for convenience of solving." For example, bolted connection regions should be simulated using bonded or beam elements rather than simple fixed constraints.

④ Contact/bonding settings: Mechanical transfer in multi-part assemblies

Humanoid robot structural components are mostly multi-part assemblies, and the connection methods between parts (welding, bolting, adhesive bonding) need to be correctly represented in the model. Bonded contact is suitable for welded or adhesive-bonded regions, while frictional contact is applicable to interfaces that can slide relative to each other. The rationality of contact settings directly affects the accuracy of load transfer paths and is one of the technically most challenging aspects of model preparation.

Who uses it · Application cases

Application scenarios of FEA model preparation in robot structural development include:

  1. Structural lightweight verification: Topologically optimized structural components need to be remodeled and verified through FEA. In the model preparation phase, the implicit geometry output from optimization must be converted into an analyzable solid model, with reasonable simplification of non-load-bearing features.
  2. Joint housing strength verification: Harmonic reducer housings withstand complex alternating loads. Model preparation requires precise mesh refinement in key regions such as bearing seats and mounting flanges, while simplifying non-load-bearing features such as cooling fins.
  3. Foot structure impact analysis: Foot-ground impact analysis requires refined contact definitions and material constitutive models. In the model preparation phase, contact pairs between the foot sole foam and structural components must be established, and contact stability must be verified.

Limitations and boundaries

The limitations of FEA model preparation are mainly reflected in:

  1. Accuracy loss due to simplification: Feature simplification inevitably introduces errors. For detailed features near the region of interest, simplification may lead to underestimation of stress concentrations. Engineering judgment: simplification should follow the principle of "away from the region of interest," and features within the region of interest must be fully preserved.
  2. Conflict between mesh quality and computational resources: High-precision meshes require more computational resources and time. Under tight project schedules, mesh density often has to be compromised, which may affect the adequacy of convergence verification.
  3. Dependence on accuracy of material data: The upper limit of model preparation accuracy is constrained by the accuracy of material constitutive data. For new materials or special process states (such as additively manufactured parts), missing material data may lead to models that are "precise but wrong."
  4. Idealization of boundary conditions: Loads and constraints in actual operating conditions often involve uncertainty, and idealized assumptions in the model preparation phase may overlook certain critical load paths.

Common misconceptions

  1. "The finer the mesh, the more accurate"—Wrong. Beyond a certain level of mesh refinement, accuracy improvement saturates while computational cost continues to rise. The correct approach is to perform mesh convergence analysis and find the balance point between accuracy and cost.
  2. "Simplification means deleting features"—Wrong. Simplification must be based on mechanical judgment: features on load-bearing paths must be preserved, and only non-load-bearing features can be simplified. Blind deletion may distort load transfer paths.
  3. "The more complex the contact settings, the better"—Wrong. Overly complex contact definitions increase convergence difficulty and computation time without necessarily improving accuracy. The simplest physically reasonable contact type should be selected based on the actual connection method.
  4. "Model preparation is a one-time task"—Wrong. After design iterations, the model needs to be updated accordingly. Model preparation should establish a reusable parametric workflow rather than starting from scratch each time.
