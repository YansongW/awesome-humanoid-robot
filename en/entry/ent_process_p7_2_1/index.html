Overview

Basic standing and walking simulation is the first subtask in the controller baseline and scenario validation phase (P7.2) of the humanoid robot development process. Its goal is to first achieve stable standing and in-place stepping in a virtual physical environment, then gradually expand to forward walking, turning, and slope walking, with the completion criteria being simulation videos, stable walking of 10 m, and successful turning/slope traversal. What it truly changes is not the superficial form of "moving a real robot into a computer," but rather transforming the high-risk problem of bipedal dynamic balance from serial trial-and-error on physical hardware to a parallel validation paradigm of simulation-first research and metric-driven development, enabling controllers to obtain a quantifiable stability baseline before physical deployment.

What it is: Precise Definition

Basic standing and walking simulation belongs to the method category. It is the first level-3 subtask (P7.2.1) under the P7.2 controller baseline and scenario validation work package, spanning the domains of software middleware and evaluation benchmarks, with value chain coverage at the intelligence layer and validation market layer. This process node builds upon the URDF/SDF models output from the P6 phase, constructing a bipedal robot dynamics model in the simulation platform. Through the combination of three method categories—LQR/MPC balance control, CPG/ZMP gait generation, and PD position control—it achieves progressive validation from static standing to dynamic walking.

From an engineering process perspective, P7.2.1 is decomposed into five level-4 subtasks in WBS V3: P7.2.1.1 Input Review and Target Quantification, P7.2.1.2 Solution/Method Design, P7.2.1.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication, P7.2.1.4 Validation and Issue Closure, and P7.2.1.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery. Each level-4 subtask follows a unified key action structure internally, forming a complete closed loop from input confirmation to output delivery.

Why it exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early humanoid robot development practices, validation of bipedal walking controllers relied heavily on physical prototypes. Every fall meant hardware damage risk, extended debugging cycles, and increased safety costs—a single fall of a full-size bipedal robot could cause joint reducer damage or shell rupture, with repair costs reaching tens of thousands of yuan. This serial model of "hardware first, remediation afterward" made the iteration cycle for walking controllers span weeks or even months.

The fundamental reason for the existence of basic standing and walking simulation lies in the high-dimensional, strongly nonlinear, hard-constrained nature of bipedal dynamic balance: high-dimensionality manifests in the need for coordinated control of dozens of degrees of freedom across the body; strong nonlinearity manifests in the high coupling of ground contact, friction cones, and center-of-mass dynamics; hard constraints manifest in joint limits, torque saturation, and the inviolable ZMP support polygon. Debugging these issues on physical hardware is extremely costly, while in simulation they can be exposed and fixed at low cost and with repeatability.

What it truly changes is not the superficial form of "replacing hardware testing with software," but rather transforming walking controller development from the physical trial-and-error loop of "fall-repair-fall again" to a parallel validation paradigm of "simulation-first research, metric-driven development, and virtual-physical linkage." By establishing a stable standing and walking baseline through P7.2.1, subsequent P7.2.2 manipulation and grasping simulation and P7.2.3 sim-to-real transfer evaluation can obtain a credible algorithmic starting point.

Principle Breakdown

① Standing Balance: Linearization Anchor for LQR/MPC

The core of standing balance is maintaining the center of mass (CoM) projection within the support polygon. In the simplified inverted pendulum model, assuming constant CoM height \(z_c\), the relationship between ZMP coordinates and CoM motion is (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.4):

\[ x_{\text{ZMP}} = x_{\text{CoM}} - \frac{z_c}{g} \ddot{x}_{\text{CoM}} \]

LQR linearizes the dynamics around the equilibrium point and solves for infinite-horizon optimal state feedback gains; MPC solves a finite-horizon constrained optimization problem in a receding horizon manner, explicitly handling joint limits and ZMP support polygon constraints. In the standing phase, LQR typically serves as the baseline, with MPC as the constrained enhancement.

② Gait Generation: Separation of Rhythm and Stability in CPG/ZMP

Walking gait generation employs two technical routes: CPG (Central Pattern Generator) produces rhythmic joint trajectories through coupled oscillators, suitable for rapid prototyping; ZMP-based gait planning optimizes foot trajectories and CoM trajectories offline based on centroidal dynamics, ensuring the ZMP remains within the support polygon at every step. These two routes form no fewer than 2 candidate solutions during the P7.2.1.2 solution design phase, with the technical route frozen after quantitative scoring via an evaluation matrix.

③ Joint Execution: Foundational Guarantee of PD Position Control

Regardless of whether the upper layer uses LQR, MPC, or CPG/ZMP, the desired trajectories must ultimately be mapped to torque commands through joint-space PD position control. The stiffness and damping parameters of PD control directly affect walking compliance and disturbance rejection capability, making them key parameters requiring focused calibration in simulation.

Who uses it · Application Cases

In the P7 simulation platform construction and validation phase, the executing entities for P7.2.1 are motion control algorithm engineers and simulation engineers. The typical application workflow is:

  1. P7.2.1.1 Input Review: Confirm the upstream URDF/SDF model version, translate "stable walking 10 m" into quantifiable KPIs (e.g., ZMP margin, CoM offset standard deviation, joint torque peaks), and establish task ownership and milestones.
  2. P7.2.1.2 Solution Design: Form two candidate solutions—LQR balance + CPG gait and MPC balance + ZMP gait—build an evaluation matrix for quantitative scoring, organize reviews, and freeze the solution.
  3. P7.2.1.3 Implementation: Build the bipedal model in the simulation platform, record key parameters (mass distribution, joint damping, contact stiffness), execute standing and walking simulations, and log anomalies and deviations.
  4. P7.2.1.4 Validation: Develop a test plan and pass criteria, execute the 10 m walking test with raw data recording, and output an issue list with improvement measures.
  5. P7.2.1.5 Delivery: Output the simulation report, update the ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chain, and publish with notification to downstream dependent parties.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Simulation-to-physical gap: Contact models and friction parameters in simulation deviate from real hardware; controllers validated in P7.2.1 still require retuning on physical hardware—this is precisely the purpose of P7.2.3 sim-to-real transfer evaluation.
  2. Computational power and simulation speed constraints: High-fidelity physics simulation (e.g., with flexible contact models) incurs significant computational overhead; insufficient real-time performance limits the receding horizon frequency of MPC, requiring a trade-off between fidelity and speed (Corpus: Key constraints).
  3. Model accuracy depends on upstream: The credibility of P7.2.1 simulation results directly depends on the accuracy of URDF/SDF models output from the P6 phase; inaccurate mass distribution or joint dynamics parameters may mislead subsequent development.
  4. Limited completion criteria: Stable walking of 10 m and turning/slope traversal are foundational thresholds and do not cover complex terrain, dynamic disturbances, or high-speed walking scenarios.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "If it passes simulation, the physical robot can walk"—Wrong. Model errors, actuator latency, and sensor noise exist between simulation and physical hardware. The value of P7.2.1 lies in exposing algorithmic-level defects, not replacing physical validation.
  2. "Choose either LQR or MPC"—Wrong. In engineering practice, LQR serves as a fast baseline and MPC as a constrained enhancement; they are progressive rather than mutually exclusive.
  3. "CPG and ZMP are the same type of method"—Wrong. CPG is a rhythm generator focused on gait timing; ZMP is a stability criterion focused on the relationship between CoM and the support polygon. They address different levels of the problem.
  4. "The simulation video is the deliverable"—Wrong. The simulation video is merely a presentation format. The true deliverables are validated controller parameters, issue lists with improvement measures, and the updated requirements traceability chain.
