Overview

A Hollow Cup Motor is a type of miniature DC servo motor featuring a coreless rotor structure. Its rotor winding is formed into a self-supporting cup shape, eliminating the torque ripple and iron losses caused by the iron core's teeth and slots in traditional motors, thereby achieving extremely low moment of inertia and exceptionally high response speed. What it truly changes is not the motor's power density itself, but the physical limits of "control precision and dynamic response" in miniature precision drive scenarios—enabling near-zero-lag torque output within extremely small spaces for devices such as dexterous robotic hands, medical pumps and valves, and drone gimbals.

Content

What it is: Precise Definition

A Hollow Cup Motor (Coreless Motor / Hollow Cup Motor) belongs to the component category and is a special branch of miniature DC servo motors. Its core characteristic lies in the rotor having no silicon steel core; the winding is wound into a cup-shaped structure in a self-supporting manner, placed directly within the magnetic field generated by permanent magnets. By eliminating the iron core, rotor inertia is significantly reduced, granting the motor extremely high acceleration capability and a very low mechanical time constant.

The Dingzhi Coreless Motor (Dingzhi Coreless Motor) is a series of products manufactured by Dingzhi Technology, a subsidiary of Jiangsu Leili Motor Co., Ltd., covering multiple series from Ø10–Ø42 mm. It belongs to the upstream value chain level, serving as a supplier of core motion components for humanoid robot complete machines.

Why it exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Traditional iron-core DC motors suffer from two inherent defects: first, the cogging effect—the interaction between the rotor core's teeth/slots and the permanent magnets produces periodic torque ripple, manifesting as noticeable "jerking" during low-speed precision control; second, iron losses—the core generates hysteresis and eddy current losses in alternating magnetic fields, reducing efficiency and causing heat generation.

These two defects are dramatically amplified in miniature drive scenarios. Taking the dexterous hand of a humanoid robot as an example, finger joint spaces are extremely limited (typically no more than Ø20 mm in diameter), yet they require smooth, precise, and perceivable torque output. Traditional motors are either too large or exhibit torque ripple that prevents fine manipulation. The hollow cup motor was born precisely to resolve this contradiction: what it truly changes is not how much torque the motor can output, but how smoothly and quickly a miniature motor can respond to commands at low speeds.

From a historical perspective, the hollow cup motor is not a new technology—its principles trace back to the mid-20th century—but it was not until the recent boom in humanoid robots, medical devices, precision optics, and other industries that its characteristics of "low inertia, no cogging, and high response" transitioned from the laboratory to large-scale production.

Principle Breakdown

① Coreless Rotor: Eliminating the Physical Source of Cogging Torque

In traditional DC motors, the interaction between the rotor core's teeth/slots and the permanent magnet field produces torque fluctuations that vary periodically with rotor position, known as cogging torque. By eliminating the iron core in hollow cup motors, the winding is directly exposed to the magnetic field, and the cogging effect is physically removed. This means the torque output at any rotor position depends solely on the armature current, independent of position—this is the fundamental guarantee of its low-speed smoothness.

② Low Moment of Inertia: The Dynamic Basis for High Acceleration

The moment of inertia \(J\) is directly related to the mass distribution of the rotor. The density of the iron core is much higher than that of copper windings; eliminating the core significantly reduces rotor mass. According to the rotational form of Newton's second law:

\[ \tau - \tau_L = J \frac{d\omega}{dt} \]

where \(\tau\) is the electromagnetic torque, \(\tau_L\) is the load torque, and \(\omega\) is the angular velocity. Under the same torque, the smaller the inertia \(J\), the greater the angular acceleration \(d\omega/dt\). The mechanical time constant of hollow cup motors is typically less than 5 ms (per corpus data), meaning it takes only a few milliseconds to go from standstill to rated speed—this is crucial for rapid grasping in dexterous hands and fast stabilization in gimbals.

③ Electromagnetic Relationship for Torque Generation

The relationship between motor output torque and armature current is determined by the torque constant \(K_t\):

\[ \tau = K_t \cdot I \]

where \(\tau\) is the output torque and \(I\) is the armature current. Stall torque corresponds to the maximum torque under the maximum allowable current and is a key indicator for driving dexterous hand fingers. Because the coreless structure eliminates magnetic saturation effects, \(K_t\) remains linear over a wide current range, enabling more precise torque control.

④ Commutation Methods: Trade-offs Between Brushed and Brushless

Brushed models use precious metal brushes or carbon brushes for commutation, offering simple construction and low cost, but suffer from mechanical wear; brushless models achieve longer life through electronic commutation but require additional drivers. In scenarios requiring high reliability, such as dexterous hands, brushless solutions are gradually becoming mainstream.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Humanoid Robot Dexterous Hands (Core Scenario)

Dexterous hands require 16–24 degrees of freedom (per corpus data), with each finger joint needing a miniature, high-response drive source capable of precise torque control. Hollow cup motors paired with planetary gearboxes or tendon-driven transmission are the current mainstream solution. Hollow cup motors from manufacturers such as TQ-RoboDrive are widely used in humanoid robot dexterous hands (per corpus data). Dingzhi's Ø10 series (e.g., 10ZWWC25) with a rated torque of 1.5 mN·m and peak stall torque of 4.5 mN·m precisely matches the torque requirements of finger joints.

② Medical Pumps and Valves

Micro-infusion pumps, insulin pumps, and similar devices require precise control of fluid delivery rates. The low inertia of hollow cup motors enables rapid start/stop and precise speed regulation, while the absence of cogging torque ensures smoothness at low speeds.

③ Drone Gimbals

Gimbals require rapid response to attitude changes to keep cameras stable. The high acceleration capability of hollow cup motors makes them an ideal choice.

④ Security Cameras and Optical Focusing

Rapid, smooth lens movement is required; the low inertia and cogging-free characteristics of hollow cup motors ensure smooth focusing processes.

⑤ 3D Printers

Extruders and heated bed leveling require precise stepper control; hollow cup motors can serve as high-precision alternatives.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited Maximum Torque: The coreless structure increases magnetic circuit reluctance, resulting in lower torque density compared to iron-core motors of the same volume. The Ø10 series has a rated torque of only 1.5 mN·m and cannot directly drive heavy-load joints without a gearbox.

  2. Higher Cost: The self-supporting winding process is complex, and steps such as winding and soldering are difficult to fully automate, leading to higher unit costs than traditional motors.

  3. Heat Dissipation Challenges: Without an iron core, heat generated by the winding cannot be conducted away through the core and must rely on surface dissipation, posing an overheating risk during sustained high-current operation.

  4. Mechanical Strength: The self-supporting winding structure is relatively fragile and less impact-resistant than iron-core rotors, requiring careful design in high-vibration environments.

  5. Lifespan Limitations of Brushed Models: Although precious metal brushes offer low-noise advantages, mechanical wear is unavoidable, and their lifespan is typically shorter than brushless solutions.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A hollow cup motor is a brushless motor"—Incorrect. "Hollow cup" describes the rotor structure (coreless), which is an independent dimension from the commutation method (brushed/brushless). Dingzhi's product line includes both brushed models with precious metal/carbon brushes and brushless models with electronic commutation.

  2. "Hollow cup motors are useless because their torque is small"—Incorrect. Their value lies not in absolute torque but in torque controllability and response speed. When paired with a gearbox, output torque can be amplified 10–100 times while maintaining the high dynamic performance enabled by low inertia.

  3. "No cogging torque = zero torque ripple"—Not entirely correct. While cogging torque is eliminated, torque fluctuations caused by commutation (especially in brushed models) still exist, though at higher frequencies and smaller amplitudes.

  4. "Hollow cup motors always have high efficiency"—It depends on operating conditions. Efficiency can reach 75–80% under high-speed, light-load conditions (per corpus data), but under low-speed, high-torque conditions, copper losses increase proportionally, and efficiency may fall below that of iron-core motors.
