Overview

CNC precision machining is a manufacturing technology that uses computer numerical control systems to govern the relative motion between cutting tools and workpieces, removing material from metal or non-metal materials to shape parts. In the field of humanoid robotics, what it truly changes is not how parts are machined, but the trade-off logic among "design freedom—batch size—cost"—enabling engineers to take responsibility for the manufacturability of every feature before the drawing is finalized.

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition

CNC (Computer Numerical Control) precision machining belongs to the technology category, falls under the manufacturing process domain, and sits at the upstream level of the value chain. Its core definition is: computer numerical control subtractive machining—where pre-programmed G-code instructions drive the motion of machine tool axes, causing the tool to remove excess material from a blank along a predetermined path, ultimately yielding parts that meet dimensional tolerances and surface quality requirements.

In the humanoid robotics supply chain, CNC machining is not an isolated process but a set of process capabilities spanning the entire part-forming sequence. It can produce final parts directly from bar stock or plates, or serve as a subsequent finishing operation for near-net-shape processes such as casting, forging, and additive manufacturing. The latter is more common in engineering practice—die-cast parts require CNC machining to ensure mating surface accuracy, forged parts need CNC machining to achieve complex contours, and additively manufactured parts require CNC machining for smooth mounting surfaces.

CNC machining, along with casting, forging, additive manufacturing, and composite materials, constitutes the five major process routes for forming humanoid robot parts. Casting involves pouring molten metal into a mold to solidify into shape; forging uses pressure to plastically deform metal and refine grain structure; additive manufacturing builds parts by depositing material layer by layer; composites consist of two or more materials (e.g., carbon fiber reinforced plastic), with the fiber layer arrangement called lamination. CNC machining, with its high precision and flexibility, plays the role of "precision backstop" among the five.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before humanoid robots, CNC machining had been maturely applied in aerospace, automotive, and mold industries for decades. However, humanoid robots introduce new structural challenges for CNC machining: many part types, small batch sizes per type, high precision requirements, and rapid iteration speed.

A humanoid robot contains dozens of structural components such as joint housings, mounting bases, and linkages. These parts vary in shape and size. If all were produced via injection molding, mold costs would be prohibitive; if all were additively manufactured, surface roughness and fatigue performance would fail to meet load-bearing requirements. CNC machining precisely fills this gap—no molds required, single-piece feasibility, and micron-level precision—making it the preferred process for small-batch, multi-variety structural parts.

From a historical perspective, CNC machining occupies a bridging position between "prototype validation" and "mass production." During the prototype phase, engineers use CNC to rapidly machine functional samples for assembly validation; during the mass production phase, CNC serves as a finishing operation for die-cast or forged parts, ensuring final assembly accuracy. What it truly changes is not the manufacturing method of a single phase, but rather advancing the "design—process—quality" feedback loop from the trial production stage to the sample stage.

Principle Breakdown

① Coordinate Control: From Digital Model to Tool Path

The core principle of CNC machining is discretizing a three-dimensional part model into a series of tool center point coordinates, then generating continuous motion trajectories through interpolation calculations. Taking a three-axis machining center as an example, the workpiece is fixed on the machine table, and the spindle drives the tool along the X, Y, and Z linear axes, removing material layer by layer according to program instructions. Positioning accuracy depends on the resolution of the servo system and the measurement accuracy of feedback elements; repeat positioning accuracy typically reaches ±0.005 mm (engineering judgment, subject to confirmation against specific machine specifications).

② Trade-off Between Material Removal Rate and Surface Quality

Material removal in CNC machining follows the three cutting elements—cutting speed, feed rate, and depth of cut. Together, these determine the material removal rate (MRR):

\[ MRR = a_p \cdot f \cdot v_c \]

where \(a_p\) is the depth of cut (mm), \(f\) is the feed rate (mm/rev), and \(v_c\) is the cutting speed (m/min). Increasing all three elements shortens machining time but accelerates tool wear, increases cutting forces and vibration, and degrades surface roughness. Parts such as humanoid robot joint housings require high surface roughness on mating surfaces (typically Ra 0.8–1.6 μm, engineering judgment), so finishing stages often employ strategies of small depth of cut, low feed rate, and high spindle speed.

③ Role in the Process Route: Separation of Roughing and Finishing

In typical humanoid robot structural part machining, CNC operations are often divided into roughing and finishing stages. Roughing targets high material removal rates to quickly eliminate most of the allowance; finishing targets dimensional accuracy and surface quality using smaller cutting parameters. Between the two stages, natural aging or stress-relief treatment is usually arranged to release residual stresses introduced during roughing, preventing post-finishing deformation.

Horizontal Comparison

In humanoid robot structural part manufacturing, CNC machining coexists with casting, forging, additive manufacturing, and composite materials in a landscape of both complementarity and competition:

Process Advantages Disadvantages Suitable Parts
Die Casting Complex shapes, high volume, low cost Porosity, lower strength than forgings Torso housings, joint shells
Sand/Investment Casting Large parts, small batches Low precision, extensive post-processing Bases, brackets
Forging High strength, high fatigue life Expensive molds, geometric constraints High-strength linkages, cranks
CNC High precision, flexibility Low material utilization, high labor hours Joint housings, mounting bases, linkages
Additive Manufacturing Complex topology, lightweight Slow, rough surfaces, limited fatigue data Topology-optimized brackets, fixtures
Composite Materials High specific stiffness, designable High cost, difficult joining, hard to recycle Lower leg, arm shells

From the table, CNC machining's core advantage lies in the combination of precision and flexibility—it does not rely on molds like die casting, is not constrained by geometry like forging, yet achieves higher surface quality and dimensional accuracy than additive manufacturing. The trade-off is low material utilization (significant chip waste) and high machining hours, causing per-unit cost to decrease slowly with increasing batch size.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Among humanoid robot OEMs and component suppliers, CNC machining is a universal process throughout the entire development workflow:

  1. Prototype Validation Phase: OEMs use CNC to machine structural parts such as joint housings and mounting bases during the concept machine stage, rapidly assembling functional prototypes for kinematics and dynamics validation. At this stage, part quantities are small and iterations frequent, making CNC's "no mold required" characteristic the only economically viable option.

  2. Small-Batch Trial Production Phase: When the design is frozen and enters small-batch trial production, CNC machining produces tens to hundreds of parts while validating assembly processes and tooling fixtures. At this stage, directly creating molds would incur extremely high modification costs; CNC machining allows continuous design adjustments during trial production.

  3. Finishing Operations in Mass Production: For batch parts produced by die casting or forging, CNC machining serves as a finishing method for critical features such as mating surfaces, threaded holes, and dowel pin holes. For example, the mating surfaces of a die-cast torso housing that interface with joint modules require CNC milling to ensure flatness and positional accuracy.

  4. Post-Processing of Additively Manufactured Parts: After topology-optimized brackets are produced via additive manufacturing, their mounting surfaces and connection holes require CNC machining to achieve smooth surfaces and precise positions, meeting bolted connection and fit requirements.

Limitations and Boundaries

CNC precision machining has clear application boundaries that engineers must recognize during process selection:

  1. Limited Geometric Complexity: CNC machining is a "subtractive" process, and tool accessibility determines geometric feasibility. Deep cavities, internal undercuts, and fine features are difficult or impossible to machine, requiring consideration of additive manufacturing or die casting instead.

  2. Low Material Utilization: CNC machining forms parts by removing material, resulting in significant chip waste, with material utilization typically only 30%–50% (engineering judgment). For expensive materials such as titanium and aluminum alloys, material waste directly drives up costs.

  3. High Machining Hours: Compared to the second-level cycle times of die casting, CNC machining per-unit time is measured in minutes or even hours, making it unsuitable for high-volume production. When annual demand exceeds tens of thousands of parts, a combination of die casting plus CNC finishing is typically more economical.

  4. Residual Stress and Deformation: Residual stresses introduced during cutting can cause deformation of thin-walled parts after machining, requiring control through process optimization (e.g., symmetric machining, step cutting) or subsequent aging treatment.

  5. Size Constrained by Machine Travel: Part dimensions are limited by machine table travel; oversized parts (e.g., robot bases) require large gantry machining centers, significantly increasing equipment costs.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "CNC machining precision is always higher than forging"—Incorrect. Forging improves grain structure through plastic deformation, yielding fatigue strength superior to CNC-machined cut parts; CNC's advantage lies in dimensional accuracy and surface quality, not material mechanical properties. High-strength linkages, cranks, and other critical load-bearing parts are often forged rather than CNC-machined.

  2. "CNC machining can fully replace additive manufacturing"—Incorrect. Additive manufacturing can achieve complex topologies such as internal channels and lattice structures that CNC cannot machine, with material utilization approaching 100%. CNC and additive manufacturing are complementary, and in practice they are often combined (additive forming + CNC finishing).

  3. "CNC machining cost is independent of batch size"—Incorrect. Although CNC requires no molds, preparation costs such as programming, fixturing, and setup are amortized over batch size; the larger the batch, the lower the per-unit cost, though the reduction rate is far slower than die casting. Process selection must consider both batch size and precision requirements.

  4. "CNC machining can achieve very high material utilization"—Incorrect. CNC is a subtractive process where chips are unavoidable, and material utilization is typically lower than near-net-shape processes. For expensive materials, a combined route of near-net-shape forming plus CNC finishing should be considered.
