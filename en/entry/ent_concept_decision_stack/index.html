Overview

The Decision Stack is a software subsystem in humanoid robot software systems that transforms perception results and task objectives into planning, policy, and behavioral decisions. It resides in the intelligence layer of the value chain. What it truly changes is not the performance of individual decision algorithms, but rather the reconstruction of the serial "perception-planning-control" pipeline into a layered, iterative, and verifiable software architecture, enabling robots to evolve from "executing single-point tasks" to "continuously making sound choices in open scenarios." The Decision Stack spans both software middleware and AI model algorithms, serving as the hub connecting environmental understanding with physical actions.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Decision Stack is a software subsystem whose inputs are the environmental state estimates output by the perception module and the task objectives issued by users or the system, and whose outputs are sequences of behavioral instructions consumable by execution layers (e.g., motion planners, real-time controllers). It consists of three layers:

  • Task Planning Layer: Decomposes abstract task objectives (e.g., "pick up goods from Zone A of the warehouse and deliver them to Zone B") into an ordered sequence of subtasks, handling temporal and resource constraints.
  • Policy Selection Layer: Selects execution policies at the subtask level—whether to invoke pre-programmed behavior libraries, trigger learned policies (e.g., VLA models), or request teleoperation assistance.
  • Behavior Decision Layer: Generates specific behavioral instructions (e.g., target poses, velocity commands, grasp postures) at millisecond-to-second timescales, while handling exceptions and fallback logic.

In the humanoid robot knowledge graph, the Decision Stack belongs to the concept type, its value chain level is the intelligence layer, its English name is Decision Stack, and its Korean name is 의사결정 스택.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before the Decision Stack concept matured, the "intelligence" of humanoid robots was highly fragmented: researchers hardcoded task logic into state machines or directly let end-to-end policy networks output joint torques. Both extremes had problems—the former could not handle long-tail scenarios in the open world, and the latter lacked interpretability and safety guarantees.

The Decision Stack exists due to three structural pain points:

  1. Temporal Scale Conflict: Task planning requires second-level or even minute-level reasoning, while joint control requires millisecond-level responses. Without layering, a single algorithm cannot simultaneously satisfy both timescales.
  2. The Contradiction Between Safety and Intelligence: End-to-end policies (e.g., VLA models) excel in perception generalization, but their outputs lack formal guarantees. The Decision Stack addresses this by introducing a policy selection layer that places learned policies within a supervised, fallback-capable framework.
  3. Engineering Iterability: When a task fails, engineers need to locate whether the error stems from perception, planning, or execution. A layered Decision Stack makes problems isolatable, debuggable, and regression-testable.

Historically, the Decision Stack is the evolution of the classic "perception-planning-control" paradigm in the AI era—it retains the engineering advantages of layering while reserving integration points for learned policies (e.g., VLA).

Principle Breakdown

① Layered Abstraction: Decoupling Temporal Scales and Information Granularity

The core principle of the Decision Stack is layered abstraction. Each layer communicates only with adjacent layers and operates at different temporal scales:

  • The task planning layer runs at 1–10 Hz, handling symbolic-level information (goals, constraints, resources).
  • The policy selection layer runs at 10–50 Hz, selecting behavioral modes based on the current state.
  • The behavior decision layer runs at 50–200 Hz, outputting specific motion instructions (e.g., target joint angles, end-effector poses).

This layering enables the system to meet real-time requirements while maintaining a global perspective for high-level decisions. Formally, the Decision Stack can be modeled as a hierarchical extension of the Markov decision process:

\[ \pi(a_t | s_t) = \pi_{\text{low}}\left(a_t \,\middle|\, s_t, \pi_{\text{high}}(s_t)\right) \]

where \(\pi_{\text{high}}\) is the high-level policy (task/policy selection) and \(\pi_{\text{low}}\) is the low-level policy (behavior generation). This decomposition allows learned high-level policies (e.g., large-model-driven task planning) to work collaboratively with classical low-level controllers (e.g., MPC, motion planners).

② Policy Selection: A Hybrid Architecture of Learned and Classical Methods

The key innovation of the Decision Stack lies in the policy selection layer. It maintains a policy library containing:

  • Pre-programmed behaviors: Such as standing, walking, grasping—validated fixed actions.
  • Learned policies: Such as action sequences output by VLA models (see Chapter 19).
  • Teleoperation fallback: When the confidence of autonomous policies is low, human-in-the-loop intervention is requested.

The selection mechanism is typically based on confidence scores and safety constraints. For example, when the VLA model's epistemic uncertainty about the current scene exceeds a threshold, the Decision Stack automatically switches to teleoperation mode—this is precisely the software implementation foundation for "using teleoperation assistance to backstop long-tail tasks while synchronously accumulating data" in home scenarios (see Chapter 27).

③ Data Flywheel: The Decision Stack as a Data Collection Interface

The Decision Stack is not only a decision-making mechanism but also a key node in the data flywheel (ent_concept_data_flywheel). Every decision—whether successful or failed—is recorded as a (state, decision, outcome) triplet and fed back into the training pipeline. Deployment generates data, data improves models, models enhance performance and unlock more deployments—the starting point of this loop is the Decision Stack's logging system.

Perception-compute configuration Sensor suite × edge-side compute Determines the input bandwidth and inference speed available to the Decision Stack

Policy switching latency Time from detecting the need to switch policies to completing the switch Determines the timeliness of safety fallbacks

Note: The Decision Stack itself does not directly determine mechanical metrics such as peak torque density or thrust-to-weight ratio, but it influences energy consumption patterns through task planning, thereby affecting energy autonomy (battery capacity / average power of typical tasks).

The relationship between the Decision Stack and the motion planner is upstream-downstream: the Decision Stack determines "what to do," while the motion planner determines "how to do it." The relationship with the real-time control framework is layered isolation: the Decision Stack operates in the low-frequency AI task domain, while the real-time control framework operates in the high-frequency control domain, communicating through well-defined interfaces.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Tesla Optimus (Data Closed-Loop Driven)

Optimus's Decision Stack is deeply tied to its data flywheel strategy—data generated from tasks performed by robots in Tesla's own factories flows directly back into training, forming a self-reinforcing loop (see Chapter 26). The uniqueness of its Decision Stack lies in the fact that the task planning layer and policy selection layer heavily reuse its automotive-grade AI infrastructure, while the behavior decision layer is customized for bipedal locomotion and dexterous manipulation.

② Agility Digit (Scenario Focus Under RaaS Model)

Digit operates in real warehouses of third-party customers such as Amazon and GXO. Its Decision Stack adopts a "narrowed task domain" strategy—focusing on logistics box handling to achieve a ≥99% task success rate (see Chapter 26). The policy library of the Decision Stack is highly streamlined, with teleoperation fallback reserved only for extreme anomalies.

③ Home Service Robots (Teleoperation-Assisted Paradigm)

Represented by GR-3, companion robots for health and wellness adopt an architecture of "entry via low-operation-intensity tasks + teleoperation backstop" (see Chapter 27). In scenarios such as companionship, reminders, and light object retrieval, the Decision Stack prioritizes invoking pre-programmed behaviors; when encountering long-tail tasks, it switches to human-in-the-loop mode while accumulating data to gradually expand the scope of autonomy.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. The Decision Stack does not solve perception errors: If the upstream perception module outputs incorrect state estimates, no matter how reasonable the Decision Stack's decisions are, they are futile. Engineering mitigation relies on multi-sensor fusion and confidence propagation.
  2. Latency cost of the layered architecture: Each layer of abstraction introduces latency and information loss. For reflex behaviors requiring extremely low latency (e.g., fall protection), the Decision Stack must allow bypass—handled directly by the real-time control framework.
  3. Difficulty in evaluating policy selection: How to assess the quality of the "policy selection layer" itself? Task success rate is the ultimate metric, but it is difficult to attribute—failure may stem from perception, planning, or execution. Engineering judgment: fine-grained logging and replay systems must be established to support offline attribution analysis.
  4. Interpretability of learned policies: Decisions output by VLA models lack formal semantics, making it difficult to embed them in safety arguments. Current engineering practice places them under the supervision of the policy selection layer rather than directly driving execution.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Decision Stack = Task Planner"—Wrong. Task planning is only the topmost layer of the Decision Stack; policy selection and behavior decision are equally core components. Ignoring the latter two leaves the system unable to handle dynamic environments.
  2. "End-to-end models can replace the Decision Stack"—Wrong. End-to-end policies excel in perception generalization, but their outputs lack temporal consistency and safety guarantees. The value of the Decision Stack lies in providing structured supervision and fallback mechanisms for learned policies.
  3. "The Decision Stack is a pure software problem"—Wrong. The architecture of the Decision Stack is strongly influenced by hardware constraints (edge-side compute, sensor configuration) and deployment scenarios (task domain breadth, safety standards). Discussing the Decision Stack in isolation from hardware is empty talk.
  4. "The more complex the Decision Stack, the better"—Wrong. Engineering judgment: the complexity of the Decision Stack should match the task domain. Digit's success lies precisely in the "streamlining" of its Decision Stack—narrowing the task domain to gain reliability, rather than stacking more policies.
