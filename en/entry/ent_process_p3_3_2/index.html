Overview

Reliability objective and allocation is a critical engineering method in the system architecture and electromechanical overall design phase. It systematically decomposes the overall machine reliability target into key components such as joints, batteries, and computing units, and forms quantifiable acceptance criteria using tools such as Reliability Block Diagrams (RBD), FMEA, and MTBF allocation. What it truly changes is not the reactive mindset of "repair after failure," but the proactive design culture of "prevention before failure"—exposing risks on paper through structured tables and scoring before failures occur.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Reliability objective and allocation is the core connecting link in reliability engineering. It receives the overall machine reliability requirements from the upstream requirements definition phase, transforms them into quantitative reliability indicators (such as MTBF target values) at the subsystem and component levels, and defines the Owner, timeline, and acceptance criteria for each indicator.

In the P3.3.2 work package of WBS V3, reliability objective and allocation is expanded into five third-level subtasks: input review and target quantification (P3.3.2.1), approach/method design (P3.3.2.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P3.3.2.3), verification and issue closure (P3.3.2.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P3.3.2.5). Each subtask follows a unified four-level key action structure, ensuring that every step from input to delivery has a clear execution path and acceptance criteria.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In early robot development, reliability design often relied on the empiricism of "success if the prototype runs," lacking systematic quantitative methods. This led to three typical problems: first, the overall machine reliability target was vague, with no clear indicator allocation relationships among components; second, reliability verification depended on long-duration actual operation testing, but the development cycle and testing costs of humanoid robots made this verification approach engineering-infeasible; third, insufficient failure data accumulation made accurate reliability prediction difficult.

Reliability objective and allocation is the engineering discipline born to address these pain points. What it truly changes is not the quantity of testing, but the decomposition logic from "overall machine target to component indicators"—all reliability design decisions must undergo quantitative justification and solution freezing before entering detailed design. By decomposing the overall machine reliability target to key components such as joints, batteries, and computing units, every component engineer can clarify their reliability responsibility early in development, rather than discovering problems only during overall machine integration.

Principle Breakdown

① Series/Parallel Logic of Reliability Block Diagrams

The Reliability Block Diagram (RBD) is the foundational tool for reliability allocation. It graphically expresses the reliability logic relationships among system components. For humanoid robots, key components such as joints, batteries, and computing units typically form a series system—the failure of any single component can lead to loss of overall machine function. The relationship between the overall system reliability and component reliabilities in a series system is:

\[ R_{\text{system}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} R_i \]

where R_system is the overall system reliability, R_i is the reliability of the i-th component, and n is the number of key components. This formula reveals the fundamental nature of series systems: the overall system reliability cannot exceed that of the weakest component. Therefore, the core task of reliability allocation is to identify weak links and allocate indicators reasonably.

② Proportional Method for MTBF Allocation

MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) is the most commonly used quantitative indicator in reliability allocation. When data is limited, engineering practice often adopts the proportional allocation method: based on historical failure rate data or engineering experience of each component, the overall machine MTBF target is allocated to components proportionally. The allocation formula is:

\[ MTBF_i = \frac{MTBF_{\text{system}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j} \cdot \frac{1}{w_i} \]

where w_i is the weight coefficient of the i-th component, reflecting its complexity, operating stress, and historical failure rate. The larger the weight of a component, the stricter the allocated MTBF target (i.e., the higher the required reliability). The engineering premise of this method is that the setting of weight coefficients must be based on sufficient Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA); otherwise, the allocation results are merely a numbers game.

③ FMEA-Driven Risk Priority Ranking

FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) plays the role of the risk identification front-end in reliability allocation. Starting from the smallest constituent units, it identifies potential failure modes one by one, evaluates their impact severity (S), occurrence frequency (O), and detection difficulty (D), and calculates the Risk Priority Number (RPN = S × O × D). The higher the RPN of a failure mode, the stricter the indicator it should receive in reliability allocation. The output of FMEA directly guides the setting of weight coefficients, moving the allocation results from "guesswork" to "evidence-based."

Key Parameters and Specifications

The core deliverables of reliability objective and allocation are the reliability target allocation table and key component MTBF targets. Its key parameters include:

Parameter Description Source
Overall machine MTBF target Provided by the upstream requirements definition phase; the starting point of allocation Upstream input
Component MTBF target Reliability indicators allocated to each key component Allocation result
Weight coefficient w_i Reflects component complexity and historical failure rate FMEA and historical data
Risk Priority Number RPN S × O × D, used to rank failure modes FMEA analysis
Test time Available time window for reliability verification Project plan
Accelerated testing capability Whether accelerated life testing conditions are available Resource assessment

Key constraints include: limited test time, insufficient accelerated testing capability, and limited data. These constraints determine that reliability allocation cannot rely excessively on statistical inference but must combine engineering judgment with qualitative FMEA analysis.

Horizontal Comparison

Reliability objective and allocation has a clear division of labor with adjacent reliability engineering methods:

Method Positioning Input Output
RBD System reliability modeling System architecture diagram Reliability functions, weak links
FMEA Failure mode identification Component list, functional descriptions Failure mode list, RPN ranking
MTBF allocation Indicator decomposition Overall machine MTBF target, weight coefficients Component MTBF target table
Accelerated life testing Indicator verification Prototype, test plan Failure data, MTBF estimates

RBD answers "how system reliability is constituted," FMEA answers "where failures might occur," MTBF allocation answers "how reliable each component should be," and accelerated life testing answers "whether actual performance meets the target." Together, the four form a complete reliability design closed loop, with reliability objective and allocation serving as the pivotal connecting link.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Reliability objective and allocation is widely applied in fields with high safety requirements, such as humanoid robots, aerospace, and automotive electronics. In humanoid robot development, its typical application scenarios include:

  • Joint module reliability allocation: Allocating the overall machine MTBF target to individual joints, with high-load joints (such as hip and knee joints) receiving stricter MTBF targets due to greater operating stress.
  • Battery system reliability allocation: Battery management system failures can trigger thermal runaway; high-risk failure modes are identified through FMEA and assigned strict reliability indicators.
  • Computing unit reliability allocation: Failure of the main control computing unit can cause loss of overall machine control; redundant design and high reliability targets are typically adopted.

In the P3.3.2 work package, the implementation path follows a five-step method: input review and target quantification (P3.3.2.1) → approach/method design (P3.3.2.2) → implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P3.3.2.3) → verification and issue closure (P3.3.2.4) → documentation output and downstream delivery (P3.3.2.5). Each step has clear four-level key actions, ensuring the execution process is traceable and verifiable.

Limitations and Boundaries

Reliability objective and allocation has clear applicability boundaries. First, it depends on the accuracy of upstream inputs—if the overall machine MTBF target itself lacks justification, no matter how refined the allocation results are, they are meaningless. Second, it is constrained by data availability—when data is limited, the setting of weight coefficients inevitably contains engineering judgment, and the confidence level of allocation results is limited. Third, it cannot replace verification—allocation is only target setting; actual reliability must be confirmed through testing (including accelerated life testing).

Additionally, the series system assumption is a simplification premise of RBD. For subsystems containing redundant design (such as dual battery backup), parallel logic changes the reliability calculation formula, and a simple series model would underestimate system reliability in such cases. Engineering practice requires selecting the correct RBD model based on the actual architecture.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Once MTBF allocation is done, everything is settled"—Wrong. Allocation is only target setting; actual compliance must be confirmed through the verification and issue closure in P3.3.2.4; otherwise, the allocation table is just numbers on paper.
  2. "FMEA is a prerequisite for reliability allocation"—Not entirely. FMEA is an important input for weight coefficient setting, but when data is limited, engineering experience can serve as the initial basis for weights, with subsequent FMEA iterations for refinement.
  3. "Reliability allocation is the job of reliability engineers"—Wrong. What it truly changes is not the role of a single function, but the engineering discipline of the entire development team—every component Owner must be accountable for their own MTBF target.
  4. "Higher RPN means higher priority for improvement"—Generally correct, but note that RPN is the product of three factors; the same RPN may correspond to different risk characteristics (e.g., high severity with low occurrence vs. low severity with high occurrence), requiring engineering judgment.
