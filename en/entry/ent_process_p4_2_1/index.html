Overview

Motor type selection is the primary process node in the joint module and drive system design (P4.2) of humanoid robots. Its task is to map the joint-level requirements (peak torque, speed, thermal limits) output by upstream dynamics analysis into a specific motor specification sheet and a selection comparison table. What it truly changes is not "choosing a motor that can spin," but the physical ceiling of the robot's overall dynamic performance and energy efficiency—selection errors are amplified step by step in subsequent reducer matching, driver design, and thermal management, ultimately determining whether the robot can avoid burnout, demagnetization, and step loss under peak operating conditions.

Content

What It Is: Engineering Mapping from Requirements to Specification Sheets

Motor type selection is a systematic decision-making process that converts joint-level dynamic requirements (peak torque, RMS current, speed range) into a specific motor model, winding parameters, and thermal verification conclusions. In humanoid robots, motor selection cannot be performed in isolation; it must be considered jointly with the reducer: the motor provides raw power, the reducer amplifies torque and adjusts speed, and together they form the joint's "usable operating region."

In WBS V3, this process is the first step in the P4.2 motor and reducer phase, building on the dynamics analysis results from Phase 3 and providing interface constraints for reducer selection, driver design, and structural integration. It covers five level-4 subtasks: input review, candidate solution evaluation, prototyping, verification closed-loop, and documentation delivery, serving as a key node to ensure downstream dependents receive qualified inputs.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Motor type selection for humanoid robots has long faced a fundamental contradiction: joint space is extremely limited, but peak torque requirements are extremely high. High-torque joints such as the hip and shoulder require frameless torque motors to maximize torque density; fingers and wrists require small housed motors to fit compact spaces. If selection focuses only on rated torque while ignoring thermal limits, the motor will demagnetize due to excessive winding temperature rise under sustained high loads; if it focuses only on peak torque while ignoring the speed range, it cannot meet the demands of rapid dynamic motion.

Traditional industrial robots can use reducers with large reduction ratios (above 100:1) to amplify torque because their loads are fixed and motion patterns are predictable; however, humanoid robots, when walking, jumping, or being pushed by external forces, must respond quickly to impacts, sense external forces, and actively comply. Motor selection must simultaneously satisfy peak torque, continuous power, thermal limits, and speed requirements, and unify the motor's electrical limits, the reducer's mechanical limits, and thermal limits into a single "usable operating region"—this is the essential difference from traditional selection.

Principle Breakdown

① Torque-Speed Characteristics: The Physical Foundation of Motor Selection

The steady-state voltage and torque equations of a brushless DC motor are:

\[ \tau = K_t I, \quad V = R I + K_e \omega \]

where \（\tau\） is the output torque, \（K_t\） is the torque constant, \（I\） is the armature current, \（V\） is the bus voltage, \（R\） is the winding resistance, \（K_e\） is the back-EMF constant, and \（\omega\） is the angular velocity. This equation reveals the core constraints of selection: peak torque is determined by peak current, which is limited by the driver's current capability; continuous torque is determined by thermal limits, which are limited by winding resistance and heat dissipation conditions. The higher the speed, the larger the back-EMF \（K_e \omega\）, the smaller the available voltage margin, and the lower the peak torque—this is the physical origin of the torque-speed curve.

② Thermal-Mechanical Boundary: The Key to Joint Motor-Reducer Selection

Motor selection cannot look at rated torque in isolation; instead, the motor's electrical limits, the reducer's mechanical limits, and thermal limits must be unified into a single "usable operating region." Peak torque corresponds to short-term overload capability, continuous torque corresponds to long-term thermal equilibrium, and stall torque corresponds to the limit output at zero speed. During selection, winding temperature rise and demagnetization risk must be verified: if the continuous RMS current exceeds the thermal limit, the winding temperature will keep rising, ultimately causing irreversible demagnetization of the permanent magnets.

③ Encoder Resolution and Back-EMF Constant

Encoder resolution determines position feedback accuracy and directly affects the performance of force control and position control. For fingertip force control, reducer backlash can be compensated through high-stiffness transmission and force closed-loop control; for position control, backlash should be \（<0.5°\）. The back-EMF constant \（K_e\） determines the proportional relationship between speed and induced voltage, affecting the utilization of the driver's bus voltage.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Who Uses It: Application Cases

In the humanoid robot development process, motor type selection is the primary process in the P4.2 motor and reducer phase, with downstream steps including reducer selection, driver design, structural integration, and thermal management. Specific application scenarios include:

  • Hip joint: Requires high-torque-density frameless torque motors, paired with reducers to achieve high torque output and meet the peak demands of walking and jumping.
  • Shoulder joint: Also requires high-torque-density motors, but inertia matching issues caused by arm reach must be considered.
  • Finger joints: Use small housed motors, paired with high-stiffness transmission and force closed-loop compensation, to achieve precise fingertip force control.
  • Wrist joints: Need to achieve multi-degree-of-freedom motion in compact spaces; motor selection must balance size and torque.

Limitations and Boundaries

Motor type selection has the following inherent limitations:

  1. The trap of isolated selection: Motor selection cannot be performed independently of the reducer, driver, and thermal management. Looking only at rated torque while ignoring thermal limits leads to excessive winding temperature rise under continuous operation; looking only at peak torque while ignoring the speed range fails to meet rapid dynamic motion requirements.
  2. The hidden constraint of thermal limits: Continuous torque is limited by thermal resistance and heat dissipation conditions, which in turn are affected by the structural integration approach. Winding temperature rise and demagnetization risk must be verified during selection, but thermal resistance parameters often rely on empirical estimation.
  3. Supply chain and lead time constraints: Even if the selection is technically optimal, if the lead time cannot meet project milestones, the solution must still be adjusted. Key constraints include driver bus voltage, current capability, and lead time.
  4. Impact of backlash on control performance: Reducer backlash leads to uncertainty in fingertip position. For position control, backlash should be \（<0.5°\）； for force control, backlash can be compensated through high-stiffness transmission and force closed-loop control, but this increases control complexity.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The higher the peak torque, the better"—Wrong. Peak torque corresponds to short-term overload capability, but continuous operation is determined by thermal limits. If only peak torque is pursued while thermal resistance is ignored, the motor will demagnetize due to excessive winding temperature rise under sustained high loads.
  2. "Rated torque being sufficient is enough"—Wrong. The dynamic motions of humanoid robots (walking, jumping) generate peak torque demands far exceeding rated values; selection must satisfy both peak and continuous requirements.
  3. "Motor selection is an isolated decision"—Wrong. The motor must be selected jointly with the reducer, unifying the motor's electrical limits, the reducer's mechanical limits, and thermal limits into a single "usable operating region." Isolated selection leads to cascading problems in subsequent stages.
  4. "Frameless motors are always superior to housed motors"—Wrong. Frameless torque motors are suitable for high-torque joints such as the hip and shoulder, but compact spaces like fingers and wrists are better suited to small housed motors. Selection must be flexibly determined based on joint position and requirements.
