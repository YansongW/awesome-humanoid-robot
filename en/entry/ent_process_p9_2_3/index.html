Overview

Thermal testing and iteration is a critical process node in the humanoid robot thermal management simulation and iteration phase (P9). Its core methods include thermocouples/infrared thermography, accelerated temperature rise testing, and comparative simulation correction. By comparing simulation results with measured data, it corrects thermal resistance/contact thermal resistance parameters, ultimately delivering a thermal test report, acceptance results with simulation-measurement error < 20%, and design iteration records. What it truly changes is not the single-point verification of "measure the temperature once and be done," but rather iterating thermal simulation from a "prediction of questionable credibility" into a "design basis calibrated by measurement"—giving every thermal resistance parameter experimental backing.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition of Thermal Testing and Iteration

Thermal testing and iteration is the process node responsible for "verification and correction" in the thermal management simulation and iteration phase (P9). Its core task is to obtain real temperature rise data through physical testing methods (thermocouples/infrared thermography, accelerated temperature rise testing), compare it with simulation results, and then correct the thermal resistance/contact thermal resistance parameters in the simulation model, gradually bringing the simulation model closer to real physical behavior. In WBS V3, this process is decomposed into five third-level subtasks: input organization and target quantification (P9.2.3.1), scheme/method design (P9.2.3.2), modeling/simulation and prototype implementation (P9.2.3.3), test execution and result analysis (P9.2.3.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P9.2.3.5), each with clear fourth-level key actions.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the early stages of humanoid robot development, thermal design often relied on empirical formulas and theoretical estimation. Thermal resistance parameters in simulation models came from material handbooks or supplier nominal values, but these parameters deviated significantly from actual assembly conditions—interface contact thermal resistance is greatly influenced by assembly pressure, interface materials, and surface roughness, with theoretical values often deviating from measured values by more than 30% (engineering judgment). If thermal design were based directly on uncalibrated simulation models, two consequences could arise: first, over-design, where heat sink volume and weight exceed specifications, affecting the robot's dynamic performance; second, under-design, where joint motor temperature rise exceeds limits under continuous operating conditions, causing permanent magnet demagnetization or winding insulation aging.

Thermal testing and iteration exists precisely to solve this pain point. It binds "simulation prediction" and "measured verification" into a closed loop: test first, then simulate; calibrate simulation with measurement; simulate again after testing—until the simulation-measurement error converges to an acceptable range. Within phase P9, it sits at the midstream layer, receiving inputs from upstream thermal requirements and models (P9.1) and thermal solution design (P9.2), and delivering verified thermal design constraints downstream to full-system integration (P15).

Principle Breakdown

① Comparative Simulation Correction: Core Logic of Thermal Resistance Parameter Calibration

The methodological core of thermal testing and iteration is "comparative simulation correction." Its working logic is: first establish a thermal network model or CFD model with initial thermal resistance parameters; then obtain measured temperature rise data via thermocouples/infrared thermography; compare measured data with simulation predictions and calculate deviations; based on the deviations, inversely correct the thermal resistance/contact thermal resistance parameters in the model; re-simulate with the corrected model, compare again with measurements, and iterate until convergence.

The mathematical basis for thermal resistance correction is the thermal network equation. For one-dimensional steady-state heat conduction, the relationship between heat flow Q and temperature difference ΔT is:

\[ Q = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{\text{th}}} = \frac{T_{\text{hot}} - T_{\text{amb}}}{R_{\text{cond}} + R_{\text{cont}} + R_{\text{conv}}} \]

where R_cond is the conduction thermal resistance, R_cont is the contact thermal resistance, and R_conv is the convection thermal resistance. After obtaining Q and ΔT from measurement, the total thermal resistance R_th can be derived inversely, then compared with the initial simulation value to locate the source of deviation—if the deviation mainly comes from interfaces, correct R_cont; if from the heat sink surface, correct R_conv.

② Accelerated Temperature Rise Testing: Engineering Technique for Time Compression

Under natural convection cooling, the temperature rise process follows an exponential law, and reaching steady state requires several times the time constant. Accelerated temperature rise testing shortens the test cycle by increasing load power or ambient temperature, allowing temperature rise to reach steady state faster. The theoretical basis is the temperature rise time constant τ = R_th · C_th (where C_th is thermal capacitance). Increasing power does not change τ, but it raises the steady-state temperature rise ΔT_ss, making temperature changes easier for sensors to resolve, thereby obtaining data with sufficient signal-to-noise ratio in a shorter time (engineering judgment).

③ Sensor Placement and Environmental Control: Guaranteeing Data Credibility

Thermocouple placement directly affects measurement accuracy. Key measurement points include: motor winding ends (hot spots), motor housing, reducer housing, heat sink base plate, and power device junction-to-case locations. Infrared thermography is used for non-contact surface temperature field distribution measurement, quickly identifying hot spot regions, but it is affected by emissivity settings and requires point calibration with thermocouples. Ambient temperature control is another key constraint—the test environment must maintain a constant temperature (typically controlled within 20–25°C range, engineering judgment); otherwise, ambient temperature fluctuations will drown out the true temperature rise signal.

Horizontal Comparison

Thermal testing and iteration has clear division of labor with adjacent process nodes. Compared with the upstream "thermal requirements and models" (P9.1), P9.2.3 does not perform heat source power consumption statistics or initial modeling, but focuses on model verification and parameter correction; compared with "thermal solution design" (other subtasks of P9.2), P9.2.3 does not perform heat sink selection or airflow channel design, but verifies whether these designs achieve the expected results. At the methodological level, thermal testing and iteration shares similarities with structural strength testing (e.g., prototype testing in phase P8)—both follow the "simulation-measurement comparison" verification logic—but thermal testing places greater emphasis on parameter calibration rather than pass/fail binary judgment. Its output is not merely a verification conclusion, but also corrected simulation model parameters.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Thermal testing and iteration is widely applied in engineering practice in the following scenarios:

  • Joint module thermal calibration: Conducting temperature rise testing on high-power-density joint modules such as hip and knee joints to calibrate the contact thermal resistance between motor, reducer, and housing, and correct the thermal network model. Typical operating conditions are continuous walking or squat cycles, with test durations typically lasting several hours (engineering judgment).
  • Computing bay thermal verification: Performing accelerated temperature rise testing on embedded computing units (e.g., industrial PCs, AI acceleration modules) to verify whether heat sink and thermal pad selections meet chip junction temperature limits.
  • Battery bay thermal management verification: Conducting charge/discharge temperature rise testing on battery modules to verify the effectiveness of thermal management strategies (active cooling or passive heat dissipation) under extreme operating conditions.

Limitations and Boundaries

Thermal testing and iteration has clear application boundaries. First, simplification of test conditions—laboratory testing typically uses constant-temperature environments and fixed load profiles, whereas actual robot operation faces variable loads, variable ambient temperatures, and coupled multiple heat sources; extrapolation of test results requires caution. Second, non-uniqueness of parameter correction—when multiple thermal resistance parameters deviate simultaneously, limited measurement point data may not uniquely determine the correction amount for each parameter; sensitivity analysis is needed to identify dominant parameters. Third, time cost—the complete "test-correct-retest" closed loop is time-consuming, and under tight project schedules, iteration rounds may be forced to compress, leading to insufficient error convergence. Fourth, sensor-induced errors—thermocouple contact thermal resistance, lead wire heat conduction, and infrared thermography emissivity settings all introduce measurement errors that must be controlled through calibration and repeated testing.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Error < 20% means a good model"—not entirely correct. The 20% figure is the acceptance floor, but different locations have different thermal sensitivities: a 20% error in motor winding temperature rise could mean a huge deviation in insulation life estimation, while a 20% error in housing surface temperature may be perfectly acceptable. In engineering, stricter error requirements should be imposed on critical thermal hot spots (engineering judgment).

  2. "More test data is always better"—wrong. The value of test data lies in information content, not quantity. Blindly adding measurement points without sensitivity analysis may yield large amounts of redundant data, actually masking deviations in dominant thermal resistances. Key measurement points should first be identified through simulation sensitivity analysis, then sensors placed accordingly.

  3. "One simulation correction is enough"—wrong. The essence of thermal testing and iteration is closed-loop iteration; a single correction often only converges part of the deviation. In particular, contact thermal resistance changes with assembly state and temperature, requiring multiple verifications under different operating conditions. The corpus explicitly requires "design iteration records" as a deliverable, precisely emphasizing this point.

  4. "Infrared thermography can replace thermocouples"—wrong. Infrared thermography measures surface temperature field distribution, with accuracy affected by emissivity, reflective environment, and measurement distance, and it cannot measure internal temperatures (such as winding ends). The correct approach is to use infrared thermography for hot spot scanning and thermocouples for precise measurement at key points—the two are complementary.
