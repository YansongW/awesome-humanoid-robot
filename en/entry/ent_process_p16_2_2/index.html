Overview

The establishment of a quality control system is the P16.2.2 process node in WBS V3, belonging to the supply chain and quality system subtask family under the "Pilot Run and Mass Production Preparation" phase. What it truly changes is not "adding a few more inspection stations," but rather shifting quality from the passive screening of "sorting out defective parts after the fact" to proactive control where "process capability is predictable, anomalies are traceable, and batch defects are preventable." This process uses SPC, FAI, incoming inspection, in-process inspection, outgoing inspection, and MSA as its methodological tools, and outputs quality plans, inspection specifications, and SPC control charts. It is the critical quality infrastructure for humanoid robots transitioning from prototypes to mass production.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The establishment of a quality control system (P16.2.2) is a process node in the product development flow that builds systematic quality assurance capabilities before mass production launch. It is not a single action, but rather a set of interlocking engineering closed loops: each subtask follows the five-step paradigm of "input review → solution design → implementation/prototype → verification closed loop → documentation delivery." In WBS V3, this process is decomposed into five third-level subtasks: P16.2.2.1 Input Review and Target Quantification, P16.2.2.2 Algorithm/Control Solution Design, P16.2.2.3 Algorithm Implementation and Simulation Verification, P16.2.2.4 Algorithm Tuning and Performance Verification, and P16.2.2.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery.

The upstream of this process is the frozen output of P16.2.1 Supplier Selection and Audit, and the downstream connects to P16.2.3 Incoming and In-Process Anomaly Handling. What it truly changes is not "adding a few more inspection stations," but rather shifting quality from the passive screening of "sorting out defective parts after the fact" to proactive control where "process capability is predictable, anomalies are traceable, and batch defects are preventable."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the transition of humanoid robots from prototypes to mass production, the necessity of establishing a quality control system stems from three deep-seated pain points:

① The quality illusion of the prototype stage. During the P15 system integration and verification testing phase, engineers manually debug and calibrate unit by unit, making each prototype a "special case." This "handcrafted workshop" quality performance cannot be replicated on the production line—when output jumps from single digits to batch scale, without systematic quality control, the defect rate will amplify exponentially.

② The high-precision requirements of humanoid robots. Core components of humanoid robots, such as joint modules, reducers, and sensors, are extremely sensitive to dimensional tolerances and assembly precision. For key parameters such as machined bearing seat dimensions, tightening torque/angle curves, press-fit force curves, critical mating dimensions of injection-molded covers, and friction and backlash data from module EOL testing, batch consistency is impossible without statistical process control.

③ The catastrophic consequences of batch defects. In the mass production phase, an undetected systematic deviation can lead to the scrapping of an entire batch. The core value of a quality control system lies in "preventing batch defects"—by using SPC control charts to monitor process stability in real time, warnings are issued before deviations exceed specification limits.

Principle Breakdown

① The mathematical foundation of Statistical Process Control (SPC)

The core of SPC is the process capability index. The process capability indices \(C_p\) and \(C_{pk}\) are defined as:

\[ C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\sigma}, \qquad C_{pk} = \min\!\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\sigma}, \frac{\mu-LSL}{3\sigma}\right) \]

where \(USL/LSL\) are the upper and lower specification limits, and \(\mu,\sigma\) are the process mean and standard deviation. \(C_p\) measures the inherent potential capability of the process (assuming the process center coincides with the specification center), while \(C_{pk}\) also accounts for process shift and is a more conservative metric. Engineering practice typically requires \(C_{pk} \ge 1.33\) (corresponding to a defect rate of approximately 30 ppm), and critical safety characteristics may even require \(C_{pk} \ge 1.67\) (engineering judgment).

② The closed-loop logic of the five-step paradigm

The five third-level subtasks of this process form a complete PDCA cycle:

  • P16.2.2.1 Input Review and Target Quantification: List all upstream inputs and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task owners, timelines, and a risk register. This is the "Plan" stage—without quantified targets, all subsequent verification lacks a baseline.
  • P16.2.2.2 Algorithm/Control Solution Design: Develop no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, establish an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize a review, and freeze the solution. This is the "Design" stage—solution comparison avoids prematurely locking in a suboptimal path.
  • P16.2.2.3 Algorithm Implementation and Simulation Verification: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulation or prototype verification, and document anomalies and deviations. This is the "Do" stage—before actual production line launch, solution feasibility is verified in simulation or with offline data.
  • P16.2.2.4 Algorithm Tuning and Performance Verification: Develop test/review plans and pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output a problem list with improvement measures. This is the "Check" stage—boundary testing exposes the solution's weak points.
  • P16.2.2.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Write documentation per templates with references to raw data, complete internal review and version control, and publish and notify downstream dependents. This is the "Act" stage—solidifying the verified solution into executable specifications.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The establishment of the quality control system is the core quality process in the P16 pilot run and mass production preparation phase, with its application spanning the entire production ramp-up process:

  • Assembly Line Planning and SOP (P16.1.3): Each station on the assembly line must set inspection points according to the inspection specifications output by P16.2.2. SPC monitoring of tightening torque/angle curves is directly embedded in the data acquisition system of the tightening tools at assembly stations.
  • Pilot Run (P16.3.1): During the pilot run, SPC control charts monitor key process parameters in real time. When the process capability index falls below the threshold, the anomaly handling process (P16.2.3) is triggered.
  • PPAP/Mass Production Readiness Assessment (P16.3.3): One of the core contents of the PPAP submission is the process capability study report, which directly references the SPC control charts and process capability indices output by P16.2.2.

Limitations and Boundaries

The establishment of a quality control system is not a panacea; its limitations and boundaries include:

① The physical limits of inspection capability. The quality control system depends on the capability of inspection equipment. If the precision of the inspection equipment is insufficient to resolve process variation, SPC control charts become meaningless. MSA (Measurement System Analysis) exists precisely to verify the acceptability of the measurement system itself—but MSA can only evaluate existing measurement methods; it cannot overcome physical measurement limits.

② The time cost of personnel training. The key constraints explicitly list "personnel training." The interpretation of SPC control charts, the application of anomaly detection rules, and FAI measurement operations all require trained personnel to execute. In the early stages of production ramp-up, insufficient operator proficiency is a common cause of quality control system failure.

③ It does not cover design quality issues. The quality control system addresses "manufacturing consistency" rather than "design correctness." If the product design itself has defects (e.g., unreasonable tolerance allocation), no quality control system, no matter how robust, can save it—this is precisely why DFM/DFA reviews (P16.1.1) are conducted upstream.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "SPC is just drawing control charts"—Wrong. The core of SPC is the judgment and maintenance of the "in-control state." Control charts are merely tools; the real value lies in root cause analysis and corrective actions after anomaly detection. SPC without a closed loop is just "self-deception through charting."

  2. "FAI is done once and that's it"—Wrong. FAI (First Article Inspection) is the first comprehensive inspection for a new product or new process, but FAI must be re-executed after process changes, supplier changes, or resumption of production after a stoppage. FAI is a "baseline confirmation," not a "one-time action."

  3. "Establishing a quality system is the quality department's job"—Wrong. Among the five subtasks of P16.2.2, P16.2.2.2 requires "developing no fewer than 2 candidate solutions" and "organizing a review and freezing the solution," which requires collaboration across process, design, and production departments. The quality control system is a cross-functional engineering activity, not a solo performance by the quality department.
