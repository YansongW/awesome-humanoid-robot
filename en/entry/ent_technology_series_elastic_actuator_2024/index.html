Overview

A Series Elastic Actuator (SEA) is a joint actuator architecture that places an elastic element (such as a spring) in series between the motor and the output load. It indirectly estimates the output force by measuring the deformation of the elastic element, thereby transforming traditional "position control" into high-precision "force control," while inherently providing impact resistance and energy storage capabilities. What it truly changes is not how the actuator generates force, but rather it converts "force"—a physical quantity that traditionally requires expensive sensors for direct measurement—into a state variable that can be indirectly inferred through inexpensive displacement measurements. This shift has moved force control from the laboratory into practical robotics.

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition

The core structure of a Series Elastic Actuator (SEA) is: motor → gearbox → elastic element → load. Unlike rigid direct coupling (motor-gearbox-load), the SEA deliberately inserts a low-stiffness elastic body into the transmission chain, causing the joint to produce a measurable deformation \(\Delta \theta\) under load. The output torque is given by Hooke's Law:

\[ \tau = k \, \Delta \theta \]

where \(k\) is the elastic stiffness. By measuring the relative displacement across the elastic element using high-resolution encoders or potentiometers, the output torque can be precisely inferred without the need for expensive joint torque sensors. This design converts the force control problem into a displacement measurement problem—and displacement measurement is far more mature, cheaper, and more reliable in engineering than force measurement.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Traditional rigid actuators (servo motors + high-ratio harmonic gearboxes) are designed for position control, but they suffer from three critical flaws in the real world faced by humanoid robots:

  1. Poor force control accuracy: In high-ratio transmissions, the relationship between motor current and output torque is severely distorted by friction, backlash, and nonlinearities, with open-loop force control errors reaching 20%–30% (engineering estimate).
  2. Weak impact resistance: In rigid transmission chains, instantaneous impacts (such as foot contact or falls) are transmitted directly to the gearbox and motor, easily damaging precision gears.
  3. Low energy efficiency: During the gait cycle, the negative work (braking) in the stance phase and positive work (acceleration) in the swing phase cannot be mutually converted, and energy is dissipated wastefully.

The introduction of SEA alleviates all three problems simultaneously: the elastic element acts as a "mechanical low-pass filter" absorbing high-frequency impact components; elastic deformation provides a clean force signal; and the spring's energy storage-release characteristics enable energy recovery during gait. Its historical positioning is as a bridge between force control precision and mechanical robustness—allowing robots to both "feel force" and withstand "collisions."

Principle Breakdown

① From "Measuring" to "Calculating" Force: The Elastic Element as a Force Sensor

The core insight of SEA is: rather than measuring force directly with expensive force sensors, use inexpensive displacement sensors to measure spring deformation. The elastic element converts a force signal into a displacement signal, and displacement measurement (encoders, potentiometers) offers resolution and bandwidth far superior to traditional force sensors. Force control accuracy is jointly determined by displacement sensor resolution and spring stiffness: the lower the stiffness, the larger the deformation for the same force, and the higher the resolution—but at the cost of reduced position control bandwidth.

② Physical Mechanism of Impact Resistance: Mechanical Low-Pass Filtering

When the load end experiences an instantaneous impact force \(F_{\text{imp}}\), the elastic element deforms and absorbs part of the energy. The force transmitted to the motor side is:

\[ F_{\text{motor}} = F_{\text{imp}} - k \, \Delta \theta \]

The high-frequency components of the impact are filtered out by the spring's compliance, and the motor and gearbox only experience slowly varying low-frequency forces. This is essentially mechanical impedance matching—by reducing the equivalent stiffness of the transmission chain, impact energy is converted into spring potential energy rather than structural damage energy.

③ Energy Storage: "Spring-Mass" Dynamics in Gait

During the stance phase of walking, body weight compresses the elastic element, storing potential energy; during the swing phase, the spring releases energy to assist leg lifting. This resembles the energy storage function of the human Achilles tendon. SEA makes the joint behave as a controllable spring-damper system, whose dynamic characteristics can be adjusted online by tuning spring stiffness and damping coefficients.

Selection Considerations: Stiffness selection is the core trade-off in SEA design—lower stiffness yields higher force control resolution and better impact resistance, but lower position control bandwidth and slower response. A compromise between "force precision" and "position response" must be made based on task requirements. Additionally, attention should be paid to the fatigue life of the elastic element (metal springs outperform rubber) and temperature stability.

SEA and QDD are the two mainstream approaches for compliant actuators in current humanoid robots: SEA sacrifices position bandwidth for force control accuracy and impact resistance, while QDD retains high bandwidth but its force control accuracy is limited by current estimation. The two are complementary rather than substitutive.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

  • MIT Cheetah Series: Uses SEA to achieve landing impact absorption and high-bandwidth physical interaction (source: neighbor card ent_paper_proprioceptive_actuator_design_mit_cheet).
  • Humanoid Robot Walking Control: Ankle joints use SEA to absorb ground contact impacts, storing energy during the stance phase and releasing it during the swing phase to reduce energy consumption (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11).
  • Human-Robot Collaborative Robotic Arms: Arm joints use SEA to ensure contact safety; during unexpected collisions, the elastic element absorbs impacts to protect the operator (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.7.6).
  • Landing Impact Absorption Control: After fall detection, the joint switches to an extremely low-impedance mode, and the SEA's elastic element extends the contact duration \(\Delta t\), reducing average impact force (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.7.4).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited position control bandwidth: The introduction of the elastic element makes the joint behave as a spring-mass system, with position loop bandwidth significantly lower than rigid actuators, making it unsuitable for tasks requiring high-stiffness position tracking (e.g., precision assembly).
  2. Limited force control bandwidth as well: Force control bandwidth is constrained by the mechanical resonance frequency of the elastic element; excessively high spring stiffness reduces force signal resolution, while excessively low stiffness limits dynamic response.
  3. Elastic element fatigue and aging: Metal springs have fatigue life limits, and rubber elastic elements age with temperature and time, requiring periodic replacement.
  4. Energy storage efficiency is not free: The spring's energy storage-release process involves hysteresis losses, and the added structural weight may offset some of the energy-saving benefits.
  5. Increased control complexity: The controller must simultaneously handle elastic element dynamics, motor dynamics, and load dynamics, making controller design more complex than for rigid actuators.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "SEA is just adding a spring"—Wrong. Spring stiffness selection, placement, and coupling design with control are systematic engineering challenges; arbitrarily adding a spring can lead to oscillation or force control failure.
  2. "SEA force control accuracy is always higher than QDD"—Not necessarily. SEA's force control accuracy depends on displacement sensor resolution and spring stiffness; if spring stiffness is too high or sensor resolution is insufficient, accuracy may actually be worse than QDD's current estimation.
  3. "SEA is only suitable for low-speed scenarios"—Inaccurate. MIT Cheetah uses SEA for landing impact absorption even during high-speed running; the key lies in the matching design of stiffness and damping.
  4. "SEA and VSA are the same thing"—Different. SEA has fixed stiffness, while VSA (Variable Stiffness Actuator) can adjust \(k\) online and is an extension of SEA (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.10).
