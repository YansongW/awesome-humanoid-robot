Summary

A 70 cm, 4.8 kg child-size bipedal humanoid platform co-developed by Westwood Robotics and UCLA RoMeLa, with 16 degrees of freedom, proprioceptive quasi-direct-drive Koala BEAR actuators with liquid cooling on key joints, and a variable-frequency MPC stack for highly dynamic walking, running and jumping.

Overview

BRUCE (Bipedal Robot Unit with Compliance Enhanced) is a child-sized bipedal humanoid platform jointly developed by Westwood Robotics (founded in 2018 by former core members of UCLA RoMeLa) and UCLA RoMeLa, with the design paper by Liu et al., ICRA 2022. The robot stands 70 cm tall, weighs 4.8 kg, and has 16 degrees of freedom (5 per leg, 3 per arm), making it one of the few small-scale high-dynamic bipedal platforms capable of "running and jumping" (source: research archive bruce-westwood.md, same below). What it truly changes is not that "small robots can walk," but that "child-sized platforms can also support the explosive power and computational capacity required for high-dynamic motion."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

BRUCE is a small bipedal humanoid platform centered on quasi-direct-drive actuators, designed for high-dynamic motion research. Its design goal is not repetitive industrial tasks, but rather to provide a hardware foundation for legged robot research that is "purchasable, runnable, and modifiable." The complete robot is sold by Westwood Robotics through commercial procurement channels, officially positioned as an "open platform," claiming open-source software and models. However, a public repository for the full control framework was not found in GitHub searches (as of 2026-07-01, its license terms could not be directly verified); component-level repositories (PyBEAR, BRUCE_SENSE, Wireless_ESTOP, etc.) are open-sourced under the Westwood-Robotics organization.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before BRUCE, high-dynamic bipedal research faced a dilemma: large platforms (such as Atlas) were extremely costly and complex to maintain, making them unaffordable for ordinary laboratories; small platforms (such as toy-grade servo robots) lacked sufficient torque density and bandwidth to support high-dynamic behaviors like running and jumping. BRUCE's positioning is precisely to fill this gap—using a 70 cm height and 4.8 kg weight to carry 16 degrees of freedom and quasi-direct-drive actuators, lowering the threshold for "high dynamics" from the million-dollar level to the tens-of-thousands-dollar level. Third-party paper comparison tables (ToddlerBot, arXiv:2502.00893 Table I) list its hardware cost at approximately $6.5K; the official page uses an inquiry-based pricing system without publicly listed prices.

Principle Breakdown

① Proprioceptive Quasi-Direct Drive: The Physical Foundation of Force Control

BRUCE is equipped with the self-developed Koala BEAR proprioceptive quasi-direct-drive actuator. The core of the quasi-direct-drive (QDD) architecture is a low gear reduction ratio (typically 3:1–10:1) combined with a high-torque-density motor, allowing external forces to "pass through" directly to the motor side. Therefore, output torque can be estimated simply by measuring motor current, enabling high-bandwidth force control without joint torque sensors. A single Koala BEAR weighs only 250 g with a peak torque of 10.5 N·m (leg joint specification). Its output torque estimation relationship is:

\[ \tau_{\text{out}} \approx G \, k_t \, I_m - J_{\text{eq}} \ddot{\theta}_m \]

where \(G\) is the gear reduction ratio, \(k_t\) is the motor torque constant, \(I_m\) is the motor current, \(J_{\text{eq}}\) is the equivalent inertia, and \(\ddot{\theta}_m\) is the motor angular acceleration. The second term is the inertia compensation term. Due to the low reduction ratio, friction and backlash nonlinearities are also small, making this estimation sufficiently accurate in engineering practice—this is the entire secret behind "force control without torque sensors."

② Liquid Cooling: Thermal Management for Explosive Power

Key joints (such as the knee) employ liquid cooling to support explosive torque and sustained high-dynamic output. High-dynamic motions (jumping, sudden stops) generate significant Joule heating in a short time; if not dissipated promptly, rising motor winding temperature can degrade the torque constant or even cause demagnetization. Liquid cooling removes heat from the joint core, allowing actuators to maintain peak output over longer durations—this is the key thermal management measure that enables BRUCE to "keep running" rather than just "jump once."

③ Variable-Frequency MPC: The Brain of Motion Control

On the software side, BRUCE employs a variable-frequency MPC motion control algorithm supporting high-dynamic behaviors such as walking, running, and jumping. The core idea of MPC (Model Predictive Control) is: at each control cycle, based on the current state and dynamics model, solve a finite-horizon optimal control problem online and execute only the first step. Variable frequency means the controller can dynamically adjust the solving rate based on motion state—increasing frequency during fast running to ensure stability, and decreasing frequency during slow walking to save computational resources. The model library and simulation environment are cited by multiple third-party papers, indicating a certain level of academic influence in its software stack.

These three represent three approaches: BRUCE is a "commercialized high-dynamic platform," ToddlerBot is a "minimalist open-source replication," and Bolt is an "academic open-source foundation." BRUCE's differentiation lies in being the only one of the three that explicitly supports high-dynamic behaviors such as running and jumping, offering the complete robot and technical support through commercial channels—at the cost of opaque pricing and limited open-source availability.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

BRUCE's primary users are university legged-robot laboratories and a small number of corporate research departments. Its design paper (Liu et al., ICRA 2022) is cited by multiple third-party papers, and its model library and simulation environment are also used for comparative research. Since the official positioning is an open platform and component-level repositories (PyBEAR, BRUCE_SENSE, Wireless_ESTOP, etc.) are open-sourced under the Westwood-Robotics GitHub organization, researchers can conduct secondary development based on its actuator and sensing solutions. The specific list of deployment institutions requires independent verification.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited open-source availability: The public repository for the full control framework was not found in GitHub searches (as of 2026-07-01, its license terms could not be directly verified), contrasting with ToddlerBot's fully open-source MIT License stack;
  2. Opaque pricing: The official channel uses inquiry-based pricing without publicly listed prices; third-party papers list approximately $6.5K—actual procurement costs may vary depending on configuration and support services;
  3. Short battery life: 3000 mAh battery, approximately 20 minutes of continuous dynamic operation, requiring multiple batteries for extended experiments;
  4. Low beginner friendliness: Rated 2 / 5 in the research archive, requiring some experience in robot control and debugging;
  5. Limited compute: Main controller at 6 TOPS, though supporting mainstream deep learning frameworks, may be insufficient for running large-scale RL policies or vision models.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "BRUCE is an open-source robot"—inaccurate. The official positioning is an "open platform," with component-level repositories open-sourced, but no public repository for the full control framework was found, and license terms could not be verified. It is closer to "semi-open": hardware purchasable, some software viewable, core framework opaque.
  2. "Quasi-direct-drive = direct drive"—wrong. The Koala BEAR has a gear reducer (specific ratio requires independent verification), just with a low enough ratio to maintain force transparency. Without the reducer, the motor torque would be insufficient to support jumping motions for a 70 cm body.
  3. "Liquid cooling is just a gimmick"—engineering judgment: for sustained high-dynamic output, thermal management is a real bottleneck. Liquid cooling does not increase peak torque; it extends the duration of peak torque.
  4. "It can be purchased for $6.5K"—this figure comes from a third-party paper comparison table (ToddlerBot, arXiv:2502.00893 Table I); the official channel uses inquiry-based pricing, and the actual price may be higher.
