Summary

A scalar, decomposable benchmarking metric that aggregates workspace coverage, torque-power requirements, bandwidth, efficiency, and thermal sustainability to quantify how close a humanoid robot's actuation is to human-level performance.

Sources

  • Human-Level Actuation for Humanoids

Overview

Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS) is a scalar, decomposable benchmark metric that transforms vague claims about a humanoid robot's actuation capabilities being "close to human-level" into a quantifiable, auditable, and reproducible evaluation system. It uses a 75 kg, 1.75 m adult male as the reference human body, requires the robot to simultaneously satisfy human torque and power demands at the same joint angle and angular velocity points via the Human Equivalence Envelope (HEE), and aggregates six physical factors to output a scalar score from 0 to 1. What it truly changes is not "measuring who is stronger," but rather providing, for the first time, a common language for cross-laboratory and cross-platform actuation capability comparisons.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

HLAS is an evaluation framework that turns "human-like actuation" from rhetoric into science. It decomposes actuation system performance into six physically measurable factors: ROM Coverage (the completeness of joint range of motion relative to human joints), DoF Sufficiency (whether the number and configuration of degrees of freedom are sufficient to reproduce human motion), Human Equivalence Envelope (HEE) Coverage (the ability to simultaneously satisfy human torque and power demands at task-relevant postures and rates), Torque-Mode Bandwidth (the actuator's dynamic response capability), Efficiency (mechanical and electrical energy conversion efficiency), and Thermal Sustainability (the ability to sustain output without overheating). These six factors are weighted and aggregated into a scalar score from 0 to 1, with the reference human body itself normalized to 1.0.

What makes HLAS unique is its "decomposability": it is not a black-box score but comes with a four-level diagnostic structure—from joint-task scores, joint scores, task scores, to the whole-body total score—allowing engineers to pinpoint exactly which joint, which task, or which physical factor is lagging. This design makes HLAS both an acceptance criterion and a design tool.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The humanoid robotics field has long suffered from "metric gaming": manufacturers claim "human-level" performance using peak torque at unspecified postures, no-load speed, and similar specifications, but these numbers do not reflect whether a joint can simultaneously provide the correct combination of torque, power, and endurance at task-relevant operating points. Peak torque specifications are especially misleading—they typically correspond to brief bursts of <500 ms, are thermodynamically unsustainable over minute-scale tasks, and obscure the fundamental trade-off between torque density and bandwidth.

What HLAS truly changes is the "grammar" of evaluation: it no longer asks "how much force can your joint produce?" but rather "at the (q, ω) operating points where humans actually perform positive work, can your joint simultaneously satisfy human torque and power demands and sustain them long enough?" Through the HEE's simultaneity condition, power-weighted sampling, and continuous safe measurement protocols, the framework structurally closes the cheating path of "taking high torque at low speed, high power at high speed, and then averaging."

From a historical perspective, HLAS belongs to the tool category of benchmarks, consistent with the trend in robotics from "component specification sheets" toward "task-oriented metrics." It does not replace motor selection calculations but provides a yardstick for "how far from human-level" the selection results are.

Principle Breakdown

① Human Equivalence Envelope (HEE): The Core of the Simultaneity Condition

HEE is the cornerstone of HLAS. It requires the robot to simultaneously satisfy the human torque demand τ_human(q, ω) and power demand P_human(q, ω) at the same joint angle q and angular velocity ω. This differs from traditional specification sheets that separately list peak torque and peak power—the latter allows misaligned sampling of "high torque at low speed, high power at high speed," whereas HEE enforces a "simultaneity" check at every operating point.

Joint power is defined as:

\[ P = \tau \, \omega \]

where τ is joint torque and ω is angular velocity. HEE coverage is calculated by, over the set of task-relevant (q, ω) operating points for humans, counting the proportion where the robot's actuation system can simultaneously satisfy both torque and power demands, with power magnitude as the sampling weight—that is, "operating points where humans exert more power receive higher weight."

② Six-Factor Aggregation: From Physical Quantities to a Scalar

HLAS aggregation uses a three-level structure. The first level is the joint-task score:

\[ s_{j,t} = \alpha^\top x_{j,t} \]

where the feature vector x_{j,t} = [ρ^ROM, d^DoF, h^(w), b^τ, η, θ^th] corresponds to ROM coverage, DoF sufficiency, power-weighted HEE coverage, torque-mode bandwidth, efficiency, and thermal sustainability, respectively, and α is the weight vector. The second level aggregates scores for the same joint across different tasks into a joint score, and the third level aggregates all joint scores with weights into the whole-body total score. This structure allows HLAS to provide both a quick judgment like "total score 0.73" and drill-down diagnostic information like "the left ankle joint's HEE coverage in the stair-climbing task is only 0.4" (all example values, engineering judgment).

③ Mapping to Actuator Physics

The six HLAS factors are not arbitrarily defined but directly correspond to core physical quantities in actuator design. Torque-mode bandwidth is limited by reflected inertia—the smaller the reflected inertia, the easier it is for the motor to accelerate the load, and the higher the bandwidth; however, increasing the reduction ratio N lowers reflected inertia but reduces output speed, so there is an optimal reduction ratio trade-off. Efficiency is directly related to backdrivability: backdrivability can be approximately measured by efficiency η, where higher efficiency means lower reverse-driving friction. Thermal sustainability is determined by continuous torque—peak torque is limited by magnetic saturation and current limits, typically reaching 2–4 times continuous torque but only for a few seconds, while continuous torque is determined by thermal constraints and requires verifying winding temperature rise under worst-case conditions.

Horizontal Comparison

HLAS contrasts sharply with traditional actuator selection methods. Traditional methods center on discrete specifications such as peak torque, no-load speed, and reduction ratio, focusing on "whether the component can do it"; HLAS centers on simultaneity satisfaction at task operating points, focusing on "whether the system can sustain it." The former is "specification sheet thinking," the latter is "task envelope thinking."

Compared with the Human Equivalence Envelope (HEE), also used to evaluate actuation performance, HLAS is a superset of HEE—HEE is one of HLAS's six factors, but HLAS additionally incorporates bandwidth, efficiency, and thermal sustainability, extending evaluation from "instantaneous capability" to "sustained capability." Compared with specific technical solutions like SEA (Series Elastic Actuators), HLAS is an evaluation framework rather than an implementation approach; it does not care what reducer or sensor you use, only the final performance at task points.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Typical application scenarios for HLAS include:

  • Cross-platform comparison: Humanoid robots from different laboratories and manufacturers are compared on actuation capability under a unified scale, eliminating "metric gaming."
  • Design iteration: During the joint module design phase, HLAS's decomposition structure is used to locate bottlenecks—whether bandwidth is insufficient or thermal sustainability is lacking—thereby guiding the selection of motors, reducers, and cooling solutions.
  • Task feasibility prediction: Before deployment, assessing whether the robot can complete specific tasks (e.g., stair climbing, heavy lifting) to avoid the embarrassment of "specifications met but task failed."

In engineering practice, HLAS complements the actuator selection process: first determine peak torque and peak speed based on task requirements, then work backward to select the motor and reducer; continuous torque is determined by thermal constraints and requires verifying winding temperature rise under worst-case conditions; the reduction ratio affects reflected inertia, backdrivability, and output speed, requiring comprehensive optimization. HLAS provides a "human-likeness" acceptance yardstick for the output of this process.

Limitations and Boundaries

The first limitation of HLAS is the singularity of its reference baseline: using a 75 kg, 1.75 m adult male as the baseline means it measures "how close to this specific human body," not "how close to the diversity of the human species." Humans of different body types, genders, and ages differ significantly in torque and power envelopes, and HLAS's single baseline may introduce bias in evaluations of robots intended for special populations (e.g., children, the elderly) (engineering judgment).

Second, the weights α for HLAS's six factors require human setting, and different weight configurations change the final score, introducing subjectivity. Although the framework provides a four-level diagnostic structure to mitigate this issue, the reasonableness of the weights themselves still requires domain consensus.

Third, HLAS focuses on actuation capability and does not address perception, planning, control, and other factors that equally affect task completion. A robot with a high HLAS score may still perform poorly due to poor control algorithms—HLAS measures "hardware potential" rather than "system intelligence."

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A high HLAS score means a good robot"—Wrong. HLAS only measures actuation capability, not perception, planning, control, or software ecosystem. A robot with HLAS 0.9 may stumble even while walking due to poor control algorithms.

  2. "Larger peak torque means higher HLAS"—Wrong. HLAS's HEE factor requires simultaneously satisfying torque and power at task operating points; peak torque only represents brief bursts of <500 ms and is thermodynamically unsustainable over minute-scale tasks. An actuator with large peak torque but small continuous torque will have its thermal sustainability factor significantly drag down the total score.

  3. "HLAS can replace actuator selection calculations"—Wrong. HLAS is an acceptance and comparison tool, not a design tool. Selection still requires calculating parameters such as peak torque, reduction ratio, and reflected inertia based on task requirements; HLAS merely provides a yardstick for "how far from human-level" the selection results are.

  4. "HLAS 1.0 is an absolute upper limit"—Wrong. 1.0 is only the normalized value of the reference human body and does not represent a physical upper limit. Certain actuator technologies (e.g., hydraulic) may exceed human capability at specific operating points and achieve scores >1.0 (engineering judgment).
