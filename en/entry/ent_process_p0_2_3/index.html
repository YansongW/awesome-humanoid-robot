Overview

Requirements Management Tools and Traceability Chains is one of the core work packages in the project initiation and commercial baseline phase (P0), numbered P0.2.3. It establishes complete traceability relationships from upstream inputs to design, testing, and change management through tools such as DOORS / Jama / Valispace / self-developed requirements matrices, ensuring that all designs, tests, and changes can be traced back to requirements, thereby avoiding scope creep. What it truly changes is not the tool selection of "which software to record requirements in," but the engineering accountability chain of "why change, and who is affected by the change."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Requirements Management Tools and Traceability Chains belongs to the method type, with domains including application and market, policy, regulation, and ethics, positioned at the verification and market layer of the value chain. Among the three parallel work packages in the P0 phase, it serves as the bridge function of "translating commercial intent into verifiable engineering constraints"—connecting upstream to commercial and strategic justification (P0.1), and providing the requirements baseline and traceability skeleton downstream for requirements definition and system solutions (P1).

The methods / tools for this work package are explicitly defined as DOORS / Jama / Valispace / self-developed requirements matrices. Among these, DOORS is the classic requirements management platform in traditional heavy industry (aerospace, automotive), emphasizing strict baseline control; Jama leans toward collaborative requirements review for agile teams; Valispace targets complex systems engineers by binding requirements to parametric models; and self-developed requirements matrices suit small and medium-sized teams that are cost-sensitive and require deeply customized traceability logic.

The core proposition of the design thinking logic is: all designs, tests, and changes can be traced back to requirements, avoiding scope creep. This means the traceability chain is not a "relationship diagram" drawn after the fact, but rather, from the moment a requirement item is created, it establishes four elements—ID, status, Owner, and acceptance criteria—and mandates that these IDs be referenced in every subsequent design decision, test report, and change request.

There are three key constraints: tool cost, team learning curve, and PLM integration. Tool cost includes license fees, server deployment, and custom development investment; the team learning curve determines the time window from "tool deployment" to "actual team adoption"; and PLM integration determines whether requirement changes can automatically synchronize to BOM, CAD models, and process documentation.

The completion criteria / deliverables are: requirements baseline established, with complete ID/status/Owner/acceptance criteria. A "baseline" refers to a set of requirements formally reviewed and approved at a specific point in time, after which any changes must go through a controlled process—it is the anchor point of the traceability chain.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before dedicated requirements management tools for humanoid robots existed, teams typically maintained requirements lists using Excel or Wiki. This approach was barely workable when the number of requirements was fewer than 100 and the team had fewer than 10 people, but once full-machine development began, problems immediately surfaced: there were no links between requirement items and design documents, the requirement versions referenced by test cases could not be confirmed, and the impact scope of requirement changes could not be assessed—a change to the torque specification of a robotic arm joint might affect motor selection, reducer selection, structural strength simulation, control algorithm parameters, and test bench design, yet no one could quickly produce a complete list.

This is precisely the historical positioning of Requirements Management Tools and Traceability Chains: it is the engineering management infrastructure for transitioning from "document-driven" to "data-driven" approaches. Establishing the traceability chain in the P0 phase means that subsequent requirements definition in P1, system solutions in P2, and even test verification in P10 can all operate within the same traceability framework. What it truly changes is not the intuitive judgment of "whether to do it," but the quantifiable decision framework of "why do it, to what extent, and at what cost"—this statement applies equally to the traceability chain itself: without it, requirement changes are a gamble.

Principle Breakdown

① The Mathematical Essence of the Traceability Chain: Directed Graph

A requirements traceability chain is mathematically a directed graph, where nodes are requirement items, design elements, test cases, and change requests, and edges are trace links. There are three fundamental types of traceability relationships:

  • Forward Traceability: Points from requirements to design and testing, answering "who implements this requirement and who verifies it."
  • Backward Traceability: Points from design and testing back to requirements, answering "which requirement does this design/test satisfy."
  • Horizontal Traceability: Dependency, conflict, and derivation relationships among requirements.

A complete traceability chain can be represented as a matrix. Let the requirement set be R = {r₁, r₂, ..., rₘ} and the test case set be T = {t₁, t₂, ..., tₙ}; then the traceability matrix M is an m × n 0-1 matrix, where:

\[ M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if test } t_j \text{ verifies requirement } r_i \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Coverage calculation is the most core quantitative metric of the traceability chain. Requirements coverage is defined as the proportion of requirements verified by at least one test case:

\[ \text{Coverage} = \frac{|\{r_i : \sum_{j=1}^{n} M_{ij} \geq 1\}|}{m} \times 100\% \]

In engineering practice, coverage for critical requirements (e.g., safety-related requirements) must reach 100%, while general requirements should be no lower than 90% (engineering judgment). Although this formula is simple, it is the fundamental reason the traceability chain exists—it transforms "whether requirements are verified" from a vague feeling into an auditable number.

② Change Impact Analysis: The Real-Time Value of the Traceability Chain

When a requirement is modified, the value of the traceability chain immediately becomes apparent. Let requirement rᵢ be modified; then the set of affected test cases is:

\[ T_{\text{affected}} = \{t_j : M_{ij} = 1\} \]

Additionally, through the traceability relationships between design elements and requirements, the affected subsystems, components, and interfaces can be further derived. This process is called "Impact Analysis" in DOORS, "Change Impact" in Jama, and is typically implemented via SQL queries in self-developed matrices. Without a traceability chain, this analysis requires manually reviewing documents, taking days; with a traceability chain, it takes seconds.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key parameters of Requirements Management Tools and Traceability Chains are not hardware metrics but engineering management metrics. The following parameters are explicitly required in the completion criteria of P0.2.3:

Parameter Specification Description
Requirement item ID Globally unique, version-immutable Format recommended as REQ-<module>-<sequence>, e.g., REQ-MECH-001
Requirement status At least include Draft / Reviewed / Approved / Obsolete Status transitions must go through the review process
Requirement Owner Each requirement must have a single responsible person Owner is accountable for the correctness of and changes to the requirement
Acceptance criteria Each requirement must have verifiable acceptance criteria Non-verifiable requirements should be rejected or split
Traceability coverage 100% for critical requirements, ≥ 90% for general requirements (engineering judgment) Calculated from the traceability matrix
Number of candidate solutions No fewer than 2 Hard requirement of P0.2.3.2

Horizontal Comparison

The four solution categories—DOORS, Jama, Valispace, and self-developed requirements matrices—each have their applicable scenarios:

Dimension DOORS Jama Valispace Self-developed requirements matrix
Typical users Aerospace, automotive, defense Medical, consumer electronics, agile teams Satellites, robotics, complex systems Small and medium teams, limited budgets
Traceability capability Strongest, supports complex matrices Strong, supports cross-project traceability Medium, parameter-level traceability Depends on implementation
Learning curve Steep, requires professional training Gentle, user-friendly interface Moderate, requires understanding of parametric thinking Depends on the team
PLM integration Mature, with standard interfaces Medium, requires customization Medium, API-friendly Requires in-house development
License cost High Medium Medium-high Low (development cost only)

Engineering judgment: For a humanoid robot—a product deeply coupling mechanics, electronics, software, and AI—DOORS' strict baseline control is the most valuable, but its high cost and steep learning curve may slow down a startup team's pace; Valispace's parametric traceability naturally aligns with the "parameter-driven design" philosophy of humanoid robots and deserves key evaluation; a self-developed requirements matrix is suitable as a transitional solution, but long-term maintenance costs may exceed those of commercial tools.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

P0.2.3.1 Input Review and Target Quantification: List all upstream input items and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task Owners, timelines, and risk registers. Upstream inputs include the business canvas, target cost, target production volume, target market, and regulatory lists (e.g., ISO 13482 service robot safety standard—verify the latest version number independently).

P0.2.3.2 Solution/Method Design: Develop no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, establish an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize reviews, and freeze the solution. Typical dimensions of the evaluation matrix include: traceability capability (weight 30%), PLM integration difficulty (weight 25%), team learning cost (weight 20%), license cost (weight 15%), and scalability (weight 10%) (weight allocation is engineering judgment).

P0.2.3.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulations or prototype verification, and document anomalies and deviations. For requirements management tools, this step typically means setting up the tool environment, importing historical requirements, configuring traceability templates, and training key users.

P0.2.3.4 Verification and Issue Closure: Develop test/review plans and pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output issue lists and improvement measures. The core of verification is checking whether traceability coverage meets targets and whether requirement IDs/status/Owner/acceptance criteria are complete.

P0.2.3.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Write documents according to templates and reference raw data, complete internal reviews and version control, and publish and notify downstream dependents. Deliverables include the requirements baseline document, traceability matrix report, and tool usage specifications, while updating ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chains.

Limitations and Boundaries

The limitations of Requirements Management Tools and Traceability Chains first lie in the fact that tools are not management. Deploying DOORS or Jama is only the first step; if the team does not establish the engineering discipline that "requirement changes must go through the traceability chain," the tool will ultimately become an expensive document repository. Second, the maintenance cost of the traceability chain increases with the number of requirements—once requirement items exceed 1,000, maintaining traceability relationships itself requires dedicated personnel (engineering judgment).

Another boundary is that the traceability chain cannot solve the quality problem of requirements themselves. If upstream input requirements are vague and non-verifiable, the traceability chain can only guarantee that "every requirement has test coverage," but it cannot guarantee that "the tests verify the right thing." This is precisely why P0.2.3.1 emphasizes "converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs"—the starting point of the traceability chain must be verifiable requirements.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The traceability chain is a testing-phase matter"—Wrong. The traceability chain must have its skeleton established in the P0 phase; otherwise, traceability relationships generated during subsequent design, testing, and changes will require extensive rework to retrofit, at a cost far higher than establishing them from the start.
  2. "DOORS is the most expensive, so it's the best"—Wrong. Tool selection must match team size and product complexity. For a humanoid robot startup team, Valispace or a self-developed matrix may be more suitable than DOORS.
  3. "100% traceability coverage is perfection"—Wrong. Coverage only answers "is there a test," not "how good is the test quality." A requirement covered by 10 low-quality tests is worse than one verified by a single high-quality test.
  4. "Once the requirements baseline is established, it cannot be changed"—Wrong. The baseline is the starting point for changes, not the end. After the baseline is established, requirement changes must go through a controlled process (submit change request → impact analysis → review → approval → update baseline), which is precisely the core value of the traceability chain.
