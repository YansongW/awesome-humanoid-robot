Summary

Learning to Walk in Costume: Adversarial Motion Priors for Aesthetically Constrained Humanoids is a 2025 work on locomotion for humanoid robots.

Sources

  • Learning to Walk in Costume: Adversarial Motion Priors for Aesthetically Constrained Humanoids (arXiv)

Overview

This paper addresses the motion control challenges of aesthetically driven humanoid robots (such as the self-developed Cosmo, whose head accounts for 16% of total mass and lacks vision) by proposing a reinforcement learning framework based on Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP). It combines CMU motion capture data with model-generated references to achieve stable standing and walking in both simulation and physical hardware. The core contribution lies in demonstrating that under extreme mass distribution and motion constraints, balancing aesthetics and functional performance is possible through discriminator-based style rewards and curriculum learning.

What It Changes

Entertainment humanoid robots have long been treated as "jointed sculptures." Aesthetics-first designs (oversized heads, protective shells, limited sensing) directly violate the fundamental assumptions of traditional control methods regarding mass distribution and dynamic identifiability. Previously, one either sacrificed appearance for control feasibility or accepted the limitations of static display. What this paper truly changes is transforming "aesthetic constraints" from being adversarial to control problems into learnable distribution conditions, enabling robots with "heavy heads" and "blind eyes" to walk with natural gaits. This is not merely algorithm transplantation but a redefinition of the humanoid robot design space—where appearance freedom is no longer unilaterally constrained by dynamic feasibility.

Method Breakdown

Overall Framework

The AMP (Adversarial Motion Priors) paradigm is adopted, embedding imitation learning into reinforcement learning: a policy network generates actions, a discriminator network distinguishes policy state transitions from reference motion data, and its output is added as a style reward on top of the task reward.

Reference Data Construction

  • Motion Retargeting: In Blender, the Rokoko plugin maps CMU Mocap dataset motions (e.g., "swaggy" walking) onto Cosmo's custom skeleton, ignoring shell collisions.
  • Model Supplementation: A whole-body controller based on hierarchical quadratic programming inverse dynamics generates experimental data, mixed with motion capture data to form a natural curriculum (typical speeds of 0.5–0.7 m/s).

Network and Observations

  • Policy Network: 3 layers (512, 256, 128) with ELU activation; critic and discriminator each have 2 layers (256, 128).
  • Observation Space: Base linear velocity, angular velocity, normalized joint positions, joint velocities, projected gravity, previous actions, base height, command signals; base kinematics estimated via an invariant extended Kalman filter.
  • Action Space: Target joint positions, converted to torques via a PD controller at a higher frequency than the policy.

Reward Structure

Divided into four groups: imitation (AMP reward), motion quality (joint velocities), safety (foot tripping, foot orientation, foot height), and task (linear velocity, angular velocity), supporting curriculum learning.

Domain Randomization

Physical parameters: friction coefficient [0.2, 1.1], base mass ±1.5 kg, PD gains [0.75, 1.13], actuator delay of 4 time steps; external disturbances: push interval 4.0 s, linear push 0.5 m/s, angular push 0.2 rad/s.

Training Pipeline

Stability analysis and self-collision detection are first performed in Isaac Sim, followed by large-scale parallel training in Isaac Gym; push-recovery/balance policies are developed first, then walking policies.

Key Innovations

  1. Explicit Modeling of Aesthetic Constraints: For the first time, entertainment-robot-specific constraints such as "head accounting for 16% of total mass" and "no vision" are treated as core design inputs rather than after-the-fact compensations. AMP fuses motion capture style with model-generated references, enabling the policy to maintain natural gaits under extreme center-of-mass conditions.
  2. Dual-Reference Curriculum Mechanism: Motion capture data provides natural human gait style, while model-generated data provides robot dynamic feasibility; the mixture forms an easy-to-hard curriculum, solving the problem that a single data source is undeployable under extreme mass distributions.
  3. Explicit Encoding of Safety Rewards: Foot tripping, foot orientation, and foot height are treated as independent reward groups. Ablation studies demonstrate that AMP style rewards alone are insufficient to constrain foot trajectories, necessitating explicit safety terms.

Static Stability Analysis

Four poses were tested in Isaac Sim, with only 2 stable within joint motion ranges; Cosmo's center-of-mass deviation is 30 mm, while ARTEMIS is 6 mm (a fivefold difference).

Hardware deployment: the balance policy maintains head stability under disturbances up to 0.15 m/s, with the system recovering within 2 seconds; the walking policy reaches peak x-direction velocities of 0.4 m/s, with joint torques remaining within the safe range of ±20 Nm.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Retargeting does not account for Cosmo's wide shell, leading to mesh interpenetration and unmodeled discrepancies between simulation and hardware.
  • Initial motion capture data (e.g., "swaggy" walking) lacks robot-specific dynamic information, providing only fixed velocities insufficient for safe deployment.
  • Ablations show that removing standing pose references leads to dangerous vertical motions (near-jumping), removing model-based references results in ineffective high-frequency gaits, and rewards alone are insufficient for correct foot trajectories.
  • Visual perception is not addressed; the system relies entirely on proprioception. The paper does not specify performance on more complex terrains (e.g., slopes, stairs).

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, first verify three points: first, the reference data mixing ratio—the proportion of motion capture to model-generated data directly affects gait naturalness and safety, and starting with reward weights [0.35, 0.35, 0.4] is recommended; second, the domain randomization range—the friction coefficient lower bound of 0.2 and actuator delay of 4 time steps are critical for sim-to-real transfer and should not be relaxed arbitrarily; third, explicit encoding of safety rewards—ablations prove that AMP style rewards alone lead to uncontrolled foot trajectories, so the three safety terms (foot tripping, orientation, height) must be retained. The most common pitfall is ignoring shell collisions during retargeting, causing mesh interpenetration in simulation and the policy learning non-physical behaviors; it is recommended to perform static stability analysis in Isaac Sim first (only 2/4 poses are stable) before training. During hardware deployment, note that the PD controller frequency must exceed the policy frequency, and the balance policy is sensitive to head mass variations (3.2 kg optimal); re-evaluation is required after changing head components.
