Overview

Product liability refers to the legal responsibility of manufacturers and sellers for personal injury or property damage caused by defective or unsafe products, and it directly applies to humanoid robots. Traditional product liability law distinguishes among manufacturing defects, design defects, and warning defects, but the learning-based behavior and continuous evolution characteristics of humanoid robots pose a systematic challenge to this framework. What truly changes is not the rule of "who pays," but the underlying logic of "when, by whom, and with what evidence a defect is defined."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Product liability is a system of strict liability in tort law, whose core proposition is: as long as a product is defective and causes harm, the manufacturer must bear compensation liability regardless of whether subjective fault exists. In the context of humanoid robots, the liable parties cover manufacturers and sellers, and the scope of liability covers personal injury and property damage.

Traditional product liability law distinguishes three types of defects:

  • Manufacturing defects: Individual products deviate from design specifications (e.g., a robot's torque sensor is uncalibrated);
  • Design defects: The design itself is unreasonable (e.g., the knee joint lacks overheating protection);
  • Warning defects: Failure to adequately communicate foreseeable risks (e.g., failing to warn "do not operate on wet surfaces").

This three-way classification constitutes the classic framework for product liability analysis and serves as the starting point for determining liability for humanoid robots.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The historical role of the product liability system lies in addressing the "information and bargaining power asymmetry between consumers and manufacturers" in industrial society. Consumers cannot inspect internal product designs or assess the compliance of production processes, so the law allocates risk to the party best able to control it—the manufacturer.

Before the advent of humanoid robots, product liability primarily targeted static mechanical and electronic products that were finalized at the factory. Humanoid robots break this premise: they possess physical force application capabilities, mobility, and increasingly autonomous decision-making abilities, and they continuously alter their own behavior through OTA updates. This introduces two new challenges:

First, the imputability of learning-based behavior—if harm results from a policy network's generalization failure outside its training distribution, does the defect lie in the data, the algorithm, or the deployer? Second, the timing of liability for continuously evolving products—when behavior after an OTA update deviates from the factory state, does liability rest with the manufacturer or the update approver?

These two issues directly challenge the "defect" definitional foundation of traditional product liability law, shifting liability determination from "whether the product was safe at the factory" to "whether the product remains safe throughout its entire lifecycle."

Principle Breakdown

The imputation logic of product liability can be formalized as a liability determination chain:

\[ \text{Liability} = f(\text{Defect Type}, \text{Harm Facts}, \text{Causal Relationship}, \text{Liability Timing}) \]

The defect type determines the difficulty of proof, and the liability timing determines the liable party. In the humanoid robot scenario, every link in this chain faces new uncertainty:

① Expansion of defect determination dimensions

In traditional product liability, defects are static, testable physical attributes. Humanoid robots introduce a "behavioral defect" dimension—does a policy network's generalization failure outside its training distribution constitute a design defect? This requires defect determination to expand from "product state inspection" to "algorithmic behavior auditing."

② Reconstruction of the evidence chain for causation

In traditional product liability, causation is typically established through physical traces (e.g., fractured components, burned circuits). Harm events involving humanoid robots may instead originate from sequences of software decisions, with evidence existing in the form of logs, model weights, and training data. Demonstrating causation requires new technical means.

③ Dynamization of liability timing

OTA updates cause products to continuously change behavior after sale. Liability timing expands from "the moment of manufacture" to "each update moment," and the liable party may shift between the manufacturer and the update approver. Currently, jurisdictions generally apply existing product liability and consumer protection frameworks, while liability legislation specifically targeting autonomous systems is still evolving.

Horizontal Comparison

Product liability is not an isolated institution; together with adjacent legal frameworks, it constitutes the liability governance system for humanoid robots:

Relationship with functional safety standards: Functional safety standards such as ISO 13849 and IEC 61508 define design requirements for "safety-related control systems," while product liability law determines the legal consequences of violating these requirements. Standards are "ex ante" engineering constraints; product liability is "ex post" legal remedy. Together they form a closed loop of "prevention—accountability."

Relationship with market access certification: Regional access certifications such as CE, UL, FCC, and CCC serve as "passports" for products entering the market. Their compliance arguments (e.g., safety architectures demonstrated per SIL/PL) can serve as evidence of "reasonable care exercised" in product liability litigation. Certification is not a shield against liability, but it can significantly reduce liability risk.

Relationship with insurance mechanisms: Product liability insurance is a financial transfer tool for liability risk. Currently viable paths include incorporating robots into additional clauses of corporate property insurance and public liability insurance, special-purpose product liability insurance for pilot projects, and government-guided first-set (set) major equipment insurance compensation mechanisms. The obstacle to precise pricing lies in the lack of accident statistics—only through large-scale deployment accumulating failure data can insurance pricing move from "conservative refusal" to "actuarial insurability."

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The application of the product liability framework to humanoid robots remains in a stage of "continuing to use old frameworks while exploring new rules":

U.S. path: There is no unified federal robotics law; safety is achieved indirectly through the OSHA occupational safety framework, product liability litigation, and industry standards (e.g., UL certification). State-level licensing and accident reporting systems in the autonomous driving sector are regarded as precedents for robot regulation. Product liability litigation is the primary channel for consumer rights enforcement in the U.S. and the most direct source of legal risk for manufacturers.

EU path: The EU has long discussed adapting the product liability framework to AI and robotics, with the core disputes centering on the burden of proof for defects and the continuation of liability after software updates. The EU AI Act imposes differentiated obligations on AI systems by risk level; humanoid robots used in law enforcement, critical infrastructure, employment screening, and similar scenarios may be classified as high-risk. The Machinery Regulation brings "machines with self-evolving behavior" into view, requiring manufacturers to conduct risk assessments and attach declarations of conformity.

Corporate practice: Under the RaaS (Robot-as-a-Service) model, the liability chain becomes further complicated—the allocation of liability among manufacturers, operators, software suppliers, and customers must be clarified through contracts. Agility Digit's warehouse deployment adopts the RaaS model, and the liability clause design in its contract structure serves as an industry reference sample.

Limitations and Boundaries

The product liability framework has clear applicability boundaries in the humanoid robot domain:

First, the imputation dilemma of learning-based behavior. If harm results from a policy network's generalization failure outside its training distribution, does the defect lie in the data, the algorithm, or the deployer? The current legal framework struggles to provide a clear answer. This is not a deficiency of legal provisions but rather that the concept of "defect" itself must expand from "physical state" to "behavioral distribution characteristics."

Second, the dynamization challenge of liability timing. When behavior after an OTA update deviates from the factory state, does liability rest with the manufacturer or the update approver? This involves liability allocation across the software supply chain. Currently, jurisdictions generally apply existing product liability and consumer protection frameworks, while liability legislation specifically targeting autonomous systems is still evolving.

Third, the data bottleneck in insurance pricing. The obstacle to precise pricing lies in the lack of accident statistics—this circles back to the data flywheel and fleet telemetry: only through large-scale deployment accumulating failure data can insurance pricing move from "conservative refusal" to "actuarial insurability."

Fourth, gaps in standards coverage. The current standards system (ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13482, ISO 13849, IEC 61508 combined with regional access certifications CE, UL, FCC, CCC) still has blank spots regarding bipedal dynamic safety, learning-based component validation, and continuously updated systems.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Product liability insurance can replace product liability"—Wrong. Insurance is merely a financial transfer tool for risk; it does not change the legal liability itself. If the liability chain is unclear, insurance cannot be priced; if insurance cannot be priced, marketization cannot advance. The two are complementary, not substitutive.

  2. "Passing CE/UL certification provides immunity"—Wrong. Certification is a market access threshold and can serve as evidence of "reasonable care exercised," but in product liability litigation, one must still prove that the product was defect-free in the specific use scenario. Certification is not a shield against liability.

  3. "Product liability concerns only the legal department"—Wrong. Product liability directly shapes engineering decisions: safety architectures must be demonstrated per SIL/PL, OTA updates require re-certification processes, and incident reporting mechanisms must be built into product design. Treating governance as an engineering problem is precisely the extension of the "from principles to products" methodology into the social dimension.

  4. "Humanoid robot liability issues can wait until the law is perfected"—Wrong. Policy and ethics are not external constraints that come after technology; they are first-class inputs to humanoid robot system design. Without passing certification tests, products cannot be brought to market; without a clear liability chain, insurance cannot be priced; without proper privacy design, the data flywheel cannot legally spin.
