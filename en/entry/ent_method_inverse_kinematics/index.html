Summary

The computation of joint angles that realize a desired end-effector pose, often solved numerically via Jacobian pseudoinverse or analytically for simple geometries.

Sources

  • Wiki extraction

Overview

Inverse Kinematics (IK) solves the mapping "given a desired end-effector pose, find the joint angles." A 6-DOF manipulator satisfying the Pieper condition (three wrist axes intersecting at a single point) has an analytical closed-form solution, allowing enumeration of all solutions at microsecond scale; 7-DOF redundant arms and general configurations require numerical methods (Jacobian pseudoinverse, damped least squares, LM, etc.). IK is a foundational component for teleoperation, motion retargeting, and task-space control (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.3.5; TRAC-IK, DOI 10.1109/HUMANOIDS.2015.7363472).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Forward kinematics \(x = f(q)\) computes the end-effector pose from joint angles; IK seeks its inverse \(q = f^{-1}(x)\). Unlike forward kinematics, IK typically has no analytical solution and may have multiple solutions or none (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.3.5). Three solution paths:

  1. Analytical solution: Geometric/algebraic methods directly yield closed-form expressions (requires the Pieper condition);
  2. Numerical solution: Iteratively minimizes pose error (Newton-Raphson, Levenberg-Marquardt, Jacobian pseudoinverse);
  3. Learned approximation: Neural network regression (trades accuracy for speed, non-mainstream).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

The natural language for commanding a robot is task space ("move the hand to the cup"), while the robot's execution language is joint space—IK is the translator between these two languages. Without it, teleoperators would have to think directly in terms of 7 joint angles, motion retargeting would be impossible, and WBC task-space objectives could not be realized.

The wrist three-axis intersection condition proven by Pieper in 1968 (the Pieper condition) is a theoretical result with profound industrial impact: it tells manipulator designers "design the wrist axes to intersect at a point, and you get a closed-form solution"—this directly shaped the standard configuration of 6-axis industrial arms in later generations (Wiki §8.3.5). Humanoid robot arms are mostly 7-DOF redundant configurations, where closed-form solutions are unavailable, making the speed, robustness, and null-space utilization of numerical IK the engineering focus. TRAC-IK (2015) turned "robust numerical IK with joint limits" into an open-source standard component (DOI 10.1109/HUMANOIDS.2015.7363472).

Principle Breakdown

① Pieper Condition and Position-Orientation Decoupling

When the three wrist axes intersect at a single point, the wrist intersection position depends only on the first three joints, and the end-effector orientation depends only on the last three joints, decoupling IK into two subproblems. From the end-effector pose, first compute the wrist intersection point \(\mathbf{p}_w = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{d}_w\), then solve the first three joints via geometric methods (intersection of two circles) or algebraic methods (tangent half-angle substitution to reduce to polynomials); finally determine the last three joints via Euler angle decomposition of \({}^3\mathbf{R}_6 = ({}^0\mathbf{R}_3)^T\mathbf{R}\) (Wiki §8.3.5).

② Numerical IK: Error Minimization

The problem is transformed into minimizing the end-effector pose error (Wiki §8.3.5):

\[ \min_{\mathbf{q}} \, \|\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{q})\|^2, \quad \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{q}) = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_{\text{des}} - \mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}) \\ \mathbf{R}_{\text{des}} \ominus \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{q}) \end{bmatrix} \]

The Jacobian pseudoinverse iteration \(q \leftarrow q + J^\dagger e\) is the basic step; Damped Least Squares (DLS) adds a damping factor to the pseudoinverse, sacrificing a small amount of accuracy for numerical stability near singularities; LM adaptively interpolates between gradient descent and Newton's method.

③ Multiple Solutions, Limits, and Redundancy

For redundant arms (n > 6), the same end-effector pose corresponds to infinitely many solutions—this is not a nuisance but a resource: null-space motion can avoid joint limits, avoid obstacles, and optimize joint torques without moving the end-effector (same origin as WBC's null-space projection, see ent_method_hierarchical_qp_wbc). TRAC-IK's engineering contribution lies in making "convergence to a feasible solution within joint limits" a high-success-rate open-source implementation (DOI 10.1109/HUMANOIDS.2015.7363472).

Who Uses It / Application Cases

  • Teleoperation and motion retargeting: An essential step in mapping human hand/body poses to robot joint angles (Wiki Chapter 17 §17.3);
  • Task-space control: WBC/impedance control task objectives are realized at the joint level via IK or differential IK (Wiki §8.4.10);
  • Open-source humanoids: Platforms such as InMoov (ent_robot_system_inmoov), Poppy (ent_robot_system_poppy_humanoid), and ROBOTIS OP3 (ent_robot_system_robotis_op3) all include IK modules in their motion pipelines;
  • Industry standard components: The default IK plugin in the MoveIt/ROS ecosystem is the TRAC-IK family.

Limitations and Boundaries

  • Ill-conditioning near singularities: The pseudoinverse diverges at Jacobian singularities; DLS trades accuracy for stability;
  • Local convergence: Numerical solutions depend on the initial guess and may converge to an undesired branch in the multi-solution landscape;
  • Limits and collisions: Basic IK does not handle self-collision or joint limits; additional constraint layers are needed;
  • Floating-base humanoids: Humanoid IK often must be coupled with center-of-mass/support constraints; single-arm IK is only a subproblem (engineering judgment).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "IK always has a solution"—No solution exists when the pose is unreachable (beyond the workspace); numerical methods then manifest as non-convergence rather than an error.
  2. "7-DOF arms can also have closed-form solutions"—Redundant configurations have no general closed-form solution; the so-called "arm-angle parameterization" is a special case that makes the redundancy explicit.
  3. "Numerical IK is slow"—Modern implementations (TRAC-IK) complete within milliseconds, more than sufficient for 100 Hz teleoperation loops (Wiki §8.3.5 speed characterization).
  4. "IK is only for arms"—Full-body humanoid IK (including center-of-mass and bipedal constraints) is a common intermediate layer for gait and teleoperation.
