Overview

The joint mounting interface and housing design is a critical process node in the structural engineering of humanoid robot bodies, belonging to one of the core tasks in Phase P5 (Body Structural Engineering and Prototyping). It addresses how joint modules are precisely, reliably, and maintainably connected to the robot's structural skeleton, and how protective housings are provided for the joints. What it truly changes is not the shape of individual parts, but the system-level capability of the entire machine in terms of assembly precision, maintenance convenience, and environmental adaptability.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Joint mounting interface and housing design refers to the complete set of design activities in humanoid robot body structural design that address the mechanical connection interfaces between each joint module (typically comprising a motor, reducer, encoder, driver, etc.) and the robot skeleton (central skeleton, limb links), as well as the housing structures enclosing the joints. Its core deliverables include joint interface drawings, housing 3D models, and sealing solutions.

In the WBS, this process is located in Phase P5 Body Structural Engineering and Prototyping, standing alongside Central Skeleton and Limb Link Design (P5.1.1) and Exterior Cover Panels and Seam Design (P5.1.3), together forming the three pillars of robot body structural design. It receives inputs from joint module selection and structural layout upstream, and provides geometric and tolerance basis downstream for structural strength simulation (P8) and mass-production process evaluation (P5.2.3).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In the early stages of humanoid robot development, connections between joints and structures often relied on ad-hoc solutions—bolts screwed directly in, exposed cables, and no sealing protection. Such practices were tolerable in laboratory prototypes, but once productization began, problems surfaced collectively:

  • Coaxiality loss: Misalignment between the joint output shaft and the link rotation center leads to reduced transmission efficiency, bearing eccentric wear, and in severe cases, seizure.
  • Difficult disassembly: Repairing a single hip joint required disassembling half of the lower limb, with maintenance times measured in hours—far from the maintainability expected of industrial products.
  • Environmental sensitivity: Dust and moisture ingress into the joint accelerates bearing and encoder failure, significantly degrading robot reliability in non-clean environments.

Joint mounting interface and housing design emerged precisely to address these systemic issues. It elevates "how to mount a joint onto a robot" from an empirical, ad-hoc operation to an engineering discipline with tolerance analysis, sealing ratings, and repeatability verification. What it truly changes is not the shape of a single part, but the system-level capability of the entire machine in assembly precision, maintenance convenience, and environmental adaptability.

Principle Breakdown

① Interface Tolerance Analysis: Where Precision Comes From

The core contradiction in joint mounting interfaces lies in the fact that both the joint module and the structural skeleton have manufacturing tolerances, and their superposition must still satisfy functional requirements for coaxiality and perpendicularity. The essence of tolerance analysis is establishing a quantitative relationship between part tolerances and assembly functionality.

For rotary joints, the most critical assembly feature is the coaxiality between the output flange and the link mounting surface. Taking the theoretical axis of the joint output shaft as the datum, and the actual positional deviation of the link mounting hole as δ, the post-assembly eccentricity can be expressed as:

\[ \delta_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\delta_{\text{joint}}^2 + \delta_{\text{link}}^2 + \delta_{\text{assembly}}^2} \]

where δ_joint is the joint flange tolerance, δ_link is the link mounting hole positional tolerance, and δ_assembly is the deviation introduced during assembly (e.g., bolt tightening deformation). The task of tolerance design is to reasonably allocate these three budgets so that δ_total falls within the allowable range of the bearings and seals. Engineering judgment: for high-load joints such as humanoid robot hips and knees, the coaxiality budget is typically controlled at the 0.05–0.1 mm level, with specific values to be confirmed based on the selected bearing clearance and sealing type.

② Dowel Pin/Flange Design: The Secret to Repeatable Disassembly

The key to repeatable disassembly lies in separating the functions of "locating" and "clamping." Dowel pins handle precise alignment, while bolts only provide clamping force. This way, even after multiple assembly/disassembly cycles, assembly precision remains consistent as long as the dowel pin holes are not worn.

A typical design features: 2 dowel pin holes (diagonally arranged) and 4–6 bolt clearance holes on the flange face. Dowel pins use interference fit or clearance fit (depending on disassembly frequency), while bolts are selected for specification and torque according to preload requirements. This "pin-centering, bolt-clamping" configuration significantly reduces the sensitivity of assembly precision to operator skill level.

③ Sealing Design: How Protection Ratings Are Achieved

The core of housing sealing is the implementation path for IP protection ratings. For rotary joints, dynamic seals (at the output shaft) and static seals (at housing seams) must be addressed separately. Static seals typically use O-rings or flat gaskets, while dynamic seals require oil seals or lip seals selected based on speed, torque, and service life requirements.

The challenge in sealing design lies in the fact that seals must have sufficient contact pressure to achieve sealing while not generating excessive friction torque that degrades joint efficiency. This contradiction is particularly prominent in scenarios requiring both high protection ratings and high joint transparency.

Horizontal Comparison

Compared with adjacent processes, the unique positioning of joint mounting interface and housing design becomes clearer:

  • Compared with Central Skeleton and Limb Link Design (P5.1.1): P5.1.1 focuses on the skeleton's own stiffness, strength, and topology optimization, addressing "whether the structure is strong enough"; P5.1.2 focuses on the connection interface between joints and the skeleton, addressing "whether it can be mounted, mounted accurately, and disassembled." The former is the mechanical design of beams and shells, while the latter is the design of fits and tolerances.
  • Compared with Exterior Cover Panels and Seam Design (P5.1.3): Exterior cover panels pursue aesthetic form and overall visual integrity, with seam design serving appearance craftsmanship; joint housings place greater emphasis on protective function and maintenance accessibility, with seam locations often determined by service requirements rather than design language.
  • Compared with Structural Strength Simulation (P8): P8 verifies critical cross-sections through finite element analysis, and its mesh and load assumptions must match the wall thicknesses, fillets, and stiffeners achievable by subsequent processes; the output of P5.1.2 is one of the geometric inputs for P8. The two form a "design–verification" upstream-downstream relationship.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

In humanoid robot product development workflows, joint mounting interface and housing design is the必经之路 for every joint module to be integrated into the complete machine. Taking leg joints as an example: the hip joint mounting interface must simultaneously withstand bending and torsional loads, with its flange face typically designed as circular or square, connected to the pelvic skeleton via dowel pins; the knee joint housing must account for the flexion angle range to avoid interference with the thigh link.

In end-effector domains such as dexterous hands, similar interface design logic applies. For instance, open-source dexterous hand designs like the Aero Hand Open feature finger joint-to-palm base connections that equally rely on precise positioning and reliable fastening, with all parts optimized for 3D printing and standard component procurement, reflecting the pursuit of manufacturability and reproducibility in interface design. The CYJ Hand-0's hybrid actuation system integrates linear motors and SMA modules into a compact drive unit mounted on a custom rear support structure, with its structural frame fully 3D-printed from AlSi10Mg metal—this equally depends on precise mounting interface design to ensure alignment and fixation between the drive unit and the skeleton.

Limitations and Boundaries

Joint mounting interface and housing design is not a panacea, and its capability boundaries must be clearly understood:

  • Cannot compensate for defects in the joint module itself: If the output flange of the joint module has insufficient precision, interface design can only provide limited mitigation through adjusted fitting methods, not a fundamental solution.
  • Tolerance analysis depends on the accuracy of input data: If upstream-provided joint dimensions, tolerances, and load data are inaccurate, even the most refined tolerance allocation is built on sand.
  • Sealing design involves performance trade-offs: Higher protection ratings often come with increased friction and degraded heat dissipation; in scenarios requiring high joint transparency (e.g., force control), the choice of sealing rating requires careful trade-off.
  • Housing design is constrained by process limitations: Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM) and mass-production process evaluation (P5.2.3) impose reverse constraints on housing wall thickness, fillets, and parting lines; interface design must consider these process limitations early on.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Tighter tolerances are always better"—Wrong. Overly tight tolerances mean higher machining costs and lower yield rates. The correct approach is to allocate tolerances based on functional requirements, relaxing them as much as possible while still satisfying assembly functionality.
  2. "Dowel pins are optional"—Wrong. Without dowel pins, bolts alone cannot guarantee repeatable assembly precision. The locating capability of bolts is limited by the clearance between bolts and clearance holes, typically far inferior to the positioning accuracy of dowel pins.
  3. "Higher sealing ratings are always better"—Wrong. Higher IP ratings require more complex sealing structures, bringing greater friction torque and worse heat dissipation. The appropriate protection rating should be selected based on the robot's actual operating environment, rather than blindly pursuing the highest rating.
  4. "Thicker housings are safer"—Wrong. Increased housing thickness adds weight and increases joint inertia, actually degrading dynamic performance. The correct approach is to achieve a balance between stiffness and weight through reasonable stiffener placement and material selection.
