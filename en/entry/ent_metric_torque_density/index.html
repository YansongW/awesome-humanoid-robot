Summary

The ratio of continuous output torque to actuator mass or volume, a key figure of merit for compact, high-performance humanoid joints.

Overview

Torque density is a core metric that measures the torque an actuator can produce per unit mass or per unit volume, typically defined as the ratio of peak torque to mass (N·m/kg) or to volume (N·m/L). It directly determines the upper limit of a robot joint's dynamic motion capability and is a key parameter for evaluating the matching quality between motors and reducers. Power density (W/kg) measures the mechanical power output per unit mass, and the two are interrelated through the speed relationship \(P = \tau \omega\).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Torque density \(\tau_d\) is defined as the ratio of an actuator's peak torque to its mass:

\[ \tau_d = \frac{\tau_{\text{peak}}}{m} \]

where \(\tau_{\text{peak}}\) is the peak torque (N·m) and \(m\) is the total actuator mass (kg). In engineering, volumetric torque density (N·m/L) is also commonly used for space-constrained joint layouts. The corresponding power density \(P_d\) is defined as:

\[ P_d = \frac{P_{\text{peak}}}{m} \]

where \(P_{\text{peak}}\) is the peak mechanical power (W). The two are related through motor speed:

\[ P = \tau \, \omega \]

where \(\omega\) is the angular velocity (rad/s). This relationship reveals a fundamental contradiction in actuator design: the motor itself operates most efficiently in the high-speed, low-torque region, while robot joints require low-speed, high-torque output, necessitating a reducer for "torque amplification."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In the history of robot actuator design, torque density was not initially a core metric. Early industrial robots used large AC servo motors paired with high-ratio harmonic reducers, resulting in heavy overall mass and slow dynamic response, but the fixed task scenarios made torque density less critical. With the rise of humanoid robots, joint modules must be integrated within limited space and mass budgets—single legs must support the entire body weight, and arms must perform fine manipulation—making torque density a bottleneck metric that determines overall machine performance.

The deeper reason for this shift is that: what it truly changes is not the motor's own performance, but the feasibility boundary of the entire machine's dynamic design. Without sufficient torque density, the peak torque required for bipedal walking cannot be achieved within a limited mass, and dynamic balance control becomes impossible. Therefore, torque density has risen from a "reference metric" to a "design constraint."

Principle Breakdown

① Physical Source of Torque Density

An actuator's torque output is jointly determined by the motor and the reducer. The motor's output torque is proportional to current (\(\tau = k_t I\), where \(k_t\) is the torque constant, in N·m/A). After amplification by the reduction ratio \(G\), the joint output torque is:

\[ \tau_{\text{out}} = G \, k_t \, I - J_{\text{eq}} \ddot{\theta}_m \]

where \(J_{\text{eq}}\) is the equivalent moment of inertia and \(\ddot{\theta}_m\) is the motor angular acceleration. The larger the reduction ratio \(G\), the greater the output torque, but the equivalent moment of inertia grows with \(G^2\), slowing dynamic response. Therefore, improving torque density is essentially about finding the optimal balance between "torque amplification" and "dynamic response."

② Allocation Logic of Mass Budget

The total actuator mass \(m\) consists of the motor mass, reducer mass, encoder, driver, and structural components. Under a given mass budget, increasing motor size can improve \(k_t\), but it compresses the mass space available for the reducer and structural parts; increasing the reduction ratio can boost output torque, but it introduces greater friction and backlash. In engineering, the goal is typically to "maximize output torque per unit mass," determining the matching parameters between motor and reducer through multi-objective optimization.

③ Time Constant of Dynamic Response

Robot dynamic response can also be described by the mechanical time constant:

\[ \tau_m = \frac{J \, R}{k_t^2} \]

where \(J\) is the equivalent moment of inertia of the rotor and load, \(R\) is the armature resistance, and \(k_t\) is the torque constant. The smaller the time constant, the faster the motor accelerates and decelerates. This formula reveals the intrinsic link between torque density and dynamic response: increasing \(k_t\) (i.e., improving torque density) simultaneously shortens the mechanical time constant, but increasing \(J\) (e.g., enlarging the rotor) offsets this benefit.

Horizontal Comparison

Among mainstream humanoid robot actuator solutions, the implementation paths for torque density show clear divergence:

  • High-reduction-ratio solutions (e.g., harmonic reducers + frameless motors): High output torque and torque density, but poor backdrivability and slow dynamic response, suitable for high-torque joints like hips and knees.
  • Low-reduction-ratio quasi-direct-drive solutions (e.g., planetary reducers + large-diameter motors): Moderate torque density, but good transparency and fast dynamic response, suitable for joints requiring force control like ankles and wrists.
  • Direct-drive solutions: No reducer, lowest torque density, but best transparency, mainly used in research platforms.

This divergence reflects the fundamental contradiction between torque density and dynamic response: pursuing extreme torque density inevitably sacrifices dynamic response, and vice versa. Engineering judgment: humanoid robot overall design typically adopts a hybrid approach, using high reduction ratios for high-torque joints and quasi-direct drive for fine manipulation joints.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The torque density metric is universally applicable in overall machine evaluation. Taking Tesla Optimus as an example, its self-developed linear/rotary actuators and reuse of automotive-grade supply chains underpin its long-term price target of 20,000–30,000 USD (source: wiki/docs/chapters/chapter-26.md). The implicit premise of this price target is: maintaining sufficient torque density under low-cost constraints; otherwise, the overall machine's dynamic performance cannot meet requirements.

In warehouse logistics scenarios, Agility Digit deploys via the RaaS model (source: wiki/docs/chapters/chapter-27.md), where box-handling tasks have clear requirements for joint peak torque, and torque density directly determines the load limit per handling operation and battery life. In home service scenarios, the GR-3 enters through low-operation-intensity tasks such as companionship, reminders, and light object retrieval (source: wiki/docs/chapters/chapter-27.md), where torque density requirements are relatively relaxed, but safety, cost, and power consumption are more critical.

Limitations and Boundaries

Torque density is not always better; its improvement is constrained by multiple factors:

  1. Thermal management bottleneck: High torque density means greater heat loss density per unit volume, making heat dissipation a hard constraint. Under sustained high-load conditions, peak torque density cannot be maintained and requires derating.
  2. Material and process limits: The machining precision and cost of high-energy-product permanent magnets and high-strength structural steels limit further improvements in torque density.
  3. Dynamic response trade-off: As mentioned, improving torque density often comes with increased equivalent moment of inertia, lengthening the mechanical time constant and degrading dynamic response.
  4. Reliability degradation: High torque density designs typically imply higher mechanical and thermal stresses, imposing stricter requirements on reducer lifespan and motor insulation levels.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The higher the torque density, the better the robot performance"—Wrong. Torque density is a necessary condition but not a sufficient one. Overall machine performance also depends on control bandwidth, force control accuracy, thermal management capability, and reliability. High torque density achieved at the expense of dynamic response can actually reduce overall motion performance.

  2. "Peak torque density equals continuous torque density"—Wrong. Peak torque density is only achievable under short-term conditions; continuous operation requires thermal derating. In engineering, both the rated torque density and the ratio of peak to rated torque density should be considered.

  3. "Torque density and power density can be directly converted"—Not entirely correct. The two are related through speed, but motor efficiency varies across speed regions, so a simple \(P = \tau \omega\) conversion is not valid. High-speed regions have high power density but low torque density, and vice versa for low-speed regions.

  4. "The larger the reduction ratio, the higher the torque density"—Wrong. Increasing the reduction ratio does amplify output torque, but the reducer's own mass and volume also increase, and the equivalent moment of inertia grows with \(G^2\), degrading dynamic response. There is an optimal reduction ratio; beyond it, torque density actually decreases.
