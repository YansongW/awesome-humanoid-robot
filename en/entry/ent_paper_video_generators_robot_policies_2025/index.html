Summary

Despite tremendous progress in dexterous manipulation, current visuomotor policies remain fundamentally limited by two challenges: they struggle to generalize under perceptual or behavioral distribution shifts, and their performance is constrained by the size of human demonstration data.

Overview

This paper proposes the Video Policy modular framework, which integrates a video generation model (based on Stable Video Diffusion) with an action generation model (based on Diffusion Policy's 1D CNN U-Net) end-to-end, directly serving as a robot visuomotor policy. The core contribution lies in a two-stage training and gradient truncation design that enables a pretrained video generation model to drive policy learning, significantly surpassing existing baselines on RoboCasa, Libero10, and real-world tasks, demonstrating the potential of video generation models as general-purpose robot policy backbones.

What It Changes

The real shift in this work is redefining the role of video generation models in robot policies. Previous works either treated video generation as an auxiliary representation learning tool or relied on hand-crafted action decoding mechanisms (e.g., keypoint tracking), which were limited by expressiveness or demonstration data scale. This paper proves that a large-scale pretrained video diffusion model (SVD) can directly serve as the policy backbone, implicitly planning actions by generating future frames, without explicitly modeling scene dynamics or hand-designing intermediate representations. This changes the perception that "video generation is just predicting pixels," elevating it to a generalizable policy representation space.

More critically, it addresses the fundamental contradiction in behavior cloning: "limited demonstration data leads to generalization gaps." By freezing the video generator's gradients (stop-gradient), the action head only learns the mapping from video features to actions, while the video features themselves are pretrained on massive internet video data. This effectively uses pretrained world model priors to compensate for the scarcity of robot demonstration data, allowing the policy to remain robust under distribution shifts (new objects, new backgrounds)—something traditional behavior cloning methods struggle to achieve.

Method Breakdown

Architecture Overview

Video Policy consists of two modules: - Video generator f: A video U-Net μθ based on Image-To-Video Stable Video Diffusion (SVD), responsible for synthesizing future frame sequences {v̂ₜ}. - Action generator g: A 1D CNN U-Net αθ based on Diffusion Policy, predicting action sequences {aₜ} from video features.

Video Generator Design

  • Input is the initial observation frame v₀ and task description c, conditioned via cross-attention using the CLIP text encoder φ(c).
  • The frozen VAE from SVD encodes the input frame as z₀, which is concatenated channel-wise with noisy frames z₁...zₜ before being fed into the U-Net.
  • Multi-view processing: RoboCasa uses 3 cameras (gripper, left, right), predicting 8 frames per view, totaling 24 frames, with the sequence padded with a single-camera frame at the start to reach 25 frames, aligning with SVD pretraining input. Libero10 uses 2 views with 12 frames per view.

Action Generator Design

  • Five uniformly spaced hidden embeddings are extracted from the video U-Net decoder layers (layers 9, 14, 17, 20, 23), converted into a single vector hᵢ via a CNN adapter.
  • hᵢ serves as the global conditioning input for the 1D CNN U-Net (adapted from Diffusion Policy), generating action sequences {aₜ} = αθ(aᵢ, i, hᵢ), with a 7-dimensional action space (6-DoF gripper pose + gripper open/close scalar).

Training Strategy

Two-stage training: 1. Stage 1 (Video Prediction): Only μθ is trained, with loss L_video = E[||ε − μθ(zᵢ, i, φ(c), zᵢ,₀)||²], learning rate fixed at 1e−5. 2. Stage 2 (Action Prediction): μθ is frozen, αθ is trained, with loss L_action = E[||ε − αθ(aᵢ, i, hᵢ)||²], learning rate 5e−5.

Key Design Decision: In Stage 2, gradient backpropagation of L_action to μθ is stopped. The authors found this yields significant performance gains (2-Stage 0.63 vs Joint 0.57), because the video network, benefiting from extensive pretraining, has stronger generalization and should act as the policy's "brain," while αθ only decodes actions.

Inference Pipeline

Given the initial observation v₀ and task description c, the model jointly generates predicted video frames and action sequences. Actions directly control the robot's end effector; in real-world deployment, impedance control follows the predicted gripper pose for 24 steps (out of 32 total).

Key Innovations

  1. Modular Video-Action Joint Framework: Unlike previous works that treated video generation and action prediction as independent tasks or simple concatenation, this paper tightly couples them via intermediate features (hᵢ), with gradient truncation embodying the "video-driven policy" design philosophy. This modular design allows independent training and debugging while remaining end-to-end differentiable.

  2. Leveraging Pretrained Video Model Generalization Priors: SVD is pretrained on massive internet video, containing rich priors about physical world dynamics. By freezing its parameters and training only a lightweight action head, the policy inherits this generalization capability—something traditional behavior cloning (relying solely on robot demonstration data) cannot achieve. Experiments show that on distribution shift tasks (e.g., PnP series in Table 8), 32-step video prediction improves average success by 37 percentage points over 0-step (pure action prediction).

  3. Two-Stage Training with Gradient Truncation: This is the key engineering innovation driving performance gains. Joint training (Joint) degrades performance due to interference between video and action objectives (0.57 vs 0.63), while two-stage training with stop-gradient lets the video network focus on learning scene dynamics and the action head on decoding actions, achieving "division of labor" optimization.

Result Interpretation: Video Policy comprehensively outperforms existing methods on simulation benchmarks, especially on RoboCasa where 50 demos surpass GR00T's 300 demos (0.63 vs 0.50), demonstrating data efficiency advantages. Ablation experiments confirm that both video prediction and two-stage training are indispensable. In real-world experiments, Upright Object and Stack Cups fail due to unrealistic video predictions (lacking physical priors), and M&Ms to Cup degrades sharply under background changes, revealing the boundaries of the current method.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • The paper only validates SVD as a single video generation model instantiation, without exploring other diffusion models or autoregressive video models, leaving the generality of conclusions in question.
  • Simulation experiments are limited to RoboCasa and Libero10 benchmarks; real-world tests use only a single embodiment (handheld gripper), without validation on more robot platforms (e.g., dual-arm, mobile manipulation).
  • Video diffusion model inference is computationally expensive: generating 25 frames on an A100 GPU takes approximately 9 seconds, far from real-time control requirements—a major bottleneck for practical deployment.
  • Real-world failure cases reveal that pretrained SVD lacks real-world physical priors (e.g., upright placement, gripper-object interaction mechanics), leading to unrealistic video predictions.
  • The paper does not mention inference frequency, GPU memory usage, or other key engineering metrics, nor does it analyze the model's robustness to initial observation noise.

Engineering Insights

  • Reproduction Priority: First verify the implementation details of two-stage training and gradient truncation, as this is the largest source of performance differences (2-Stage 0.63 vs Joint 0.57). Ensure the Stage 1 video model fully converges (approximately 368k steps) before training the action head.
  • Data Configuration: 50 demos per task in simulation suffice for good results, but real-world requires 200 demos. If data is limited, consider MimicGen for automatic data generation (300 demos can further improve to 0.66).
  • Common Pitfalls: Multi-view video concatenation must strictly align with SVD pretraining input format (25 frames, first frame padded); action space must align with video prediction time steps (video subsampled at stride 4, 32 action prediction steps correspond to 8 frames/view); real-world training requires two-stage fine-tuning from low resolution (256×192) to high resolution (448×320).
  • Deployment Notes: Use 30-step denoising and classifier-free guidance scale 2.0 at inference; real-world requires handling gripper force feedback (applying closure correction when exceeding 300g threshold); the 9-second video generation latency means the policy cannot respond in real time, requiring open-loop or low-frequency control strategies.
  • Selection Recommendations: If downstream tasks demand high robustness to distribution shifts (e.g., new objects, new backgrounds), Video Policy has clear advantages; but if high-frequency real-time control is needed, the current inference cost is unacceptable, suggesting exploration of distillation or reduced denoising steps.
