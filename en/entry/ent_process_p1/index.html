Overview

Requirements Definition and System Concept (Concept / Pre-A) is the first stage in the full development process of humanoid robot products, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. The core mission of this stage is to transform business intent into verifiable engineering specifications, freeze product specifications and platform strategies, and provide an input baseline for all subsequent engineering stages. What it truly changes is not the number of documents, but the decision-making approach—from "shooting from the hip" to "traceable."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Requirements Definition and System Concept (Concept / Pre-A) is the P1 stage among the 17 stage entities (research/methods/ent_process_p0 … p16) in the knowledge graph covering the complete process of humanoid robots from project initiation to mass production. In the process skeleton, P0 (Project Initiation and Commercial Baseline) determines target cost, target production volume, and target market, while the core deliverable of P1 is freezing product specifications and platform strategies. This means the output of P1 is not a "wish list," but a set of engineering constraints that have been reviewed, quantified, and frozen.

In WBS V3, this stage is expanded into two major work packages: P1.1 Requirements Engineering and P1.2 System Concept Design. Each work package is further subdivided into several level-3 subtasks, and each level-3 subtask is uniformly expanded into five level-4 key actions: input review and target quantification, solution/method design, implementation/prototype/sample fabrication, verification and issue closure, and documentation output and downstream delivery. This "five-step method" structure ensures that every subtask has clear inputs, methods, outputs, and acceptance criteria.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the early days of the humanoid robot industry, teams often skipped P1 and went directly into detailed design. The result: mechanical engineers selected motors by intuition, software engineers defined interfaces by imagination, and only at the integration stage did they discover insufficient joint peak torque, inadequate communication bandwidth, or substandard safety levels—rework costs rising exponentially.

P1 exists precisely to counter this "design debt." Its historical positioning is similar to the "platform pre-research" stage in the automotive industry: before investing heavily in physical prototyping, risks are exposed on paper through documents, models, and reviews. The knowledge graph places P1 after P0 and before P2–P3 (Industrial Design and Mechatronic Overall Design), with a clear logic: freeze specifications first, then discuss styling and structure. P2–P3 determine the upper limit of manufacturability, but P1 determines the input quality for P2–P3.

Principle Breakdown

① Requirements Engineering: From Business Language to Engineering Language

The core action of P1.1 Requirements Engineering is "translation." When stakeholders say "it must be able to climb stairs," engineers must translate this into "step height 200 mm, climbing speed ≥ 0.5 m/s, continuous climbing of 20 steps without surging." This translation process relies on two key tools:

  • Kano Model: Distinguishes Must-have (basic needs, failure if absent), Differentiator (excitement needs, exceeding expectations), and Exciter (attractive needs, surprise points). The mathematical foundation of the Kano model is the satisfaction function: $$ S = f(\text{degree of requirement fulfillment}, \text{requirement type}) $$ where the satisfaction curve for Must-have is a concave function—satisfaction drops sharply when fulfillment is insufficient, but growth slows after exceeding the threshold; the curve for Exciter is convex—satisfaction does not decline at low fulfillment levels, but rises sharply after exceeding the threshold.

  • SysML Use Case Diagrams and Requirements Trees: Decompose stakeholder requirements layer by layer into system requirements, subsystem requirements, and component requirements, forming a requirements traceability chain. The SMART principle (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) ensures each requirement is verifiable.

② System Concept Design: The Game of Divergence and Convergence

P1.2 System Concept Design follows the engineering paradigm of "divergence first, then convergence." P1.2.1 (Concept Divergence and Convergence) requires the use of brainstorming, morphological sketches, Morphological Matrix, Pugh Matrix, and other methods to systematically evaluate multiple whole-machine layouts, actuation schemes, and sensing schemes, ultimately outputting at least 3 concept schemes, a scoring matrix, and the rationale for the selected scheme.

The decision logic of the Pugh Matrix is: using the baseline scheme as a reference, score each candidate scheme on multiple criteria (technical maturity, cost, manufacturability) as (+1/0/-1), then select the best via weighted summation. Its mathematical form is: $$ S_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \cdot c_{ij} $$ where \(w_i\) is the weight of the \(i\)-th criterion, and \(c_{ij}\) is the score of the \(j\)-th scheme on the \(i\)-th criterion.

③ Proof of Concept (POC): Kill the Biggest Risk at Minimum Cost

The logic of P1.2.4 (Proof of Concept) is to validate the highest-risk items (e.g., joint peak torque, single-leg balance, finger force control) early, rather than discovering problems at whole-machine integration. Methods include rapid prototyping, key subsystem bench testing, and technical de-risking. Deliverables are the POC report, risk downgrade records, and Go/No-Go decisions.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key parameters of the P1 stage are not motor torque or reduction ratios, but the degree of quantification of requirements. The table below lists the core deliverables of the P1 stage and their acceptance characteristics:

Deliverable Key Parameters Acceptance Criteria
Stakeholder List Number of covered roles, representativeness, and conflict records All key stakeholders identified and confirmed
Requirements Pool Number of requirement items, priority ranking Each requirement has a unique ID and source traceability

The key distinction: P0 answers "why do it," P1 answers "what to do," and P2–P3 answer "how to do it." P1 is the only stage with "freezing" as its goal—the cost of changes in subsequent stages rises sharply as the process advances.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The methodology of the P1 stage has been validated in multiple scenarios:

  • Socially Assistive Robot (SAR) Design: Research by Liberman-Pincu et al. (2022) points out that SAR manufacturers often apply the same appearance design across different scenarios, overlooking the critical impact of usage context on user experience. The study constructed a contextual framework with four dimensions—domain, physical environment, user, and role—and collected user feedback on expected features and visual quality for four typical SARs (nursing home service robot, hospital medical assistant, COVID-19 inspection robot, and home personal assistant) via an online questionnaire survey (N=228). The results show that user expectations for robot appearance and functional features vary significantly with usage scenarios—a typical application of "stakeholder requirements collection" in the P1 stage.

  • Humanoid Robot Mass Production Preparation: The assembly line planning and SOP development in the P16 (Small-Batch Trial Production and Mass Production Preparation) stage have their upstream input precisely in the product specifications frozen by P1. The station design, tooling and fixtures, standard operations, and cycle time balancing in P16.1.3 (Assembly Line Planning and SOP) all depend on the BOM structure and assembly feasibility analysis output by P1.

Limitations and Boundaries

The P1 stage has three inherent limitations:

  1. Requirements are never complete: Stakeholders cannot foresee all usage scenarios at the Pre-A stage. Engineering judgment: the goal of P1 is not to "capture all requirements," but to "capture enough key requirements and establish a change management mechanism."

  2. Quantified metrics may be distorted: When translating business language into engineering language, certain metrics (e.g., "user experience") are difficult to quantify. The Kano model can classify, but cannot precisely measure "excitement." It must be acknowledged: different teams may have significantly different quantification standards for the same requirement.

  3. Platform strategy vs. innovation tension: Freezing the platform strategy may limit subsequent innovation space. P1 must balance "standardization" and "flexibility," which is essentially a management decision rather than a technical one.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "P1 is just writing documents"—Wrong. The core of P1 is decision-making; documents are merely the carrier of decisions. A P1 without reviews, quantitative scoring, or Go/No-Go decisions is just armchair strategy.

  2. "Requirements collection is just sending out surveys"—Wrong. Interviews, surveys, workshops, and on-site observation each have their applicable scenarios. On-site observation can uncover latent needs that users themselves are unaware of, while surveys can only validate known assumptions.

  3. "The more concept schemes, the better"—Wrong. P1.2.1 requires at least 3 concept schemes, but convergence matters more than divergence. The value of the Pugh Matrix lies not in the scoring itself, but in forcing the team to clarify criterion weights—which often exposes real disagreements.

  4. "Passing the POC means everything is fine"—Wrong. The POC only validates the highest-risk items; it does not mean all risks have been eliminated. The output of P1.2.4 is a "risk downgrade record," not a "risk elimination certificate."
