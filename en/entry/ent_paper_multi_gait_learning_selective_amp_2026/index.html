Summary

Learning diverse locomotion skills for humanoid robots in a unified reinforcement learning framework remains challenging due to the conflicting requirements of stability and dynamic expressiveness across different gaits.

Overview

This paper proposes a method for learning five gaits (walking, march, stair climbing, running, jumping) for a humanoid robot within a unified reinforcement learning framework. The core contribution is a "Selective Adversarial Motion Priors" (Selective AMP) strategy: the AMP discriminator is activated only for periodic gaits with moderate joint amplitudes (walking, march, stair climbing), while it is disabled for gaits requiring explosive dynamics (running, jumping), preventing its regularization constraints from hindering the policy from reaching necessary motion limits. The method is trained in Isaac Gym and deployed with zero-shot transfer on the Lite_11 humanoid robot.

What It Changes

Existing AMP-based works (e.g., Peng et al. 2021, Escontrela et al. 2022, Lerario et al. 2024) treat AMP as a "universal additive" applied uniformly across all tasks, assuming it always improves behavior quality. The insight of this paper is that AMP's effectiveness heavily depends on the kinematic characteristics of the target gait—for periodic gaits with moderate joint amplitudes (walking, march), AMP effectively reinforces stylized motion patterns; however, for gaits requiring large joint excursions, rapid acceleration, and explosive dynamics (running, jumping), human motion reference data is scarce, and the AMP discriminator's regularization becomes a constraint, forcing the policy toward conservative, over-regularized motion patterns and hindering it from reaching necessary motion limits.

This finding changes the core design logic of multi-gait learning: instead of pursuing a universal solution of "one reward function + one AMP configuration," AMP is treated as an "on-demand stylization tool" selectively activated based on the dynamic requirements of the gait. Furthermore, this paper demonstrates that under a unified MDP structure, unified observation space, and unified reward formulation, a single policy network can learn five gaits with vastly different motion characteristics solely through differentiated reference trajectory parameters, reward weights, and AMP configurations, challenging the previous convention of "independent training for each gait."

Method Breakdown

Unified RL Framework

All five gaits share the same MDP structure, policy architecture (Actor MLP [512,256,128], ELU activation), observation space (50 dimensions/frame, stacked over 21 frames = 1050 dimensions), and reward formulation (r_total = Σ_i w_i r_i), distinguished only by reference trajectory parameters, reward weights, and AMP configurations.

Observation Space (50 dimensions/frame)

  • Base linear velocity v_b ∈ ℝ³ (scaled by 2.0)
  • Velocity command c ∈ ℝ³
  • Phase encoding φ ∈ ℝ²: [sin(2πφ_t), cos(2πφ_t)]
  • Base angular velocity ω_b ∈ ℝ³ (scaled by 0.25)
  • Projected gravity vector g_p ∈ ℝ³
  • Joint position error q_e ∈ ℝ¹² (difference from default standing configuration)
  • Joint velocity q̇ ∈ ℝ¹² (scaled by 0.05)
  • Previous action a_{t-1} ∈ ℝ¹²
  • Phase variable φ_t ∈ [0,1), advanced by φ_{t+1} = (φ_t + Δt/T_cycle) mod 1

Reference Trajectory Generation

  • Periodic gaits (walking, march, running, stair climbing): sinusoidal reference q_ref^L,hip = -s·𝟙_{s<0}, q_ref^L,knee = 2s·σ·𝟙_{s<0}, where s = sin(2πφ_t) and σ is the target joint position scaling (walking/running 0.26, march 0.28). Each gait is modulated by (T_cycle, stance_ratio): walking (0.8s, 0.6), march (0.7s, 0.45), running (0.4s, 0.35), stair climbing (0.7s, 0.6).
  • Jumping: piecewise 4.0s cycle trajectory with five phases (crouch φ<0.30, takeoff 0.30-0.42, flight 0.42-0.48, landing 0.48-0.75, standing φ≥0.75), with curriculum learning gradually increasing crouch depth between 2000-10000 PPO iterations.

Actions and PD Control

  • 12-dimensional action vector, target joint position q_target = a_t·k_action·k_gain + q_default, with k_action varying by gait (walking/stair climbing 0.25, running 0.3, march 0.4)
  • PD controller τ = K_P(q_target - q) - K_D q̇, with K_P ranging from 75-280 N·m/rad (significantly higher for march: hip pitch 450, knee 800), policy at 50Hz, PD at 200Hz, action delay of 5-10 steps

Reward Design (Four Categories)

  1. Joint tracking: r_track = 0.15 Σ(e^{-4e²} + e^{-20e²}), with knee term masked only during swing phase
  2. Base balance and posture: posture, base height, linear velocity tracking (running w=20.0, jumping 0.0), angular velocity tracking
  3. Motion smoothness: action smoothing penalty, joint velocity, nominal torque
  4. Safety and gait-specific: termination conditions (|roll|>0.8 rad), foot swing height, jumping-specific (height target 0.275m Gaussian reward w=150.0, vertical takeoff velocity piecewise nonlinear w=120.0)

Selective AMP Strategy

  • Discriminator: 3-layer MLP [1024,512,256], ReLU, input is (s_t, s_{t+1}) concatenation
  • AMP reward: r_AMP = α·max(0, 1 - ¼(D-1)²), linearly interpolated with task reward r_combined = (1-β)·r_AMP + β·r_task
  • Discriminator loss: L_D = E_policy[(D+1)²] + E_expert[(D-1)²] + λ·E_expert[‖∇D‖²], λ=10
  • Key configurations: walking (α=0.3, β=0.8), march (α=0.6, β=0.7), stair climbing (α=0.3, β=0.8), running (α=0.0, β=1.0), jumping (α=0.0, β=1.0 with symmetry loss disabled)

Training and Deployment

  • PPO, 4096 parallel environments (running/jumping 2048), learning rate 5×10⁻⁴, γ=0.99, GAE λ=0.95
  • Domain randomization: contact properties, base mass [-2,+5]kg, inertia [0.5,1.8], motor strength, PD gains [0.8,1.2], action delay curriculum [2→5] steps
  • Zero-shot Sim-to-Real: ONNX export, policy at 50Hz, PD at 500Hz, base linear velocity observation channel zeroed

Key Innovations

  1. Selective AMP activation mechanism: First systematic demonstration that AMP is not beneficial for all gaits, proposing a strategy for selective activation based on gait dynamic characteristics. AMP is activated for periodic gaits (walking, march, stair climbing) to reinforce stylized motion, while disabled for explosive gaits (running, jumping) to avoid regularization constraints. This design decision is based on in-depth analysis of the AMP discriminator's mechanism: AMP excels at reinforcing periodic repetitive patterns but hinders large joint excursions and rapid acceleration.

  2. Multi-gait learning under a unified framework: Under the exact same MDP structure, observation space (50 dimensions × 21 frames), and reward formulation, five gaits with vastly different motion characteristics are learned solely through differentiated reference trajectory parameters (T_cycle, stance_ratio), reward weights, and AMP configurations. This avoids the traditional approach of designing separate reward functions and hyperparameters for each gait, significantly reducing engineering complexity.

  3. Dedicated rewards and curriculum design for jumping: For jumping, the most difficult gait to learn, a piecewise nonlinear vertical takeoff velocity reward (linear ramp below 1.0 m/s, quadratic above) and a Gaussian reward with a 0.275m height target are designed, combined with a crouch depth curriculum (progressively increased over 2000-10000 iterations), enabling convergence within 11000 iterations without AMP assistance.

AMP Comparison Experiments (Figure 3)

  • March with AMP: rapid increase in total reward, low training error, rapid failure rate decay, validating AMP's positive effect on periodic gaits
  • Jumping without AMP: still achieves sustained reward growth and low failure rate, with joint tracking signals steadily improving, validating that disabling AMP does not affect explosive gait learning

Key Findings

  • March uses higher AMP weights (α=0.6, β=0.7) to enforce a distinctive straight-leg style, with training error of only 5.4 and success rate of 97%
  • Jumping's joint tracking metric of 15.5 is significantly higher than other gaits, reflecting its large joint excursion characteristics, but its posture stability metric of 8.6 is also the highest, indicating that jumping demands more from balance control
  • The authors explicitly state that Table III values are intended to characterize training behavior under the unified framework, rather than strictly normalized physical quantities across tasks

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Jumping height not verified: The paper does not mention whether the actual jumping height reaches the 0.275m target, nor does it provide measured jumping height values
  • Running speed not quantified: No actual measured running speed values are provided
  • AMP reference data source unclear: The specific motion types and subject information for human motion capture are not described
  • Stair climbing parameters missing: Specific stair dimensions, step height, and incline are not mentioned
  • Insufficient real hardware testing details: Execution duration, number of repeated trials, and external disturbance test setups for each gait on the real robot are not mentioned
  • No comparison baselines: No quantitative benchmark comparisons with other multi-gait learning methods
  • Entropy coefficient not specified: The authors mention a slightly higher entropy coefficient for jumping but do not provide the specific value
  • Analytical explanation of AMP limitations: The discussion of "strong AMP may increase behavioral conservatism" for running is an analytical judgment rather than experimental validation

Engineering Insights

  1. AMP configuration is the primary item to check: When reproducing, first confirm the α and β settings for each gait—walking/stair climbing (0.3, 0.8), march (0.6, 0.7), running/jumping (0.0, 1.0). This is the most critical design decision in this paper; incorrect AMP configuration will cause running and jumping policies to be overly conservative or fail to reach motion limits.

  2. March PD gains are the biggest pitfall: March uses significantly higher stiffness (hip pitch 450, knee 800 N·m/rad) and damping (knee K_D up to 40), far exceeding other gaits. If the walking PD parameters are used uniformly, the straight-leg swing style of march cannot be achieved. It is recommended to configure PD gains separately per gait rather than sharing them.

  3. Sensitivity of jumping rewards: The jumping height target Gaussian reward center at 0.275m, vertical takeoff velocity threshold at 1.0 m/s, and horizontal velocity penalty w=-15.0 are highly coupled. It is recommended to first fix the height reward, then tune the takeoff velocity reward, and finally fine-tune the horizontal velocity penalty to avoid interference between parameters.

  4. Engineering trick of zeroing base linear velocity: When base linear velocity cannot be directly measured on hardware, set the corresponding observation channel to zero and rely on the 21-frame observation history stack to implicitly capture velocity information. This trick is crucial for deployment, but it must be ensured that training also simulates this missing channel (rather than zeroing it only at deployment).

  5. Differences in training iterations: March requires only 20001 iterations and jumping 11000, while walking/stair climbing/running require 40001 iterations. If training resources are limited, prioritize full training for walking and stair climbing; march and jumping can be stopped early.

  6. Domain randomization ranges need gait-specific adjustment: Jumping uses more conservative randomization ranges (motor strength [0.9,1.1]), while running/march use wider ranges [0.8,1.2]. When reproducing, avoid using unified randomization parameters for all gaits, as this would compromise the robustness of the jumping policy.
