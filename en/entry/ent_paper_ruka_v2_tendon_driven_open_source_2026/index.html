Summary

Lack of accessible and dexterous robot hardware has been a significant bottleneck to achieving human-level dexterity in robots. Last year, we released Ruka, a fully open-sourced, tendon-driven humanoid hand with 11 degrees of freedom - 2 per finger and 3 at the thumb - buildable for under $1,300. Institutions per source list: New York University、New York University Shanghai.

Overview

Ruka-v2 is an open-source tendon-driven dexterous hand developed by a team at New York University. Building on Ruka, it adds a decoupled 2-DoF parallel wrist and finger adduction/abduction capabilities, with a total cost of approximately $1.5K. Its core contribution lies in approximating human hand kinematics with low-cost hardware, validated through user studies, thermal endurance tests, and policy learning for practical utility and reproducibility in robot learning tasks.

What It Changes

The dexterous hand field has long faced an awkward trilemma: direct-drive hands (Allegro, LEAP) are bulky with large embodiment gaps, small direct-drive hands (Wuji, Sharpa) suffer from thermal throttling or prohibitive costs, and tendon-driven hands (Shadow Hand) are too expensive to普及. What Ruka-v2 truly changes is loosening the constraints of this trilemma—it demonstrates that at a $1.5K cost, one can simultaneously achieve human-sized form factors, low inertia from tendon-driven actuation, and the wrist and finger abduction degrees of freedom that most prior designs have overlooked.

More critically, it elevates the "wrist" from an accessory to a first-class citizen in design. Most existing hands focus solely on the fingers, but in daily human manipulation, the wrist contributes substantial kinematic redundancy and postural adjustment capabilities. Ruka-v2 implements a 2-DoF wrist via a decoupled parallel mechanism. Although its flexion/extension range (45°/30°) still falls short of human capabilities (80-90°/70°), it is sufficient to support teleoperation and policy learning tasks. Another change is replacing expensive motion-capture gloves with magnetic encoders, directly lowering the reproduction barrier—whereas Ruka previously relied on professional equipment for teleoperation, now an ESP32 plus a few AS5600 sensors suffice.

Method Breakdown

Overall Architecture

  • Tendon-driven humanoid hand with 20 total DoF / 16 active DoF; actuators placed proximally on the forearm to reduce distal inertia
  • All structural components are 3D-printed; bearings, fasteners, and springs are off-the-shelf parts; total material cost <$1,500
  • Tendons route through the wrist and palm to the fingers; guides prevent sharp bends and reduce tension fluctuations during wrist posture changes

Wrist Mechanism

  • Decoupled parallel design: flexion/extension and radial/ulnar deviation are independently driven, with motors located beneath the wrist
  • A rectangular linkage surrounds the wrist, with rotation axes converging at a single geometric pivot point; a passive ball joint defines the common center of rotation
  • The ball joint features through-holes allowing tendons to pass near the center of rotation; bearing-supported routing plates redirect tendons outward to the forearm

Finger Abduction/Adduction Module

  • Independent knuckle modules within the palm, accommodating both the primary MCP flexion axis and a secondary abduction/adduction axis
  • The middle finger is fixed as a structural reference; other fingers are each driven by dedicated tendons
  • Small extension springs provide restoring force when tendons are slack, increasing contact compliance, reducing bidirectional control requirements, and defining a clear default posture

Optional DIP/PIP Coupling

  • Fixed-length coupling strings pass through internal channels, anchoring proximal and distal segments to force synchronized PIP and DIP motion (α ≈ β)
  • Improves repeatability and simplifies control at the cost of losing passive compliance

Sensing and Controller

  • AS5600 magnetic angle sensors (12-bit) with magnet holders press-fit into joint pins; ESP32 QTPy communicates via I2C multiplexer
  • Joint-to-motor mapping uses a linear model: p = p_min + c · (θ/(θ_max − θ_min)) · (p_max − p_min)
  • Scaling factor c compensates for internal tendon friction, finger weight, or joint angle underestimation; calibration is an automated process

Teleoperation and Policy Learning

  • Retargeting uses AnyTeleop vector-based Dex Retargeting, optimizing link vector alignment rather than fingertip position matching
  • Teleoperation: Open Teach framework + Oculus VR; wrist trajectories mapped to the Franka end-effector
  • Policy learning: BAKU framework, approximately 100 demonstrations per task; Gaussian noise injected during training to enhance robustness
  • Observation space: 23-dimensional proprioception (7 DoF Franka + 16 DoF Ruka-v2) + RGB video stream; ResNet-18 visual encoder + MLP proprioceptive encoder + transformer backbone + action chunking head

Key Innovations

  1. Tendon routing design for the decoupled parallel wrist: Ball joint through-holes plus bearing-supported routing plates allow tendons to pass near the wrist's center of rotation, maintaining low inertia while avoiding tendon entanglement during large-angle rotations. This is not a simple mechanism addition but a rethinking of the overall tendon-driven system layout—the wrist DoF is not "added on" but "integrated in."

  2. Spring-based restoring mechanism for the abduction/adduction module: Extension springs provide passive restoring force, avoiding the need for bidirectional active actuation of every abduction joint. This reduces actuator count, increases contact compliance, and defines a clear default posture. For policy learning, this means a smaller action space and more stable initial states.

  3. Attachable magnetic encoder kit: Compatible with both Ruka and Ruka-v2, replacing motion-capture gloves with AS5600 sensors. This directly addresses the reproducibility pain point of the previous generation—now only a 3D printer and off-the-shelf electronic components are needed to build a complete teleoperation system, reducing costs from tens of thousands of dollars to a few hundred.

Experiments and Results

User Study Comparison (Ruka vs Ruka-v2)

10 participants, 3 single-arm tasks × 3 trials, 2 minutes familiarization time, 120-second time limit. Average completion time reduced by 51.3%, overall success rate improved by 21.2%. This indicates that the newly added wrist and abduction DoF provide perceptible benefits in real manipulation tasks.

Thermal Endurance Test (5 hours continuous operation)

Motor Location Peak Temperature Steady-State Temperature Temperature Rise
Fingers 30.75°C 30.35°C 0.81°C
Thumb 47.33°C 46.97°C 1.70°C
Wrist 46.00°C 43.80°C 7.25°C

No thermal throttling or performance degradation; temperatures reached steady state after approximately 30 minutes. Compared to Wuji's 40-minute thermal throttling, this is a significant advantage.

Payload Test

Non-thumb DIP-PIP maximum 1200g (15s), MCP 780g (15s), adduction 150g (15s); thumb 835g (20s); wrist 835-1215g in each direction (20s). Adduction payload is lower, but acceptable given its primary role in posture adjustment rather than load bearing.

Controller Accuracy

20 random angle samples, overall average error 8.26° (10.68%). Per-joint errors range from 2.76° (5.01%) for thumb IP to 12.77° (8.51%) for index MCP. MCP errors are largest, likely related to longer tendon paths and greater friction.

Policy Learning

3 tasks (bread pick-and-place, music box, pen grasping), each with 10 random initial positions × 5 rollouts; success rates reported on a 10-point scale. Tasks leveraging abduction/adduction joints (pen grasping) validated the value of the new DoF.

Joint Range of Motion Comparison

DIP/PIP/MCP/thumb joints all exceed human requirements; index abduction 20° (requirement 20-25°), ring finger 23° (requirement 20-25°), little finger 45° (requirement 30-35°) all pass; middle finger abduction fixed (fail); wrist flexion 45° (requirement 80-90°) and extension 30° (requirement 70°) do not meet human ranges.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Wrist flexion/extension range is significantly lower than human (45°/30° vs 80-90°/70°), limiting tasks requiring substantial wrist posture adjustments
  • Middle finger abduction/adduction is fixed, lacking this DoF, differing from human anatomy
  • The linear assumption in joint-to-motor mapping is not formally validated; authors acknowledge that data-driven approaches (e.g., as in Ruka) may be needed to improve accuracy
  • No mention of long-term durability tests (>5 hours), real-world long-term deployment, tactile sensing integration experiments, multi-hand coordinated policy learning, or quantitative comparison of force/velocity characteristics with human hands
  • Magnetic encoders may be subject to magnetic interference from adjacent sensors; the extent of interference is not characterized, and "out-of-the-box" tactile integration is not yet achieved

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, first verify three things: the guide layout for wrist tendon routing (the most likely source of issues, directly affecting tension stability during large-angle rotations), the preload of abduction springs (too tight increases friction, too loose leads to unstable default posture), and the alignment of magnetic encoder installation (press-fit tolerances directly affect angle reading accuracy).

The most likely pitfall is the scaling factor c in the controller's linear mapping—it compensates for tendon friction and finger weight, but the optimal value varies with assembly differences. It is recommended to perform an automated calibration first, then fine-tune on the target task. Additionally, while the DIP/PIP coupling strings simplify control, they sacrifice passive compliance; if downstream tasks require grasping fragile objects, retaining the uncoupled configuration is advisable.

For downstream teams, the value of Ruka-v2 lies in providing a reproducible baseline platform: $1.5K cost, open-source CAD, off-the-shelf components, sufficient to support teleoperation data collection and policy learning experiments. If tasks require substantial wrist motion (e.g., pouring water, unscrewing bottle caps), its flexion/extension range limitations should be noted; if tasks depend on middle finger abduction (e.g., specific gestures), alternative solutions should be considered.
