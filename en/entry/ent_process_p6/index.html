Overview

URDF modeling and kinematics verification (Kinematics & URDF) is the 6th stage in the full humanoid robot product development workflow, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. The core task of this stage is to convert CAD designs into standardized robot description files that can be correctly parsed by simulation, control, and planning toolchains, and to ensure consistency between the model and the physical prototype through kinematics verification. What it truly changes is not the act of "drawing a robot model" itself, but rather establishing, for the first time, a quantifiable and traceable engineering bridge between "design intent" and "algorithm executability."

Content

What It Is: Engineering Translation from CAD to Computable Models

URDF (Unified Robot Description Format) is an XML format in the ROS ecosystem for describing robot kinematics and dynamics properties. It defines the robot's topological connections, joint types (revolute/prismatic/fixed), kinematic parameters (origin, axis, limits), and inertial properties through a tree structure of <link> and <joint> elements. In the humanoid robot development workflow, the URDF modeling and kinematics verification stage serves as a bridge: it receives mass property data from P3.1.2/P3.1.3 and structural design outputs from stage P5 upstream, and provides a simulatable model foundation for P10 motion control algorithm development downstream.

Why It Exists: The "Translation Error" Pain Point Between Simulation and Physical Robot

Humanoid robots are typical "high-degree-of-freedom, strongly coupled, multi-constrained" systems. Directly importing CAD models into simulation environments presents three critical problems: First, CAD models contain numerous decorative geometries (fillets, threads, chamfers), and using them directly for collision detection causes computational explosion; Second, coordinate system definitions in CAD assemblies are often based on design convenience rather than kinematic conventions (e.g., DH parameters require the z-axis to align with the joint axis); Third, if inertial parameters are not calibrated through physical measurement, the dynamic behavior in simulation will deviate significantly from the actual robot. The existence of the URDF modeling stage is precisely to establish a controlled translation process between the "design model" and the "computational model," ensuring that downstream algorithms such as inverse kinematics, trajectory planning, and state estimation receive a "trustworthy" model.

Principle Breakdown

① Tree Topology: Mathematical Expression of Kinematic Constraints

URDF uses a tree structure to describe the robot, with each joint connecting one parent link and one child link. For humanoid robots, the typical topology is: base_link (pelvis) → torso → head; base_link → left/right hip → thigh → shank → foot. The <origin> field of a joint defines the position and orientation of the child link coordinate frame in the parent link coordinate frame, which is essentially a 4×4 homogeneous transformation matrix:

\[ T_{i}^{i-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{i}^{i-1} & p_{i}^{i-1} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

where \(R\) is the rotation matrix and \(p\) is the translation vector. Forward kinematics involves successively left-multiplying these transformation matrices along the tree structure to obtain the pose of end effectors (such as the foot or hand tip) in the base coordinate frame.

② Inertial Parameters: The "Mass Code" of Dynamics Simulation

Each <link> in URDF must include an <inertial> tag defining mass \(m\), center of mass position \(c\), and inertia tensor \(I\). These parameters directly determine the dynamic response in simulation. Mass properties come from the calibration results of P3.1.2/P3.1.3, rather than default estimates from CAD software—because density settings and simplified modeling approaches in CAD can introduce errors. The inertia tensor is expressed in the link coordinate frame as:

\[ I = \begin{bmatrix} I_{xx} & I_{xy} & I_{xz} \\ I_{xy} & I_{yy} & I_{yz} \\ I_{xz} & I_{yz} & I_{zz} \end{bmatrix} \]

③ Collision Bodies and Visual Models: Trade-off Between Computational Efficiency and Realism

URDF allows defining <visual> (visual model) and <collision> (collision body) for each link. Visual models are used for rendering in visualization tools such as RViz and can retain fine geometry; collision bodies are used for collision detection in physics simulation and must use simplified geometry (boxes, spheres, cylinders). The key constraint is that "collision bodies must not be too dense"—overly detailed collision bodies significantly increase simulation computational cost and can even cause solver instability. Engineering judgment: for humanoid robots, approximating each link's collision body with 1-3 basic geometric primitives is sufficient for the vast majority of simulation needs.

Engineering judgment: The tree-structure limitation of URDF means that if a humanoid robot has closed kinematic chains (such as parallel ankle joints), this must be addressed by adding virtual joints or switching to SDF. However, within the ROS ecosystem, URDF remains the de facto standard and can achieve parametric modeling through the Xacro tool, significantly reducing repetitive code.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

① Simulation-driven control algorithm development (preceding P10 stage): Load URDF models in Gazebo or Isaac Sim for simulation verification of balance control and gait planning. Model quality directly determines the success rate of simulation-to-real transfer.

② Kinematics verification closed loop (P6.2 subtasks): Verify through forward/inverse kinematics solving that joint limits and link lengths in the URDF are consistent with the CAD model. The acceptance criterion for neighbor card P6.2.1 is "IK success rate > 99% for 1000 random poses, position error < 1 mm"—this effectively tests whether URDF parameters accurately reproduce the design intent.

③ Workspace and reachability analysis (P6.2.2 subtask): Based on the URDF model, use Monte Carlo sampling to generate reachable workspace point clouds for the foot/hand tip, verifying coverage of P2.2.1 ergonomic requirements. Key constraints include self-collision constraints and joint limits.

④ State estimator development (P10.1.1): IMU and joint encoder fusion algorithms require an accurate kinematic model as the prediction equation; the joint positions and link lengths provided by URDF form the basis of the state transition matrix.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Tree-structure limitation: URDF does not support describing closed kinematic chains. For humanoid robots with parallel mechanisms (such as certain ankle joint designs), additional handling is required.

② Dynamics parameter accuracy: Inertial parameters in URDF come from CAD estimation or separate calibration and still deviate from the physical robot. For highly dynamic motions (such as running, jumping), this deviation can cause significant differences between simulation and real-robot behavior.

③ No sensor models: URDF does not include installation positions and noise characteristics for sensors (IMU, force sensors, etc.); this requires additional plugins or extension formats.

④ Version management challenges: URDF files have implicit associations with CAD models and BOM lists. When design changes occur, if the URDF is not updated synchronously, downstream algorithms may be developed based on outdated models.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "URDF is just another export format of the CAD model"—Wrong. URDF is a structured description oriented toward computation, requiring manual judgment on which geometries enter the collision bodies, how coordinate systems are defined, and how inertial parameters are calibrated. Direct export often fails to meet simulation requirements.

  2. "The finer the collision bodies, the better"—Wrong. Overly dense collision bodies cause a dramatic increase in simulation computational cost and can even lead to solver non-convergence. Engineering judgment: collision bodies only need moderate precision in key contact areas (foot sole, palm), while simplified geometry suffices for other parts.

  3. "Successful URDF loading means the model is correct"—Wrong. Successful loading only indicates that the XML syntax is correct and geometry references are complete; it does not mean the kinematic parameters are consistent with the physical prototype. Closed-loop verification through P6.2 kinematics verification (IK success rate, position error) is mandatory.

  4. "Inertial parameters can directly use CAD defaults"—Wrong. Density settings and simplified modeling approaches in CAD introduce errors; trustworthy data must be obtained through the P3.1.2/P3.1.3 calibration process.
