Overview

Structural material selection is a critical process node (P5.2.1) in the humanoid robot body structure engineering and prototyping (P5) phase. Its task, after the structural design of the central skeleton, limb linkages, joint mounting interfaces, and exterior cover panels is complete, is to determine the material and process combination for each component by comparing candidate materials such as aluminum alloys 7075/6061, carbon fiber, titanium alloy, and engineering plastics across strength, stiffness, density, cost, and mass-production feasibility, ultimately outputting a "Material Selection Table." What it truly changes is not the simple decision of "choosing a material," but rather the systematic process by which "design intent is first translated into the physical language of stiffness, weight, tolerances, and process constraints"—this table will directly determine whether the mesh and load assumptions in the subsequent P8 structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA) hold, and whether the DFM/DFA review in P16 pilot production and production ramp-up (Pilot & Production Ramp) can pass.

Content

What It Is: Definition and Boundaries of Structural Material Selection

Structural material selection is a level-three subtask under the P5 body structure engineering and prototyping (Mechanical Structure) phase, numbered P5.2.1. It follows P5.1 structural design (central skeleton and limb linkage design P5.1.1, joint mounting interface and housing design P5.1.2, exterior cover panel and parting line design P5.1.3) and runs in parallel with Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM, P5.2.2) and mass-production process evaluation (P5.2.3), together forming the P5.2 materials and processes work package.

Its responsibility boundary is very clear: it does not handle structural topology optimization (that is P5.1's job), does not handle additive manufacturing process details (that is P5.2.2's job), and does not handle mass-production line planning (that is P16's job). It answers only one question: Given the specified structural shape and load conditions, what material and what process should each component be manufactured with, and why.

The output is the "Material Selection Table," where each row corresponds to a component and contains three core columns of information: material, process, and rationale. This table is the material prerequisite for all subsequent work—P8 structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA) needs the material's elastic modulus and yield strength to set boundary conditions, and P16 pilot production and production ramp-up (Pilot & Production Ramp) needs the material and process to conduct the DFM/DFA review.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the early stages of humanoid robot development, structural material selection was often treated as a supplementary task "filled in casually after drawing completion." Engineers chose materials based on experience and set processes by feel, only to find insufficient stiffness during the P8 simulation phase or cost overruns or unavailable supply chain materials during the P16 pilot production phase, forcing a redesign—the cost of one rework far exceeds the cost of spending two extra weeks on systematic evaluation during the selection phase.

The root cause of this pain point is that humanoid robot structural components simultaneously bear multiple loads—static loads when standing, dynamic loads when walking, and impact loads when falling—and are extremely weight-sensitive (weight directly affects joint motor selection and battery life). A selection error is not a "slightly inferior material" problem, but rather a "the entire robot's dynamic performance is dragged down" problem.

Therefore, P5.2.1 is designed as a five-step closed-loop formal process: input review and target quantification (P5.2.1.1) → candidate solution development and evaluation (P5.2.1.2) → implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P5.2.1.3) → verification and issue closure (P5.2.1.4) → documentation output and downstream delivery (P5.2.1.5). Each step has clearly defined level-four key actions, ensuring the selection process is traceable, reviewable, and quantifiable.

Principle Breakdown

① Input Review and Target Quantification (P5.2.1.1)

The first step of selection is not choosing a material, but translating "completion criteria" into "quantifiable KPIs." This step includes three level-four key actions:

  1. List all upstream input items and confirm versions—upstream inputs include the 3D structural model, key cross-section designs, and initial BOM from P5.1, as well as the load spectrum, weight budget, and cost targets from the requirements document. Confirming versions prevents "selecting new materials with an old model" during subsequent iterations.
  2. Convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs—for example, "linkage stiffness" becomes "maximum deformation does not exceed 0.5 mm (engineering judgment inferred from corpus data)," "weight" becomes "single thigh linkage mass does not exceed 1.2 kg (to be confirmed independently)," and "cost" becomes "per-unit structural BOM cost does not exceed X yuan (to be confirmed independently)."
  3. Establish task Owner, timeline, and risk register—clarify who is responsible for the selection outcome, when the solution must be frozen, and which risks (e.g., supply chain disruptions, low process yields) need to be registered in advance.

② Candidate Solution Development and Evaluation (P5.2.1.2)

The core of this step is the mandatory requirement of "no fewer than 2 candidate solutions"—selecting a single material based on experience alone is not acceptable. The candidate solution library includes at least four material categories: aluminum alloys 7075/6061, carbon fiber, titanium alloy, and engineering plastics. Evaluation dimensions include cost, performance, supply chain, and maintainability.

The quantitative scoring logic of the evaluation matrix can be expressed as:

\[ S_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i} w_i \cdot s_i \]

where \( S_{\text{total}} \) is the comprehensive solution score, \( w_i \) is the weight of the \( i \)-th evaluation dimension (determined by the KPIs from P5.2.1.1), and \( s_i \) is the score for the \( i \)-th dimension (on a 1–5 scale, scored by the engineering team based on material data sheets and supplier quotes). The solution with the highest final score enters the review stage, where the solution is reviewed and frozen.

③ Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication (P5.2.1.3)

After the solution is frozen, the next step is not directly producing the table, but fabricating a prototype or sample to verify feasibility. This step includes three level-four key actions: build a model/prototype and record key parameters, execute simulation or prototype verification, and record anomalies and deviations. For example, if the selection table states "high-load linkage uses aluminum alloy/CNC," then at least one CNC-machined aluminum alloy linkage sample must be produced, with its stiffness and weight measured to confirm consistency with theoretical calculations.

④ Verification and Issue Closure (P5.2.1.4)

The verification stage requires developing a test/review plan and pass criteria, executing tests and recording raw data, and outputting an issue list with improvement measures. Pass criteria come from the KPIs in P5.2.1.1—if measured stiffness does not meet requirements, either change the material, modify the structure, or adjust the KPIs (requiring a change process).

⑤ Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery (P5.2.1.5)

The final output is the "Material Selection Table," with documentation written according to the template, raw data referenced, internal review and version control completed, and publication with notification to downstream dependents. Downstream includes P8 structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA) and P16 pilot production and production ramp-up (Pilot & Production Ramp).

P5.2.1 is the pivotal hub that connects upstream and downstream: it translates P5.1's "shape" into "material + process" and provides material parameters for P5.2.2, P5.2.3, and P8. Without it, all subsequent simulations and process evaluations lack boundary conditions.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

In the full humanoid robot product development process, typical application scenarios for P5.2.1 include:

  • Central skeleton and limb linkages: High-load linkages use aluminum alloys 7075/6061 with CNC machining to ensure stiffness and precision; if the weight budget is tight, titanium alloy substitution can be evaluated (cost increment to be confirmed independently).
  • Joint mounting interfaces and housings: Medium-load structural components use aluminum alloy 6061, balancing strength and machinability.
  • Exterior cover panels and parting line design: Shells/brackets use SLS/MJF 3D printing or injection-molded engineering plastics to reduce weight and cost, while enabling rapid iteration of exterior styling.

In the WBS process, the output of P5.2.1 feeds directly into P5.2.2 Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM)—if the selection table determines that a component will be SLS-printed, DfAM must optimize wall thickness, fillets, and reinforcing ribs; it also feeds into P8 structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA), where the mesh and load assumptions must match the wall thickness, fillets, and reinforcing ribs achievable by the subsequent process (from corpus).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. The selection table is not the final design: The "Material Selection Table" only determines "material + process + rationale" and does not include specific wall thickness, fillets, reinforcing ribs, and other details—these are determined by P5.2.2 DfAM and P8 simulation iterations.
  2. Material data depends on suppliers: Parameters such as yield strength and density in the table come from material data sheets; actual batches may deviate and must be verified through physical testing during the P5.2.1.3 sample fabrication stage.
  3. Cost data is time-sensitive: Raw material prices fluctuate significantly; cost evaluations during selection may become invalid at the mass-production stage (P16) and must be recalculated during P16.2.1 supplier selection and review.
  4. Process details are not covered: CNC tool paths, injection mold design, and 3D printing layer thickness and orientation are outside the scope of P5.2.1, handled by P5.2.2 and P16.1.2 mold and tooling design.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The more expensive the material, the better"—wrong. Titanium alloy has a high strength-to-weight ratio but high cost and difficult machinability; it is reasonable for high-load critical components but wasteful for shells. The essence of selection is "finding the optimal solution under KPI constraints," not "choosing the most expensive."
  2. "Done once the material is selected"—wrong. P5.2.1 is a five-step closed loop; sample verification (P5.2.1.3) and test closure (P5.2.1.4) must be completed before delivery. Skipping verification and directly outputting the table pushes risk onto P8 and P16.
  3. "The more candidate solutions, the better"—wrong. The corpus requires "no fewer than 2 candidate solutions" to ensure comparison, not to stack unlimited solutions. The weights in the evaluation matrix come from the KPIs in P5.2.1.1; too many solutions dilute the review focus.
  4. "The selection table is a final document"—wrong. The selection table requires version control; when upstream design changes (e.g., increased loads) or supply chain changes occur (e.g., a material becomes unavailable), the change process must be followed to update the selection table and notify downstream.
