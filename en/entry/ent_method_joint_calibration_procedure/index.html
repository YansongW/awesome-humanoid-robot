Overview

The Joint Calibration Procedure is a systematic method for determining the absolute zero position, encoder offset, and direction flag of each actuator after assembly. It addresses the critical, non-skippable step in transitioning a robot from "parts assembled" to "capable of precise motion"—without it, there is no basis for understanding how many turns the motor has made or what angle a joint is at. What it truly changes is not the operational methods on the assembly floor, but the credibility of the kinematic model as it moves from paper to physical reality.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Joint Calibration Procedure falls under the method category, with the English name Joint Calibration Procedure. Its core tasks can be broken down into three parts:

  • Absolute zero position: The reference angular position on the joint's mechanical structure, typically determined by mechanical limits, alignment pins, or zero marks.
  • Encoder offset: The fixed difference between the motor or joint encoder reading and the mechanical zero position. Since encoder readings are random at power-up, a mapping from "electrical reading → mechanical angle" must be established through calibration.
  • Direction flag: An indicator of consistency between the encoder counting direction and the actual joint rotation direction. A reversed direction can cause positive feedback, leading to instantaneous divergence of the control system.

This procedure belongs to both assembly integration testing and design engineering, occupying a mid-stream position in the value chain—it connects upstream component manufacturing with downstream system commissioning, serving as the dividing line between a robot that "can rotate" and one that "rotates accurately."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Before the Joint Calibration Procedure existed, post-assembly robot tuning relied on manual visual inspection and empirical judgment. Operators manually rotated joints to a visually estimated "zero position" and then recorded the current encoder reading as the offset in the controller. This approach had three fundamental pain points:

First, precision was uncontrollable. The repeatability of manual visual inspection is typically on the order of several degrees, whereas modern humanoid robot motion planning requires joint angle errors within 0.1° (engineering judgment). For a 300 mm link, a 0.1° angular error produces approximately 0.5 mm of positional deviation at the end effector (following the extrapolation logic of "±0.1 mm deviation amplified to ±(n×0.1) mm" from the source material).

Second, efficiency was low. A full-size humanoid robot has 20–40 joints (engineering judgment), and manually calibrating each one takes hours, with a high risk of omissions or repetitions.

Third, traceability was poor. Manual calibration results depended on operator records, making standardization, verification, and automatic integration with kinematic models difficult.

From a historical perspective, the Joint Calibration Procedure is an inevitable product of the robotics industry's transition from "laboratory prototypes" to "mass-produced products." It borrows mature kinematic calibration methods from the industrial manipulator field but has been specifically adapted for the high degrees of freedom, floating base, and multiple closed kinematic chains characteristic of humanoid robots.

Principle Breakdown

① Mapping Between Encoder Readings and Mechanical Angles

The mathematical essence of joint calibration is establishing an affine transformation between the raw encoder reading \( \theta_{\text{raw}} \) and the mechanical joint angle \( \theta_{\text{joint}} \):

\[ \theta_{\text{joint}} = s \cdot \theta_{\text{raw}} + \theta_{\text{offset}} \]

where \( s \) is the direction flag (+1 or -1) and \( \theta_{\text{offset}} \) is the encoder offset. The goal of the calibration procedure is to determine these two parameters.

② Relationship with Kinematic Calibration

Joint calibration is a prerequisite step for kinematic calibration. Kinematic calibration identifies true geometric parameters through external measurement data, with the core equation:

\[ \Delta \mathbf{p} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial a_i} \Delta a_i + \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial \alpha_i} \Delta \alpha_i + \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial d_i} \Delta d_i + \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}}{\partial \theta_i} \Delta \theta_i \right) = \mathbf{J}_{\text{cal}} \, \Delta \boldsymbol{\phi} \]

where \( \mathbf{J}_{\text{cal}} \) is the calibration Jacobian and \( \Delta \boldsymbol{\phi} \) is the vector of parameter errors to be identified. If the joint zero position itself is inaccurate, the \( \Delta \theta_i \) term will contaminate the entire identification process—this is why joint calibration must be completed before kinematic calibration.

③ Identifiability Constraints

Joint calibration also faces the identifiability problem: certain parameter errors have minimal impact on the end-effector pose, or are coupled with joint zero errors, making independent identification impossible. For humanoid robots, due to the floating base and multiple closed kinematic chains (both legs in contact with the ground simultaneously), the calibration problem is more complex than for fixed-base manipulators and typically requires a combination of IMU, vision, and motion capture systems.

Horizontal Comparison

Joint calibration and kinematic calibration are two processes at different levels that are often confused:

Dimension Joint Calibration Kinematic Calibration
Calibration target Zero position, offset, direction of individual joints Whole-robot kinematic parameters (link lengths, twist angles, etc.)
Measurement tools Mechanical limits, alignment pins, encoder readings Laser trackers, coordinate measuring machines, vision measurement
Output Offset and direction flag for each joint Corrected DH or PoE parameters
Timing After assembly, before power-on debugging After full robot assembly, before shipment
Precision impact Absolute accuracy of joint angles Absolute positioning accuracy of the end effector

The two are progressive: joint calibration is the prerequisite for kinematic calibration, and kinematic calibration is an extension of joint calibration. High-end humanoid robots typically undergo a complete kinematic calibration process before shipment, which can reduce absolute end-effector errors from several millimeters to ±0.3 mm or even lower.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Unitree H1 Humanoid Robot (ent_robot_unitree_h1_humanoid_robot_2024) is a typical application case of the Joint Calibration Procedure. The H1 uses Unitree's self-developed M107 permanent magnet synchronous motor, with a peak knee joint torque of 360 N·m and a peak torque density of approximately 189 N·m/kg. Its 4 DOF basic version arm (upgradeable to 7 DOF with the H1-2 option) must undergo joint calibration for each joint after assembly to achieve precise motion control at a running speed of 3.3 m/s.

In the H1's calibration procedure, each joint module (a standardized unit integrating motor, reducer, and sensors) requires:

  1. Determining the absolute zero position via mechanical limits or alignment pins;
  2. Recording the current encoder reading as the offset;
  3. Verifying the direction flag to ensure positive feedback cannot occur;
  4. Writing the calibration results to the controller parameter file for use by the kinematic model.

Limitations and Boundaries

First, temperature sensitivity. Joint calibration is performed at room temperature, but temperature increases during robot operation cause thermal expansion of mechanical structures, leading to zero drift. For high-precision applications, temperature compensation or periodic recalibration is required.

Second, mechanical wear. Reducer eccentricity and bearing clearance increase with usage time, gradually invalidating initial calibration results. This explains why industrial robots typically require periodic recalibration.

Third, non-identifiable parameters. Certain parameter errors have minimal impact on end-effector pose, or are coupled with joint zero errors, making independent identification impossible. Methods to improve identifiability include: selecting measurement configurations that yield a well-conditioned calibration Jacobian, fixing certain known parameters, and employing closed-loop kinematic chain constraints.

Fourth, floating base challenges. Humanoid robots have a floating base and multiple closed kinematic chains (both legs in contact with the ground simultaneously), making the calibration problem more complex than for fixed-base manipulators. This typically requires combining IMU, vision, and motion capture systems.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Joint calibration is the same as kinematic calibration"—Wrong. Joint calibration only addresses the zero position, offset, and direction of each joint; it does not involve identifying geometric parameters such as link lengths or twist angles. The latter falls under kinematic calibration and requires external measurement equipment.

  2. "High encoder resolution means calibration is unnecessary"—Wrong. Encoder resolution determines the lower bound of measurement precision, but offset and direction are systematic fixed errors that cannot be eliminated without calibration. High-resolution encoders only make calibration results more precise; they cannot replace calibration itself.

  3. "Calibrate once, valid forever"—Wrong. Temperature changes, mechanical wear, and reducer eccentricity all cause calibration results to gradually degrade. For high-precision applications, periodic recalibration is required.

  4. "The direction flag doesn't matter; it can be changed in software anyway"—Wrong. A reversed direction causes positive feedback, leading to instantaneous divergence of the control system. Although it can theoretically be reversed in software, if not detected during the calibration phase, debugging will waste significant time troubleshooting.
