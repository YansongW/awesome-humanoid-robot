Overview

A Rotary Actuator is an actuator that produces rotational motion, typically composed of a frameless torque motor, harmonic reducer, encoder, and torque sensor. It is the core drive unit for joint motion in humanoid robots, and its performance directly determines the overall machine's dynamic response, precision, and load capacity.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Rotary actuators belong to the component category, within the domain of components (02_components), and sit at the upstream level of the value chain. They are a class of mechatronic devices that convert electrical energy into rotational mechanical energy, with a typical structure comprising a frameless torque motor, harmonic reducer, encoder, and torque sensor. The English name is Rotary Actuator, and the Korean name is Rotary Actuator.

In the humanoid robot industry chain, rotary actuators occupy the upstream component position, serving as the critical link between raw materials (such as neodymium-iron-boron magnets) and the complete robot system. Their upstream directly depends on frameless torque motors and harmonic reducers, while downstream they serve the joint drive of complete robot systems.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before rotary actuators became the mainstream solution, humanoid robot joint drive faced threefold challenges:

① The contradiction between motor speed and joint requirements. Motors typically operate at high speeds in their efficient range, while humanoid robot joints require high-torque, low-speed output. Direct drive would make the motor excessively large and heavy, failing to meet the power density requirements of the complete machine.

② The introduction of reducers brought new precision issues. Traditional reducers (such as planetary reducers) exhibit backlash, friction, and elastic deformation. These nonlinear factors significantly reduce control precision in force-control scenarios, preventing robots from performing delicate operations.

③ The lack of torque sensing. Early solutions relied on current estimation for torque, but the presence of friction and backlash made estimation errors unacceptable, limiting the implementation of force-control algorithms.

The emergence of rotary actuators is essentially an engineering compromise addressing the above contradictions: what they truly changed is not the motor itself, but the fact that "motor + reducer + sensing" was redesigned as an integrated whole—by selecting low-ratio, high-precision harmonic reducers, paired with frameless torque motors and high-resolution encoders, nonlinear factors are compressed into a controllable range while retaining sufficient torque output.

Principle Breakdown

① Frameless Torque Motor: The Source of Torque

The frameless torque motor is the power core of a rotary actuator. Compared with framed servo motors, it has no housing or bearings and is integrated directly into the joint structure, significantly reducing weight and volume. Its rotor uses neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnets, and the stator windings are driven via field-oriented control (FOC), with output torque proportional to the q-axis current:

\[ \tau_m = \frac{3}{2} p \, \lambda_f \, i_q \]

where \(p\) is the number of pole pairs, \(\lambda_f\) is the permanent magnet flux linkage, and \(i_q\) is the quadrature-axis current. This formula reveals the essence of the frameless torque motor: torque control is current control, and FOC is the core method for achieving this control.

② Harmonic Reducer: The Balance of Precision and Reduction

The harmonic reducer achieves speed reduction through the elastic deformation of the flexspline, with reduction ratios typically between 50–160 (engineering judgment). It features zero backlash, high transmission precision, and a compact structure. Its core advantages include:

  • Zero backlash: Eliminates return error found in traditional gear transmission, enabling more precise torque control
  • High reduction ratio: Achieves large reduction ratios within a compact volume, meeting joint torque requirements
  • Coaxial input and output: Simplifies mechanical structure design

③ Encoder and Torque Sensor: The Sensing Closed Loop

A high-resolution encoder (typically magnetic or optical) provides rotor position feedback and is a prerequisite for FOC control. A torque sensor (optional) directly measures joint output torque, providing true feedback for force-control algorithms. In solutions without a torque sensor, torque can be estimated from current, but accuracy is affected by friction and temperature (engineering judgment).

④ Signal Flow and Control Interface

The internal signal flow of a rotary actuator is: encoder position → controller (FOC current loop + velocity loop + position loop) → inverter → motor. External interfaces include:

  • Communication bus: Real-time buses such as EtherCAT, CAN (specific models to be confirmed)
  • Power interface: High-voltage DC bus (typically 48V–600V, to be confirmed)
  • Control interface: Switching among position/velocity/torque modes

The core advantage of rotary actuators lies in achieving the best balance between torque density and force-control precision. Direct-drive solutions offer the highest force-control precision but insufficient torque density, while planetary reduction solutions provide acceptable torque density but backlash limits force-control precision. Through the zero-backlash characteristic of harmonic reducers, rotary actuators achieve force-control precision approaching that of direct drive while maintaining high torque density.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Tesla Optimus Gen 3

Building on the 173 cm torso of Gen 2, Tesla Optimus Gen 3 increases single-hand degrees of freedom from 11 DOF to 22 DOF, with 25 actuators per hand and 50 actuators across both hands. These actuators extensively adopt the rotary actuator solution, significantly enhancing fine grasping and tool manipulation capabilities.

② Unitree H1

Unitree H1 uses the self-developed M107 permanent magnet synchronous motor, with a knee joint peak torque of 360 N·m and a peak torque density of approximately 189 N·m/kg. It once set a running speed record of 3.3 m/s for a full-size humanoid robot. Its joint drive solution is based on the rotary actuator architecture.

③ Sanhua Intelligent Controls

As a Tier 1 supplier, Sanhua Intelligent Controls leverages its precision valve, pump, and actuator technologies to extend into robotic mechatronic actuators, with its product line covering robotic thermal management and mechatronic actuator fields.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Upper limit of torque density

Constrained by the magnetic energy product of permanent magnet materials (neodymium-iron-boron) and the mechanical strength of harmonic reducers, the torque density of rotary actuators has a physical upper limit. Further improvement requires new materials or structural innovations.

② Thermal management challenges

High power density implies high heat generation. Rotary actuators require effective thermal management solutions under sustained high-load conditions; otherwise, temperature rise can cause permanent magnet demagnetization or lubrication failure.

③ Cost and supply chain

The manufacturing costs of harmonic reducers and frameless torque motors are relatively high, and the supply chain is also highly concentrated (e.g., Harmonic Drive Systems dominates the harmonic reducer field). This limits the adoption of rotary actuators in low-cost robots.

④ Physical limits of force-control precision

Even with zero-backlash harmonic reducers, the elastic deformation of the flexspline, bearing friction, and temperature drift still introduce nonlinear errors. For scenarios requiring extremely high force-control precision (such as precision assembly), additional torque sensors or more complex compensation algorithms may be necessary.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A rotary actuator is just a motor + reducer"—Wrong. It is an integrated mechatronic unit containing an encoder, torque sensor (optional), drive circuitry, and communication interfaces. It is a "plug-and-play" intelligent component, not a simple mechanical combination.

  2. "Zero backlash in harmonic reducers = zero error"—Wrong. Zero backlash eliminates gear return error, but the elastic deformation of the flexspline (limited torsional stiffness) still produces angular deviation under load, which must be compensated through control algorithms.

  3. "Frameless torque motor = direct-drive motor"—Wrong. Frameless torque motors still require a reducer; "frameless" refers to the mechanical structure (no housing, no bearings), not "no reduction."

  4. "Higher torque density is always better"—Wrong. High torque density typically means high heat generation and low overload capacity, requiring a trade-off among torque density, thermal management, and reliability.
