Summary

Long-horizon robot manipulation policies trained with reward shaping can still achieve high return through inefficient interactions, while rare efficient behaviors discovered during training may be forgotten. Institutions per source list: Duke University、General Robotics Lab.

Overview

This paper proposes the Time Self-Imitation Learning (TSIL) framework to address the issue of long-horizon robot manipulation policies being misled by inefficient behaviors under dense rewards. TSIL converts fast successful trajectories discovered during training into reusable supervision signals through configuration-conditioned adaptive time targets and efficiency-weighted self-imitation replay, significantly improving success rates and behavioral efficiency across 15 Meta-World tasks.

What It Changes

The reward shaping dilemma for long-horizon manipulation policies lies in the fact that dense rewards guide policies toward "collecting rewards" rather than "efficiently completing tasks," while sparse success rewards weaken guidance and increase variance. Existing remedies—per-step penalties, dense-to-sparse scheduling, fixed time limits—all rely on manually designed temporal preferences and fail to distinguish between "high-return but inefficient" and "fast success" behaviors. What TSIL truly changes is elevating temporal efficiency from a "deployment preference" to a "learning signal": it no longer assumes the reward function can encode efficiency preferences but instead directly mines efficiency information from the policy's own historical successful trajectories, allowing targets to adaptively tighten as learning progresses. This shift transforms efficiency optimization from an "external constraint" to an "internal emergence," avoiding sensitive tuning of manual schedules and penalty strengths.

Method Breakdown

Configuration-Conditioned Adaptive Time Targets

  • Observation extended to õ_t = (o_t, T^used_t, D_i(φ)), where T^used_t is the elapsed time and D_i(φ) is the time target for the current configuration φ
  • Target update: D_{i+1}(φ) = min(D_i(φ), min_{τ∈T_i(φ), 1_succ(τ)=1} T^succ(τ)), initialized to T^max
  • Targets are not hard constraints; rollouts can exceed D(φ) until completion or truncation at T^max

Target-Conditioned Rewards

  • Success reward: r_K^succ = r_succ · 1_succ · [1 + min(D_i(φ)/T^used, 1)], achieving completion before the target yields maximum temporal reward
  • Total reward: r_t = r_t^task + r_t^succ

Efficiency-Weighted Self-Imitation Replay

  • Each configuration maintains a top-k (k=5) buffer of fastest successful trajectories, filled with high-return trajectories when insufficient early on
  • Before replay, observations and rewards are re-annotated with the current target D_i; return computation includes truncated bootstrapping: G^τ_t = Σ γ^{j-t} r^τ_j + γ^{|τ|-t}(1-d_τ)V_ψ(ô^τ_{|τ|})
  • Trajectory-level sampling prevents fast successful trajectories from being diluted by uniform transition sampling
  • Trajectory weights: w_i(τ) = 1 + 1_succ(τ) · min(D_i(φ^τ)/T^succ(τ), 1)
  • Auxiliary loss: L_SI = E[−w_i(τ) A_t^+(τ) log π_θ(a_t^τ | ô_t^τ) + (λ_V,SI/2)(A_t^+(τ))²], where A_t^+(τ) = [Ĝ_t^τ − V_ψ(ô_t^τ)]_+ is a soft gate

Implementation Details

  • Based on PPO, five-layer MLP [512,256,128,64,32], tanh activation + LayerNorm
  • Temporal features exclude running-statistic normalization, instead scaled by dividing by T^max
  • Each parallel environment independently maintains its configuration, time target, and replay buffer

Key Innovations

  1. Configuration-Conditioned Adaptive Time Targets: Unlike fixed time limits or global targets, TSIL maintains targets independently per task configuration and automatically tightens them as faster solutions emerge. This addresses the issue of large variations in feasible completion times across different initial conditions for the same task, avoiding manual schedule or penalty specification.

  2. Efficiency-Weighted Self-Imitation Replay: Existing SIL methods use high-return experiences but do not distinguish between fast and slow successes. TSIL uses speed-priority weights and trajectory-level sampling to focus replay pressure on fast successful behaviors. The positive-advantage soft gate ensures replay pressure disappears when the critic deems the current policy already matches stored behaviors, avoiding over-constraint.

  3. Temporal Efficiency as a Learning Signal Rather Than a Deployment Preference: The core assumption is that fast successful trajectories contain information that rewards alone cannot isolate. By conditioning time targets into observations and rewards, the policy learns not only "what to do" but also "how fast to do it," and this signal comes entirely from the policy's own experience without requiring external temporal preference design.

Key implications: TSIL achieves the best overall performance in success rate, AUC, and completion time, with significantly better robustness to policy gradient noise compared to variants using only adaptive targets or generic replay. Fixed time targets (FTTL) yield the worst completion time (1.448), indicating that fixed temporal constraints actually encourage inefficient behaviors.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • TSIL does not solve the initial exploration problem: it only improves exploitation and consolidation after rare successful trajectories emerge; the base learner still requires sufficient exploration or task guidance to discover the first success
  • Temporal efficiency is the only behavioral signal considered; if deployment requires slower, safer, or smoother behaviors, additional metrics must be incorporated
  • Successful trajectories must be stored in memory; currently uses a small fixed-size buffer (k=5), with more memory-efficient replay and compression unexplored
  • No real-robot experiments were conducted, only simulation environments (Isaac Gym); no comparison with model-based or offline RL methods
  • The paper does not explicitly mention hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (e.g., scans of λ_SI, λ_V,SI)

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, prioritize three key points: Temporal feature normalization—elapsed time and targets must exclude running-statistic normalization, instead scaled by dividing by T^max, otherwise target conditioning fails; Trajectory-level sampling—sampling must be done per trajectory rather than uniform transition sampling, otherwise fast successful trajectories are diluted by numerous ordinary transitions; Replay buffer filling strategy—when early successful trajectories are fewer than k, fill with high-return trajectories, otherwise minibatch updates become unstable. The most common pitfall is configuration conditioning in the target update formula: each parallel environment must have a fixed configuration and independently maintain its target and buffer; cross-environment sharing leads to target confusion. For downstream teams planning deployment, it is recommended to first verify whether the base PPO can discover initial successes on the task (TSIL does not address exploration), then introduce TSIL to improve efficiency; in perturbation experiments, TSIL shows the strongest robustness to policy gradient noise, making it suitable for unstable training scenarios.
