Overview

Leg system integration and debugging is the first subsystem integration process in the humanoid robot full-machine integration and verification testing phase (P15). Its core task is to integrate the leg joints, drives, sensing, and control systems before full-machine joint debugging, and to verify joint coordination and control performance through single-leg test benches, standing, stepping, and other actions. What it truly changes is not "attaching the legs," but rather exposing and closing the loop on the most critical physical risk of the full machine—"whether the legs can stably support and walk"—independently before full-machine joint debugging. This method uses single-leg test benches, standing, stepping, and joint coordination as primary tools, with single-leg/dual-leg standing stability and joint tracking error below target values as core acceptance criteria. It serves as the pivotal process connecting subsystem component verification and full-machine safety testing.

Content

What It Is: From "Components Correct" to "Legs Correct as a System"

Leg system integration and debugging refers to integrating the mechanical structures, joint motors and reducers, drivers, encoders/IMU sensing units, and low-level control algorithms involved in the legs into an independently operable physical subsystem before full humanoid robot assembly, and verifying whether this subsystem meets design goals through dedicated test benches and action sequences.

What it truly changes is not the torque of a specific joint or the accuracy of a specific sensor, but the granularity of verification—advancing from "each component passes in its own test environment" to "the legs work coordinately as a complete subsystem in the physical world." In the full-machine integration phase, the legs are the core of balance and walking, so they must be verified independently before entering full-machine joint debugging. In WBS V3, this process is located in the P15 full-machine integration and verification testing phase, alongside arm and hand integration (P15.1.2) and perception-planning-control closed-loop integration (P15.1.3), collectively forming the first half of subsystem integration; it is followed by safety function testing (P15.2.1), performance benchmark testing (P15.2.2), and environmental adaptability testing (P15.2.3).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the development history of bipedal humanoid robots, the leg system has long been the subsystem with the highest failure rate. Its pain points have three layers:

The first layer is physical risk front-loading. During full-machine joint debugging, if leg control is unstable, a robot fall can damage not only the legs themselves but also the arms, torso sensors, and even surrounding equipment. By testing the legs independently on a test bench, safety harnesses and limit protections can confine physical risks to a controllable range.

The second layer is difficulty in problem attribution. In the full-machine state, a phenomenon of unstable standing may stem from multiple factors, including leg joint tracking errors, torso IMU noise, planning-layer gait parameters, and even ground friction coefficients. If leg-specific issues are not ruled out at the subsystem level first, fault localization during full-machine joint debugging will fall into a quagmire of "full-chain troubleshooting."

The third layer is the independence of control parameter tuning. The PID/feedforward parameters, torque limits, and compliance control gains of leg joints need to be tuned independently without full-machine interference. Tuning in the full-machine state would allow torso sway to contaminate the frequency response identification data of the leg control loop.

From a historical positioning perspective, leg system integration and debugging is the first third-level subtask (P15.1.1) of the P15 full-machine integration and verification testing phase. Its upstream is the assembly and individual component testing of leg parts, and its downstream is arm and hand integration (P15.1.2) and perception-planning-control closed-loop integration (P15.1.3). It serves as the "first gate for full-machine safety"—if the legs cannot stand stably, all subsequent full-machine-level tests lack a safe physical foundation.

Principle Breakdown

① Single-Leg Test Bench: Reducing the Full-Machine Problem to a Single-Leg Problem

The core principle of the single-leg test bench is constraint dimensionality reduction. The robot is fixed to the test bench, with a safety harness bearing part of the weight and limit protections restricting joint motion ranges, thereby individually exciting each leg joint while eliminating the risk of full-machine tipping. At this point, the input to the leg control loop is a clear desired joint trajectory, the output is the actual joint response, and the difference between the two is the joint tracking error.

② Standing and Stepping: Progressive Verification from Static to Dynamic Stability

Standing verifies the leg's position-holding capability under static or quasi-static conditions, with core metrics being whether the center of mass projection falls within the support polygon and whether joint tracking error is below the target value. Stepping introduces dynamic effects—the switching between single-leg support phase and swing phase—requiring the control loop to provide sufficient stiffness to resist gravitational torque during the support phase and sufficient compliance to complete the leg swing during the swing phase. Joint coordination is explicitly tested in this process: if there is deviation in the temporal coordination of the hip, knee, and ankle joints, it will directly manifest as distortion in the stepping trajectory or center of mass sway.

③ Quantification of Joint Tracking Error

Joint tracking error is the core quantitative metric of leg system integration and debugging. It is defined as the deviation between the desired joint angle and the actual joint angle:

\[ e_{\text{track}} = \theta_{\text{desired}} - \theta_{\text{actual}} \]

Under standing conditions, this error should approach zero; under stepping conditions, the peak value of this error should be below the target value (the specific target value needs to be confirmed independently). In engineering practice, the mean, peak, and root mean square values of the error are typically monitored simultaneously to distinguish steady-state deviation from dynamic lag.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key parameters of leg system integration and debugging can be divided into four categories:

Parameter Category Specific Parameter Description
Mechanical Constraints Joint motion range limits Implemented via limit protections to prevent over-travel damage
Safety Constraints Safety harness load-bearing ratio Needs independent confirmation; typically partial body weight support
Control Performance Joint tracking error Should be below target value under both standing and stepping conditions (needs independent confirmation)
Test Conditions Standing/stepping duration and repetitions Needs independent confirmation; should cover both static and dynamic conditions

Among the above parameters, joint tracking error is the core quantitative acceptance metric, while the remaining parameters serve the safety and repeatability of testing.

From the comparison, it is evident that P15.1.1 is the subsystem integration process with the highest physical risk but the clearest verification boundary among the three—its verification object is "the legs themselves," rather than "the interaction between the legs and the external world."

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Leg system integration and debugging is a standard process in bipedal humanoid robot development, widely applied in the following scenarios:

  • Humanoid robot laboratories in universities and research institutions: Before full-machine joint debugging, new leg structures and control algorithms are verified on single-leg test benches to reduce the physical risk of full-machine testing.
  • Engineering development workflows of humanoid robot startups: Under the WBS V3 framework, P15.1.1 is the first process entering the full-machine integration and verification testing phase, and its completion quality directly affects the progress of subsequent arm integration and closed-loop integration.
  • Development of bipedal platforms for industrial inspection/logistics scenarios: Leg stability is a prerequisite for bipedal platforms walking on unstructured terrain, and the standing and stepping verification in P15.1.1 lays the foundation for subsequent complex terrain walking tests.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary of leg system integration and debugging is: it only verifies the legs themselves, not the coupling between the legs and the full machine. Specific limitations include:

  1. Lack of full-machine center of mass influence: On the single-leg test bench, the mass distribution of the torso and arms differs from the full-machine state, resulting in different load characteristics on the legs. Passing standing and stepping verification does not guarantee leg stability in the full-machine state.
  2. Lack of ground interaction verification: The ground conditions of the test bench differ from real ground (friction coefficient, hardness, flatness), so leg performance on the test bench cannot be fully extrapolated to real scenarios.
  3. Lack of perception closed loop: P15.1.1 does not involve the closed loop between vision/IMU and leg control, whereas full-machine walking relies on the full-chain coordination of perception-planning-control.

Therefore, the completion criterion for P15.1.1 is "the legs themselves meet design requirements," not "the legs meet design requirements within the full machine." The latter is verified by P15.1.3 perception-planning-control closed-loop integration and the P15.2 series of tests.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Passing leg integration debugging = no problems with full-machine walking"—Wrong. P15.1.1 only verifies the legs themselves; full-machine walking also depends on torso posture control, perception closed loop, and gait planning, which are covered by P15.1.3 and subsequent tests.
  2. "The single-leg test bench can be omitted; go directly to full-machine joint debugging"—Wrong. Omitting single-leg verification means bringing leg risks into full-machine joint debugging. If standing is unstable, the scope of damage will expand from the legs to the full machine, and fault attribution difficulty will increase significantly.
  3. "The smaller the joint tracking error, the better"—Not entirely correct. An excessively small tracking error may indicate overly high control stiffness, which will lack compliance during the swing phase of stepping, leading to excessive landing impact. In engineering, a balance should be struck between stiffness and compliance (engineering judgment).
  4. "The safety harness is just a precautionary measure and does not affect test results"—Wrong. The load-bearing ratio of the safety harness changes the actual load experienced by the legs. If the load-bearing ratio is too high, the load conditions of standing verification will deviate from design conditions, distorting the acceptance results.
