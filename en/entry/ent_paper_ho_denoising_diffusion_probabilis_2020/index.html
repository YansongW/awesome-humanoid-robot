Summary

Ho, Jain, and Abbeel (2020) introduce a denoising diffusion probabilistic model trained with a simplified weighted variational bound, achieving high-quality image synthesis on CIFAR-10 and LSUN datasets.

Overview

This paper, authored by Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel, is the first to demonstrate that Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) can generate high-quality images comparable to GANs, establishing theoretical connections between diffusion models and denoising score matching, annealed Langevin dynamics, and autoregressive decoding. The core contribution lies in the mean parameterization via noise prediction and a simplified training objective, reducing the FID of diffusion models from 25.32 (NCSN) to 3.17 (CIFAR10 unconditional generation).

What It Changed

Before DDPM, diffusion models lagged far behind GANs and autoregressive models in generation quality. While NCSN demonstrated the potential of score matching, its sampler coefficients were manually set after training, the training process did not directly optimize sampling quality, and the forward process lacked tight alignment with the prior. What this paper truly changed was training the sampler itself directly as a latent variable model—strictly deriving each step's coefficients of the Langevin-style sampler from the forward process's β_t via the variational lower bound, rather than adjusting them post hoc. This allowed diffusion models to surpass the strongest GAN variants of the time on FID for the first time (StyleGAN2 + ADA's FID 3.26 vs. this paper's 3.17), and revealed that Gaussian diffusion can be viewed as a form of generalized bit-order autoregressive decoding, offering a new theoretical perspective for generative models.

Method Breakdown

Model Definition

  • Latent variable model pθ(x0) ≔ ∫ pθ(x0:T) dx1:T, where x1,…,xT share the same dimensionality as the data x0.
  • The forward process is a fixed Markov chain: q(xt|xt−1) ≔ N(xt; √(1−βt) xt−1, βt I), with βt increasing linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02, and T=1000.
  • The reverse process is a learnable Markov chain: pθ(xt−1|xt) ≔ N(xt−1; μθ(xt, t), Σθ(xt, t)).

Key Design Decisions

  1. Mean Parameterization: Instead of directly predicting the posterior mean μ̃t, the model predicts the noise εθ(xt, t), i.e., μθ = (1/√αt)(xt − (βt/√(1−ᾱt)) εθ). The rationale is its similarity to Langevin dynamics and the simplification of the variational bound.
  2. Simplified Training Objective: Lsimple(θ) ≔ Et,x0,ε[‖ε − εθ(√ᾱt x0 + √(1−ᾱt) ε, t)‖²]. By discarding the per-term weights of the variational bound, the lower-weight terms for small t are down-weighted, forcing the network to focus on the harder denoising tasks at large t.
  3. Fixed Variance: The reverse process variance σt² is set to βt or β̃t (not trained), with similar results for both.
  4. Data Scaling: Image integers {0,…,255} are linearly scaled to [−1,1], and L0 is computed using an independent discrete decoder.

Sampling (Algorithm 2)

xt−1 = (1/√αt)(xt − (1−αt)/√(1−ᾱt) εθ(xt, t)) + σt z, where z~N(0,I) for t>1 and z=0 for t=1.

Architecture

U-Net backbone (based on PixelCNN++), group normalization, Transformer sinusoidal positional embeddings for time t, and self-attention at 16×16 resolution. The CIFAR10 model has 35.7M parameters, while the LSUN/CelebA-HQ models have 114M parameters.

Key Innovations

  1. Predicting noise rather than the posterior mean: This is a fundamental difference from prior diffusion model work. Predicting ε makes the training objective equivalent to denoising score matching, and the sampling formula aligns with the form of Langevin dynamics, thereby unifying three independent research lines (diffusion models, score matching, Langevin sampling) into a single framework.
  2. Simplified weighted objective: Discarding the theoretical weights of the variational bound, seemingly "not rigorous," improved FID from 13.51 (full L) to 3.17. This reveals an important principle: generation quality and likelihood are not monotonically related; focusing on difficult denoising tasks (large t) is more effective than precisely optimizing likelihood.
  3. Forward process scaling factor: Each step scales the data by √(1−βt) to keep the variance stable, ensuring DKL(q(xT|x0) ∥ N(0,I)) ≈ 10⁻⁵ bits/dim, so that the prior closely matches the aggregated posterior, preventing sampling distribution shift—a key property missing in NCSN.

Progressive Compression (Section 4.3)

Highest-quality CIFAR10 model: rate = 1.78 bits/dim, distortion = 1.97 bits/dim (RMSE 0.95). The first 100 steps of the reverse process consume the vast majority of the 1.78 bits/dim, while the last 900 steps contribute only 0.004 bits/dim, confirming that most bits are used for imperceptible details.

Training/Inference Cost

CIFAR10 training takes 10.6 hours (TPU v3-8, 800k steps); sampling 256 images takes 17 seconds; LSUN 256² sampling of 128 images takes 300 seconds. Inference requires T=1000 network evaluations.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Log-likelihood does not compete with other likelihood-based models (CIFAR10 NLL 3.75 vs Sparse Transformer 2.80), only surpassing energy models and score matching estimates.
  • Progressive compression is only a proof of concept, relying on minimal random coding, which is infeasible for high-dimensional data and has not become a practical compression system.
  • Not validated on larger-scale, more class-diverse datasets such as ImageNet; non-image modalities were not tested.
  • The decoder does not use a more powerful conditional autoregressive model; the authors explicitly leave this as future work.
  • The paper does not mention mitigation measures for malicious use (e.g., image forgery), only noting that CNN-generated images have detectable artifacts.
  • The option of predicting x0 led to worse sample quality in early experiments and was not adopted by the authors.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, prioritize checking the following three points: First, the β_t schedule must ensure LT ≈ 0 (linear 10⁻⁴→0.02 suffices); otherwise, a mismatch between the prior and the aggregated posterior will cause sampling shift. Second, be sure to use the ε prediction + Lsimple combination; any "improvement" to the full variational bound or learned variance will worsen FID by 4–10× (as confirmed in Table 2). Third, EMA decay 0.9999 and Adam learning rate 2×10⁻⁴ are the default safe choices, but for 256×256 images, the learning rate must be reduced to 2×10⁻⁵. The easiest pitfall is dropout—CIFAR10 requires 0.1, while other datasets require 0; it cannot be unified. At inference, T=1000 steps is the balance point between quality and speed; if acceleration is needed, one can try shortening T, but the β_t schedule must be re-scanned. LSUN Cat's FID of 19.75 is significantly worse than Bedroom's 4.90, indicating that the method still requires tuning for datasets with high intra-class variance.
