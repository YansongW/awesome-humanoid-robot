Overview

Software middleware and system integration is the 14th stage in the full development process of humanoid robot products, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. The core mission of this stage is to establish a reliable communication and scheduling bridge between algorithms, hardware, and real-time control, ensuring that the instruction chain between high-level intelligent decision-making and low-level joint execution is connected with low latency and high determinism. What it truly changes is not the performance of a single algorithm module, but the engineering credibility of the entire robot system—from "being able to run a demo" to "being able to achieve stable mass production."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Software middleware and system integration is an engineering stage in the humanoid robot development process that bridges the upper and lower phases, positioned between algorithm validation (after P10) and full-machine integration (before P15). It is responsible for three things: selection—determining the technology stack for middleware and real-time frameworks; adaptation—encapsulating algorithm modules into nodes or processes that conform to the target architecture; integration validation—verifying on simulation and prototype platforms whether communication links, scheduling timing, and resource isolation meet design requirements.

In WBS V3, this stage uses P14.1 (Middleware and Real-Time Framework) as its backbone, expanding downward into sub-work packages such as P14.1.1 (Middleware Selection and Adaptation), P14.1.2 (Real-Time Control Framework), and P14.1.3 (Hardware Abstraction and Driver Layer). Each sub-work package follows a unified five-step engineering action template: input review, solution design, implementation/prototype, validation loop, and documentation delivery.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The software stack of humanoid robots inherently involves a conflict between two types of time scales. The upper layer consists of AI tasks—visual perception, path planning, large model inference—which run at a rhythm of 10–100 Hz and tolerate millisecond-level or even hundred-millisecond-level latency jitter; the lower layer consists of joint control loops—torque loops, velocity loops, position loops—which require update rates above 1 kHz, with jitter strictly limited to the microsecond to tens-of-microseconds range. If both share the same communication and scheduling mechanism, the determinism of the control loop will be disrupted by bursty loads from AI tasks, leading to joint jitter in mild cases and system instability in severe ones.

This contradiction was not prominent in early wheeled robots, because wheeled platforms have weak dynamic coupling and low safety risks. However, humanoid robots are underactuated, highly coupled, non-minimum phase systems whose balance depends on precise torque output every millisecond. The existence of stage P14 is precisely to draw a clear "red line" at the software level: real-time control and AI computation must be physically or logically isolated, and the isolation strategy must be validated before full-machine integration (P15).

Principle Breakdown

① Middleware Selection: Communication Paradigm Determines Architecture Ceiling

The essence of middleware selection is a trade-off between development efficiency and real-time determinism. ROS2, based on the DDS (Data Distribution Service) protocol, provides a publish-subscribe communication model with out-of-the-box node discovery, Quality of Service (QoS) policies, and distributed deployment, making it suitable for rapid algorithm iteration; DDS itself supports multiple QoS configurations (e.g., BEST_EFFORT, RELIABLE, KEEP_LAST), allowing reliability levels to be adjusted for different message types. LCM (Lightweight Communications and Marshalling) is more lightweight and suitable for low-latency inter-process communication. Hard real-time control loops (e.g., torque loops above 1 kHz) are typically offloaded to independent tasks on an RTOS, communicating directly with drives via shared memory or fieldbuses such as EtherCAT, without passing through middleware forwarding.

There are four key constraints in selection: real-time performance (end-to-end latency and jitter limits), message latency (single-hop latency budget), ecosystem (community activity, toolchain maturity, talent pool), and License (impact of commercial vs. open-source licensing on productization). These four form an evaluation matrix, and candidate solutions (no fewer than 2) must be quantitatively scored on this matrix before being frozen.

② Real-Time Control Framework: Isolation Is the Means, Determinism Is the Goal

The design core of a real-time control framework is ensuring that control loops (≥1 kHz) are not disturbed by AI tasks. Two isolation strategies are commonly adopted in engineering:

  • Temporal isolation: Partition CPU cores, with control tasks exclusively occupying one or more cores and AI tasks running on the remaining cores, achieved through CPU affinity binding.
  • Communication isolation: Control loop data travels through deterministic channels such as EtherCAT or shared memory, while AI task data travels through the middleware network, with the two physically or logically separated.

EtherCAT has become the mainstream choice for joint communication in humanoid robots due to its "processing on the fly" mechanism: slave stations read and write data instantly as the data frame passes through, without needing to fully receive the frame before forwarding. Combined with distributed clocks (DC), this achieves sub-microsecond synchronization accuracy. The working counter (WC) is used to confirm whether each slave station has successfully processed the data, providing hardware-level assurance of communication integrity.

③ Hardware Abstraction and Driver Layer: Masking Differences, Exposing Unified Interfaces

The design logic of the Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL) is to mask different hardware implementations, facilitating algorithm portability and replacement. The unified joint interface, sensor HAL, and board-level driver three-layer structure allows upper-layer algorithms to remain agnostic to which vendor's motor driver or IMU chip is used underneath. Key constraints are compatibility, real-time performance, and maintainability. Deliverables include HAL interface specifications, driver coverage lists, and unit tests.

EtherCAT vs. Traditional Fieldbuses: EtherCAT's "processing on the fly" mechanism gives it significantly higher frame utilization than traditional master-slave polling buses, and distributed clocks provide all slave stations with a unified time reference, which is critical for synchronized control of multiple joints. Traditional buses (such as CANopen) struggle to meet the bandwidth and synchronization accuracy requirements of high-degree-of-freedom humanoid robots.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

RoboKube Framework (neighbor card ent_paper_liu_robokube_establishing_a_new_fo_2024) demonstrates a frontier direction in the integration of middleware and cloud-native technologies. This framework, built on the Kubernetes ecosystem, constructs a unified deployment platform spanning the device-edge-cloud continuum, supports the modernization of containerized ROS 2 applications, and addresses network configuration challenges in heterogeneous environments. A case study using a UR5 robotic arm teleoperation testbed validated the complete deployment workflow from device to cloud. This represents another path in middleware selection: containerizing the robot software stack and managing its lifecycle with cloud-native toolchains, suitable for scenarios requiring remote operations, multi-machine coordination, or offloading computation to the cloud.

In typical humanoid robot products, the practical implementation path for stage P14 is usually: perception and decision algorithms are developed based on ROS2 and run on the Linux side; joint control loops are implemented based on an EtherCAT master station + RTOS and run on dedicated cores or separate MCUs; the two communicate via shared memory or Ethernet bridging. The hardware abstraction layer ensures that replacement of different batches of motors or sensors does not affect upper-layer algorithms.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundaries of stage P14 are clear: it is not responsible for improving the accuracy of algorithms themselves, not responsible for mechanical structure design, and not responsible for system tuning after full-machine integration (that is P15's responsibility). Its deliverables are "interfaces, architectures, strategies, and validation reports," not "working tasks."

Its limitations lie in: once middleware selection is frozen, subsequent replacement costs are extremely high—this requires the selection stage to fully assess requirement evolution over the next 2–3 years; isolation strategies for real-time frameworks are thoroughly validated in simulation, but issues such as interrupt latency and cache coherence on real hardware only fully surface during the P15 full-machine stage; the degree of HAL abstraction must balance "generality" against "performance"—over-abstraction introduces additional overhead, while insufficient abstraction leads to difficulties in algorithm portability.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "ROS2 can be used for hard real-time control"—Wrong. Although ROS2's node scheduling and DDS's QoS mechanisms are configurable, their end-to-end latency jitter still cannot meet the determinism requirements of 1 kHz control loops. Hard real-time control must be offloaded to an RTOS or a dedicated hardware channel.
  2. "Middleware selection only considers performance"—Wrong. Ecosystem and License are equally critical. A middleware with excellent performance but a declining community will become a bottleneck during product iteration; a solution with an unfriendly License may trigger legal risks during mass production.
  3. "The more HAL abstraction, the better"—Wrong. Over-abstraction introduces additional function call overhead and memory copies, which is fatal for microsecond-sensitive paths like control loops. The granularity of the HAL should be based on "meeting algorithm portability needs," not on pursuing unlimited generality.
  4. "Passing validation means integration is worry-free"—Wrong. P14 validation is typically conducted on simulations or standalone prototypes; issues such as electromagnetic interference, bus timing deviations, and multi-core cache contention on real hardware only surface during P15 full-machine integration. P14 validation is a "necessary condition," not a "sufficient condition."
