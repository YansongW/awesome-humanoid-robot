Overview

Design for Manufacturing (DFM) is an engineering design approach that makes parts and assemblies easy, repeatable, and economical to manufacture at scale. What it truly changes is not how individual parts are processed, but the entire decision-making chain from product concept to mass production—holding design teams accountable for mold costs, yield, and cycle time before drawings are frozen.

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition of Design for Manufacturing

Design for Manufacturing (DFM) belongs to the method category, with its domain in manufacturing processes and its value chain position at the upstream level. Its core definition is: an engineering design approach that makes parts and assemblies easy, repeatable, and economical to manufacture at scale. The English name is Design for Manufacturing (DFM), and the Korean name is 제조 용이성 설계(DFM).

In the humanoid robot industry chain, DFM is not a standalone process step but a set of constraint rules woven throughout the upstream design phase. It requires engineers to answer three questions at the 3D model stage: Can this feature be produced with existing processes? Can production ensure consistency? Is the unit cost acceptable at volume? These three questions correspond to manufacturability, repeatability, and economy—together forming the complete meaning of DFM.

DFM and Design for Assembly (DFA) frequently appear as a pair. DFM focuses on the manufacturing difficulty of individual parts, while DFA focuses on the assembly efficiency of multiple parts. In practice, the two are combined into a DFM/DFA review, serving as a critical gate for products transitioning from prototype to mass production.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the full humanoid robot product development process, the pain point of DFM stems from a structural contradiction: the evaluation criteria for design differ fundamentally between the prototype stage and the mass production stage.

The prototype stage (e.g., 3D printing, CNC machining) prioritizes rapid functional validation; unit cost is high but acceptable, geometric freedom is large, and mold constraints are almost nonexistent. The mass production stage (e.g., die casting, injection molding, sheet metal) prioritizes unit cost and cycle time; mold costs are high, yield directly determines profit, and geometric features are strictly limited by rules such as demolding, draft angles, and wall thickness uniformity. If the design team does not consider mass production processes in advance during the prototype stage, they often face two outcomes: either mold modifications spiral out of control, driving costs up, or the product must be redesigned, delaying market launch.

The historical role of DFM is precisely this bridge—it shifts the constraints of mass production processes upstream to the design stage, allowing the design team to complete process feasibility validation before drawings are frozen. In WBS V3, the DFM/DFA review is explicitly listed as a key subtask of the small-batch trial production and mass production preparation stage (Stage 16), with its design thinking logic being "converting 3D printing/CNC prototypes into mass production processes such as die casting, injection molding, and sheet metal," its key constraints being "mold cost, yield, cycle time," and its completion criteria being "DFM/DFA report, engineering change list, cost impact assessment."

Principle Breakdown

① Mathematical Expression of Process Constraints

The essence of DFM is converting the physical limitations of manufacturing processes into constraint inequalities on design variables. Taking injection molding as an example, wall thickness \(t\) must satisfy the constraints of minimum wall thickness \(t_{\min}\) and maximum wall thickness \(t_{\max}\), while the rate of change between adjacent wall thicknesses is limited by flow resistance:

\[ t_{\min} \le t(\mathbf{x}) \le t_{\max}, \quad \left| \frac{\partial t}{\partial s} \right| \le k_{\text{flow}} \]

where \(\mathbf{x}\) is the coordinate in the design space, \(s\) is the arc length along the flow direction, and \(k_{\text{flow}}\) is the maximum allowable wall thickness change rate for the material. Similarly, die casting requires a draft angle \(\alpha \ge \alpha_{\min}\), and sheet metal requires a minimum bend radius \(r \ge r_{\min}\). The core work of a DFM review is to check, item by item, whether design features satisfy these process constraints.

② Driving Logic of the Cost Model

The economic judgment of DFM is built on a cost model. The unit cost \(C_{\text{unit}}\) of a mass-produced part can be decomposed as:

\[ C_{\text{unit}} = \frac{C_{\text{mold}}}{N} + C_{\text{material}} + C_{\text{processing}} + C_{\text{quality}} \]

where \(C_{\text{mold}}\) is the mold cost, \(N\) is the total production volume, \(C_{\text{material}}\) is the material cost, \(C_{\text{processing}}\) is the processing cost, and \(C_{\text{quality}}\) is the quality cost (including scrap and rework). When \(N\) is small, mold cost amortization dominates, so processes with lower mold costs (such as CNC) should be prioritized; when \(N\) is large, unit processing cost and yield become dominant, making mold-intensive processes such as die casting and injection molding more advantageous. The essence of DFM decision-making is selecting the process route and design feature combination that minimizes \(C_{\text{unit}}\) given a specified \(N\).

③ Coupling of Yield and Cycle Time

Yield and cycle time are two key constraints of DFM. Yield is significantly affected by design features—for example, deep ribs, thin walls, and sharp corners tend to cause short fills, sink marks, or flash, reducing yield. Cycle time is affected by cooling time, demolding difficulty, and post-processing steps. A DFM review must find a balance between the two: overly conservative designs (such as excessive draft angles) may improve yield but can increase material usage and processing time; overly aggressive designs sacrifice yield for material savings. Engineering judgment: for humanoid robot structural parts, yield takes priority over material utilization, because the cost of scrap far exceeds the material cost saved.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key parameters involved in a DFM review can be divided into four categories:

Parameter Category Typical Parameters Description
Process parameters Mold cost, yield, cycle time Core economic indicators determining process route selection
Geometric parameters Draft angle, minimum wall thickness, minimum bend radius Design constraints determined by specific process and material
Assembly parameters Assembly labor hours, part count, fastener count Inputs shared with the DFA review
Quality parameters Tolerance grade, surface roughness, geometric tolerances Affecting repeatability and yield

In the field of bolted connections, DFM also involves the selection of preload control methods. The turn-of-nut method continues tightening by a specific angle after snugging, with an error of approximately ±10%; yield control monitors the inflection point of the tightening curve, with an error of approximately ±5%; the hydraulic tensioner stretches the bolt directly before tightening the nut, with an error of approximately ±5%; strain gauge/load-indicating washer sensors measure preload directly, offering the highest accuracy. The choice of these methods directly affects assembly labor hours and quality consistency, making it an important decision point in the assembly portion of the DFM/DFA review.

Horizontal Comparison

The relationship between DFM and adjacent methods can be compared in the following table:

Method Focus Core Question Output Relationship to DFM

DFA (Design for Assembly) Relationships between parts Can it be assembled efficiently? DFA report, assembly labor hour estimate Sister method, often combined as DFM/DFA review

BOM Cost Engineering Bill of materials How is the total machine cost decomposed and controlled? BOM cost table, cost reduction plan Downstream method, taking over DFM cost assessment results

The difference between DFM and DFA is that DFM optimizes the manufacturability of individual parts, while DFA optimizes the assembly efficiency of multiple parts. The two often conflict—for example, splitting a complex part into two simpler parts is better from a DFM perspective (each part is easier to manufacture) but worse from a DFA perspective (assembly steps increase). The value of a joint DFM/DFA review lies precisely in finding the global optimum amid such conflicts.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Humanoid Robot OEMs

In the full humanoid robot product development process, the DFM/DFA review is explicitly listed as a key subtask of the small-batch trial production and mass production preparation stage (Stage 16). Its three-level subtasks include: input organization and target quantification (P16.1.1.1), solution/method design (P16.1.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P16.1.1.3), and test execution and result analysis (P16.1.1.4). Four-level key actions include: listing all upstream input items and confirming versions, converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, establishing task owners with timelines and risk registers, developing no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, building an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, and organizing reviews to freeze the solution.

② Electrical, Electronic, and Energy Systems

In the Electrical, Electronic, and Energy Systems work package (P13.1.2), DFM is listed as one of the key constraints. This work package uses tools such as carrier boards, joint driver boards, sensor interface boards, and safety monitoring boards to consolidate computing, communication, power, and safety functions into reliable hardware. Key constraints include EMC, SI/PI, thermal, space, and manufacturability, with completion criteria being schematics, PCB, BOM, and DFM report. This reflects the application of DFM in PCB design—manufacturing process capabilities (such as minimum trace width, minimum hole size, and stack-up structure) must be considered during the layout and routing stage.

③ Open-Source Robot Case

MEVIUS2 is a typical case embodying the DFM philosophy. Existing open-source quadruped robots mostly rely on 3D printing, resulting in fragile structures that are difficult to scale up, typically remaining small in size. MEVIUS2 achieves a large and robust body—comparable in size to Spot—through sheet metal welding and metal machining techniques while reducing part count. The design allows all structural components to be ordered from e-commerce platforms, which is essentially the embodiment of the DFM principle of "prioritizing standardized purchased parts"—replacing custom 3D-printed parts with standardized sheet metal parts and machined aluminum, reducing cost while improving repeatability.

Limitations and Boundaries

DFM is not a universal solution; its limitations are reflected in the following aspects:

① Dependence on Process Knowledge: The effectiveness of DFM is highly dependent on the completeness of the process knowledge base. Different material–process combinations produce different constraint parameters; if the process knowledge base is incomplete, review results may be distorted. Engineering judgment: for new materials (such as carbon fiber composites) or new processes (such as metal additive manufacturing), DFM constraint parameters must be confirmed independently and cannot be directly borrowed from traditional process data.

② Simplified Cost Model: The aforementioned cost model assumes linear amortization of mold cost over production volume, but in reality, mold life is finite—when production volume exceeds mold life, additional mold investment is required. Furthermore, quality cost is not a linear function—when yield falls below a certain threshold, scrap costs may rise exponentially.

③ Tension Between Design Freedom and Manufacturing Constraints: Overly strict DFM may stifle innovative design. Topology optimization can find the optimal material distribution within a given design space, loads, and boundary conditions, satisfying objectives (such as maximum stiffness or minimum mass) under constraints, but its output is often irregular, organic shapes that are difficult to directly satisfy with traditional process constraints. In such cases, iteration between topology optimization and DFM is needed: first obtain a conceptual configuration through topology optimization, then perform geometric reconstruction according to process constraints.

④ Not Applicable to Ultra-Small Batches: When production volume is extremely low (e.g., research prototypes, one-off custom parts), mold cost amortization becomes meaningless, and the economic analysis framework of DFM no longer applies. In such cases, mold-free processes such as CNC machining or 3D printing should be prioritized.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "DFM is just checking drawings"—Wrong. DFM is not a post-hoc inspection but an upstream constraint embedded in the design process. It requires the design team to collaborate with manufacturing engineers during the conceptual design stage, rather than waiting until drawings are complete to conduct a review. True DFM is part of design decision-making, not a standalone quality inspection step.

  2. "DFM only focuses on individual parts"—Wrong. Although DFM focuses on single-part manufacturing, it must be considered jointly with DFA. A part that is easy to manufacture but difficult to assemble does not necessarily yield the optimal overall cost. The joint DFM/DFA review is the complete methodology.

  3. "DFM restricts design innovation"—Wrong. DFM does not limit design freedom; it guides design freedom within a feasible range. The combination of topology optimization and DFM proves that the two can work together: first explore the concept space through topology optimization, then convert the concept into a mass-producible design through DFM constraints.

  4. "DFM only applies to the mass production stage"—Wrong. DFM constraints (such as draft angles and minimum wall thickness) should be considered as early as the prototype stage. If DFM is completely ignored during the prototype stage, the cost of engineering changes in the mass production stage will far exceed the cost of modifications during the design stage. Engineering judgment: every 1 yuan invested in DFM review during the design stage can save more than 10 yuan in change costs during the mass production stage (estimated based on corpus data).

  5. "The DFM report is the final deliverable"—Wrong. The DFM report is only an intermediate output; the real value lies in the engineering change list and cost impact assessment. The report itself does not change the design—the change list drives design iteration.
