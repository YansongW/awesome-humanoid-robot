Summary

This paper proposes an energy tank-based, passivity-based control framework that directly enforces the energetic bounds defined by ISO/TS 15066 for collaborative robots, avoiding conservative velocity limits while preserving stability during free motion and human contact. The approach was validated in simulation on a KUKA LWR 4+ 7-DOF force-controlled manipulator.

  • Energy Tank-based Control Framework for Satisfying the ISO/TS 15066 Constraint

Overview

This paper proposes a passivity-based control framework using a modulated energy tank to directly satisfy the time-varying energy bounds (PFL) specified in the ISO/TS 15066 standard, without requiring an accurate robot dynamics model. The method handles external energy injection by online adjusting the energy tank lower bound and a variable damper, ensuring formal stability while significantly reducing the conservatism of traditional velocity-limiting approaches. Simulations are conducted on a KUKA LWR 4+ 7-DOF manipulator, demonstrating strict compliance with energy bounds under the influence of posture changes and self-motion.

What It Changes

Traditional ISO/TS 15066 implementation achieves PFL through velocity limits, but these limits rely on strong assumptions of perfectly inelastic collisions and a two-body model, leading to severe conservatism—the robot's actual motion speed can be higher than the standard-computed velocity upper bound while still satisfying the energy constraint. Existing CBF-based methods (e.g., Ferraguti et al. 2020) define safety sets too conservatively (ellipsoidal sets) and exhibit non-smooth behavior due to sudden activation/deactivation of the ellipsoid. More critically, the energy-based approach of Lachner et al. 2021 heavily depends on accurate knowledge of dynamic parameters such as the inertia matrix, which is often unavailable or inaccurate in industrial scenarios.

What this paper truly changes is shifting PFL compliance from a "velocity-limiting" paradigm to an "energy-limiting" paradigm, without requiring model knowledge. It directly addresses the kinetic energy upper bound defined in ISO/TS 15066, enforcing safety constraints through power-flow control of the energy tank, while providing formal stability guarantees (Prop. 2 and Prop. 3). This resolves three core pain points: conservatism (overly strict velocity limits), model dependence (requiring accurate inertia matrices), and non-smoothness (CBF ellipsoid activation issues). The method can serve as a universal safety layer for any force-controlled robot without modifying the underlying controller.

Method Breakdown

System Modeling

  • Robot dynamics: n-degree-of-freedom Euler-Lagrange model, M(q)q̈ + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q) = τ(t) + J(q)ᵀFₑ(t)
  • Operational space model: Λ(x)ẍ + S(x,ẋ)ẋ = F(t), where F(t) = -F_c(t) + F_e(t)
  • Kinetic energy computation: H(t) = ½ ẋ(t)ᵀ Λ(x) ẋ(t) ≥ 0

Control Architecture

  • The modulated energy tank is interconnected with the robot, with Cartesian velocity ẋ(t) as input and control force F_c(t) as output
  • Objective: achieve the desired control force F_des(t) while guaranteeing the kinetic energy bound H(t) ≤ H̄

Optimization Problem (Discrete-Time Convex Optimization)

  • Basic form: minimize ||F_c(k) - F_des(k)||², subject to τ F_c(k)ᵀ ẋ(k) + T(k-1) ≥ ε
  • Direction-preserving modification: minimize ||α F_des(k) - F_des(k)||², subject to τ α F_des(k)ᵀ ẋ(k) + T(k-1) ≥ ε, where F_c = α F_des

Key Design Decisions

  • Initial condition: T(0) = H̄ - H(0) + ε, guaranteeing the kinetic energy bound (Prop. 2)
  • External energy handling: The tank dynamics are modified to ẋ_t(t) = (1/x_t(t))[-F_e(t)ᵀ ẋ(t) + b(t) ẋ(t)ᵀ ẋ(t)] + a(t)ᵀ u(t), where b(t) is a variable damper activated only when F_e(t)ᵀ ẋ(t) > 0 and T(t) = ε, used to dissipate externally injected energy
  • Variable energy bound: When the closest body part changes, the tank lower bound is adjusted online as ε(k_i) = T(0) - H_i + H(0), switching the kinetic energy upper bound (Prop. 3 guarantees stability)

Model Independence

  • The method does not require knowledge of robot dynamic parameters (inertia, Coriolis terms), relying only on operational space kinetic energy computation

Key Innovations

  1. Model-independent energy bound compliance: Unlike Lachner et al. 2021, this method does not depend on accurate knowledge of the inertia matrix, requiring only operational space kinetic energy computation, significantly lowering the barrier to practical deployment. This is a key improvement over existing energy-based methods, making the approach applicable to scenarios with uncertain or time-varying dynamic parameters.

  2. Online switching mechanism for time-varying energy bounds: By adjusting the tank lower bound ε(k_i) = T(0) - H_i + H(0), the kinetic energy upper bound is smoothly switched when the closest body part changes, with Prop. 3 providing formal stability guarantees. This addresses the practical issue where different body parts correspond to different energy limits in multi-segment motion, a scenario not adequately handled in prior ISO/TS 15066 compliance work.

  3. Active dissipation of external energy injection: The variable damper b(t) is activated only when external energy is injected and the tank energy reaches its lower bound, actively eliminating external energy effects through the dissipation term b(t) ẋ(t)ᵀ ẋ(t), ensuring the system remains passive under physical interaction. This mechanism makes the method applicable to contact scenarios in human-robot collaboration, not just free motion.

Experiments and Results

Simulation Setup

  • Platform: KUKA LWR 4+ 7-DOF force-controlled manipulator, MATLAB modeling, Robotics Toolbox for dynamics computation, sampling time 1 ms
  • Task: Two-segment motion, with the closest body part being the chest in the first segment and the shoulder in the second

Key Behaviors

  • Phase 1: The robot follows the desired velocity profile as long as kinetic energy remains below E_max1 (tank energy above ε1)
  • Phase 2: The robot decelerates below v_max2 after 7.5 sec, due to posture conditions and self-motion causing kinetic energy to reach E_max2
  • P_ext = 0 W during free motion

Implications of Results

  • The robot's actual motion speed exceeds the velocity values imposed by v_max1 and v_max2 while still satisfying the PFL energy requirement—demonstrating that velocity limits are indeed conservative
  • The energy tank mechanism approximates the input via equation (28) when the bound is reached, trading performance for safety

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Simulation-only validation: All results are obtained in a MATLAB simulation environment and have not been validated on real hardware. Model errors, actuator delays, and sensor noise in actual robots may affect the accuracy of energy tank power-flow computation.
  • Incomplete stability proof: Formal proof of stability and safety during the boundary switching phase is listed as future work; the current Prop. 3 only guarantees stability after switching and does not cover transient behavior during the switch.
  • No quantitative comparison with existing methods: The paper does not provide quantitative performance comparisons with CBF-based methods (Ferraguti et al. 2020) or velocity-limiting approaches, making it impossible to directly assess the specific degree of conservatism reduction.
  • Insufficient validation of external energy handling: The variable damper mechanism is only described theoretically, and since P_ext = 0 W in the simulation, its effectiveness in actual contact scenarios is not validated.
  • Kinematic redundancy not exploited: The authors explicitly list "exploiting kinematic redundancy to reduce apparent mass" as future work; the current method does not optimize robot posture to reduce collision risk.

Engineering Insights

  • Prioritize verification of kinetic energy computation: Implementation must ensure accurate computation of the operational space inertia matrix Λ(x). Although the method does not rely on a full dynamic model, kinetic energy computation still requires Λ(x). It is recommended to use mature tools such as the Robotics Toolbox to verify the correctness of Λ(x).
  • Pay attention to tank lower bound switching timing: The switching of ε(k_i) = T(0) - H_i + H(0) must be synchronized with closest-body-part detection; delays may lead to energy bound violations. It is recommended to implement atomic operations for body-part detection and tank parameter updates in the controller.
  • Sensitivity to sampling time: The optimization problem is solved in discrete time and performs well at a 1 ms sampling time, but if the sampling time increases, the convex optimization solution delay may affect real-time performance. It is recommended to evaluate the worst-case computation time of the optimization solver.
  • Tuning of external energy dissipation parameters: The gain of the variable damper b(t) needs to be tuned according to actual contact scenarios; too large a value may cause sluggish system response, while too small a value may fail to effectively dissipate external energy. It is recommended to first validate system behavior under different b(t) values in simulation.
  • Most common pitfall: The initial condition T(0) = H̄ - H(0) + ε must be set precisely. If H(0) is computed incorrectly (e.g., non-zero initial velocity), the energy tank's initial energy will be wrong, affecting the safety guarantee for the entire task. It is recommended to perform a zero-velocity calibration at startup.
