Summary

Gallant first recovers the scene, object, or motion representation from camera images/multi-view observations, ontology state and joint sequences, depth/point cloud/altitude maps, and then the image is reconstructed from the depth/point cloud/altitude maps, then the strategy network and control module are used to generate the terrain/scene representation, navigation/reaching target. The key point is to put the policy network and the control module in the same training/deployment link, reducing the discontinuity between high-level goals and low-level actions.

Overview

Gallant is the first work to use a voxel grid as the sole perception input, training humanoid robot locomotion and local navigation policies through end-to-end reinforcement learning. It was developed by the Unitree-related team and achieves zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on the Unitree G1. Its core contribution lies in replacing 2.5D elevation maps with 3D voxel representations, combined with a lightweight LiDAR simulation pipeline and domain randomization, enabling a single policy to simultaneously handle three types of spatial constraints: ground-level, lateral, and overhead.

What It Changes

The fundamental flaw of existing perception approaches is "dimensionality reduction": elevation maps compress 3D point clouds into 2.5D height fields, directly discarding vertical structure information such as overhangs, low ceilings, and stair undersides; depth images are limited by narrow FoV and finite range, making them insufficient for reasoning about spatially extended environments. More critically, this perception-control decoupled architecture—where a high-level planner outputs paths and a low-level policy tracks them—forces the policy to passively follow rather than actively exploit terrain geometric features (such as ducking early or lifting feet to step over). Gallant changes this paradigm: it no longer asks "what does the environment look like," but rather "what does the policy need to see to make correct decisions." By encoding the entire environment as a binary voxel occupancy grid and letting the policy learn behavior directly from raw geometry, it demonstrates that end-to-end learning can replace traditional hierarchical navigation and cover all spatial obstacle types.

Method Breakdown

Problem Formulation

The perception-motor task is modeled as a POMDP, trained with PPO using an asymmetric actor-critic. The actor receives only the voxel grid, while the critic additionally receives a height map as privileged information, avoiding reliance on latency-sensitive channels during deployment.

Voxel Perception Representation

  • Perception volume Ω=[-0.8,0.8]m×[-0.8,0.8]m×[-1.0,1.0]m, resolution Δ=0.05m, producing a 32×32×40 binary occupancy tensor X∈{0,1}^{C×H×W} (C=40 height slices, H=W=32).
  • Each voxel is set to 1 if at least one LiDAR point falls within it, otherwise 0.

z-Grouped 2D CNN

The z-axis is treated as the channel dimension, with 2D convolutions applied in the x-y plane. The output formula is: Y_{o,v,u}=σ(Σ_c Σ_{Δv,Δu} W_{o,c,Δv,Δu}·X_{c,v+Δv,u+Δu}+b_o) Compared to a 3D kernel k³, computation and memory costs are reduced by approximately a factor of k.

LiDAR Simulation Pipeline (NVIDIA Warp)

  • A BVH is precomputed for each mesh in the local coordinate frame; during simulation, only the ray origin is transformed, and the direction only applies the rotation component: raycast(TM, p, d) = T⁻¹·raycast(M, T⁻¹p, R⁻¹d).
  • Parallel kernel shape (N_envs, N_meshes, N_rays).
  • Domain randomization: pose perturbation N(0,1)cm / N(0,(π/180)²)rad; hit position perturbation N(0,1)cm; delay 100-200ms; random masking of 2% of voxels.

Reward Design (General, Geometry-Aware)

  • Goal arrival reward r_reach = 1/(1+‖P_t‖²) · 𝟙(t>T-T_r)/T_r, T_r=2s.
  • Directional velocity reward r_velocity_direction = (a(p,g)·v_t) / ‖a(p,g)·v_t‖₂, radius r=1m, κ=0.8.
  • Head height reward r_head_height = exp(−4(H_head_est − H_head)²), where H_head_est is computed by moving 0.45m forward along the goal direction, averaging terrain height within a 0.5×0.5m square, and subtracting a 0.1m offset.
  • Foot clearance reward r_feet_clearance = exp(−4(H_feet_est − H_feet)²), where H_feet_est queries the terrain 0.5m ahead of each foot.

Curriculum Training

8 terrain types (Plane/Ceiling/Forest/Door/Platform/Pile/Upstair/Downstair), each parameterized by a scalar difficulty s∈[0,1], with parameter interpolation p_τ(s)=(1-s)·p_τ^min+s·p_τ^max. Success upgrades difficulty, failure downgrades it.

Symmetry Augmentation

The voxel grid is mirrored along the y-axis, aligned with flipped proprioceptive inputs, with rewards unchanged. Both original and flipped samples are stored in the rollout buffer.

Key Innovations

  1. End-to-end policy with voxel grid as the sole perception input: This is the first work demonstrating that a pure voxel representation (no height maps, no depth images, no explicit planner) can support humanoid locomotion and local navigation on complex 3D terrain. Compared to elevation maps, voxels preserve complete vertical and multi-layer structural information, enabling the policy to actively perceive overhead constraints and adjust posture.
  2. Lightweight LiDAR simulation pipeline with domain randomization: Through precomputed BVH, transforming only ray origins, and parallel kernel shape (N_envs, N_meshes, N_rays), efficient and scalable LiDAR simulation is achieved. Explicit modeling of delay (100-200ms), noise, and pose perturbation is key to successful sim-to-real transfer—ablation experiments show that NoDR (no domain randomization) significantly degrades performance on ground-level terrain.
  3. z-grouped 2D CNN architecture: The z-axis is treated as the channel dimension with 2D convolutions in the x-y plane, reducing computational cost to approximately 1/k of a 3D convolution. This design maintains 3D geometric information while substantially reducing computation and memory overhead, enabling real-time operation on Orin NX.

Real-World Experiments

  • The same policy is deployed directly on the Unitree G1 without fine-tuning. Scenarios include flat terrain, random-height ceilings, lateral clutter, elevated platforms with gaps, stepping stones, and stairs.
  • Gallant consistently outperforms HeightMap and NoDR baselines on all real-world terrains (Fig. 5, 15 trials).
  • HeightMap fails on Ceiling and Door due to 2.5D representation limitations; NoDR degrades significantly on ground-level terrain due to unmodeled LiDAR delay and noise.
  • Simulation-to-real correlation (Fig. 6): terrains with high simulation success rates also perform well on hardware, validating that large-scale simulation evaluation can predict real-world performance.

Key Bottleneck

  • Pile terrain plateaus at approximately 80% success rate; with zero LiDAR delay in simulation, it improves to over 90%, indicating that real sensor latency (over 100ms at 10Hz) is the key bottleneck.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • The paper does not explicitly list a limitations section, but experimental data reveals: Pile terrain (rock piles) has a success rate of only 82.1%, the lowest among the 8 terrains, and the authors acknowledge LiDAR latency as the primary bottleneck—at 10Hz operating frequency, each scan incurs over 100ms delay due to light reflection and communication overhead, limiting proactive reaction capability.
  • No specific real-world success rate numbers are provided per scenario (Fig. 5 only notes "15 trials" without numerical values in the text).
  • No direct real-world comparison experiments with other non-ablation methods (e.g., depth-image-based approaches) are mentioned.
  • No generalization tests on completely unseen terrain types are mentioned.
  • No specific computational latency in milliseconds is mentioned (only "lower inference latency").
  • No specific PPO hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, GAE parameters, etc.) or total training duration are mentioned.

Engineering Insights

  1. Verify LiDAR delay modeling first: This is critical for sim-to-real transfer success. The NoDR ablation shows that unmodeled delay causes significant performance drops on ground-level terrain. When reproducing, explicitly add 100-200ms delay in simulation and verify the delay distribution matches the real sensor.
  2. 5cm voxel resolution is the sweet spot: 2.5cm causes performance collapse (Ceiling 13.3% vs 97.1%), while 10cm degrades Platform performance (75.5% vs 96.1%). Do not blindly pursue higher resolution; noise and computational overhead will offset precision gains.
  3. Self-scan is indispensable: w/o-Self-Scan drops success rates from 82-96% to 22-33% on Platform/Pile/Upstair. Ensure the policy can perceive the relative position between itself and the environment, rather than relying solely on external observations.
  4. z-grouped 2D CNN outperforms 3D CNN: Sparse-3D-CNN achieves only 52.4% on Pile vs Gallant's 82.1%, with higher computational cost. When reproducing, prioritize the z-grouped 2D convolution architecture.
  5. Communication optimization is key for deployment: TCP transmission of LiDAR data and LCM internal state sharing cause approximately 200ms delay; cropping LiDAR output and using shared memory (voxel grid and observations, state reading and action commands) can eliminate almost all communication delay. Always check the end-to-end latency chain during deployment.
  6. Training configuration reference: 8 RTX 4090 GPUs, 1024×8 environments, 4000 iterations, PPO clip 0.2, entropy coefficient 0.003, GAE λ 0.95, γ 0.99, learning rate 5e-4. Evaluation scale is 5 independent evaluations × 1000 episodes, reporting mean ± standard deviation.
