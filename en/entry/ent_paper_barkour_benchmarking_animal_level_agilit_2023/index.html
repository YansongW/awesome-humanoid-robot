Summary

Animals have evolved various agile locomotion strategies, such as sprinting, leaping, and jumping. There is a growing interest in developing legged robots that move like their biological counterparts and show various agile skills to navigate complex environments quickly. Institutions per source list: Google DeepMind.

Overview

Barkour is an agility benchmark for quadruped robots proposed by teams including Google Research, inspired by dog agility competitions. It sets up four obstacles—a pause box, weave poles, an A-frame, and a broad jump—within a 5m×5m area, and provides a quantitative scoring formula aligned with AKC rules. The paper also offers two baseline approaches: a manual state machine switching among three expert policies (omnidirectional walking, slope climbing, jumping), and distilling the expert policies into a single Transformer generalist policy (Locomotion-Transformer), with approximately 3,600 real course attempts completed on a custom 11.5kg quadruped robot.

What It Changes

The legged robotics field does not lack controllers; it lacks a unified metric for horizontally comparing control strategies and hardware. Previous evaluations were mostly ad hoc metrics, with each lab reporting its own results, making "agility" impossible to quantify. Barkour's substantive contribution is turning "animal-level agility" from a slogan into a computable, reproducible score—it defines not a single task but a complete protocol including obstacle specifications, timing rules, penalty details, and success criteria, allowing any team to score strategies under identical conditions.

More critically, the paper answers the open question of "whether a single RL policy can cover a multi-skill course" through action. The authors do not merely provide a benchmark; they also provide two reproducible baselines—expert policy ensembles and distilled generalist policies—and honestly demonstrate their gap compared to real dogs (0.77/0.73 vs 1.0). This shifts the field's inertia of "SOTA on single tasks," forcing researchers to confront real problems such as multi-skill transfer and high-speed sim-to-real gaps.

Method Breakdown

Benchmark Design

  • Area: 5m×5m, four obstacles: pause box (1m×1m×0.1m), weave poles (5 flexible poles, spacing 0.5–0.8m), A-frame (30° slope, 1m peak height), broad jump (0.5m long board, flush with ground).
  • Scoring formula: R_agility = 1.0 − max(t_run − t_allotted, 0) × 0.01 − penalties, where t_allotted = Σ(d_obstacle / v_target), v_target = 1.69 m/s; total time limit 10.64s, total nominal distance 18m.
  • Success criteria detailed down to torso center displacement (0.7m off the table, 0.4m onto the table), distance from weave pole centers (0.1m), crossing the A-frame bottom line, and not touching the broad jump board.

Specialist Policies

  • All three policies trained in IsaacGym with PPO, sharing observation space (desired velocity v̄, proprioception s^p, terrain height field s^v, previous action a_{t−1}), with action space as target motor angles.
  • Omnidirectional Walking Policy (OWP): uneven terrain course, velocity commands v_x∈[-1.5,1.5] m/s, v_y∈[-1,1] m/s, ω_z∈[-2.5,2.5] rad/s.
  • Slope Climbing Policy (SCP): slopes increasing from 5° to 33°, velocity commands v_x∈[-1.0,1.5] m/s.
  • Jumping Policy (JP): three-stage course—flat sprint (up to 2.25 m/s) → gaps 0.3–0.6m → fixed 0.6m gap fine-tuning; termination condition: knee height above gap < 0.06m.
  • Key domain randomization: default parameters effective below 1 m/s, but sim-to-real gap significant above 2 m/s, requiring additional randomization of torso inertia (40%–165%), motor modeling, and joint static friction (0–0.7).

Generalist Policy: Locomotion-Transformer

  • Distills expert policies via behavior cloning into a causal Transformer: input sequence {s^p_0, a_0, …, s^p_{t−1}, a_{t−1}, s^p_t, s^v_t}, predicting next action a_t with L2 regression loss.
  • Architecture: two-layer Transformer, context length 15 (corresponding to 0.3s), internal representation 256-d; height field encoded via two convolutional layers (kernel 3, channels 8) into 64-d embedding; proprioception and actions each tokenized via 256-d projections; ReLU activation.
  • Data collection: 17,636 simulated episodes (57.58 robot hours), covering four environments—random steps, stairs, gaps, slopes; for policies not receiving velocity commands, the average actual velocity over 1s is used as the effective command.

High-Level Navigation Controller

  • State machine design, accessing full environment state and true pose, outputting velocity commands; waypoints include target position, orientation, and error tolerances (weave poles d_lim=0.3m, ψ_lim=0.2rad; tightened to 0.1m, 0.17rad before jumps).
  • Velocity command formula: v_x = α·sin(φ)·‖d‖, v_y = β·cos(φ)·‖d‖, ω = γ·Δ(ψ).

Key Innovations

  1. Turning dog agility competitions into a computable robot benchmark: Barkour is not just another simulation task but a complete protocol with physical obstacle specifications, timing rules, penalty details, and success criteria, with a scoring formula aligned with AKC rules, giving "animal-level agility" a horizontally comparable quantitative score. This is the first systematic transplantation of animal competition rules into robot evaluation in the field.

  2. Distilled generalist policy replacing manual switching: Locomotion-Transformer fuses three expert policies into a single network via behavior cloning, requiring no explicit obstacle type at deployment—only velocity commands. Ablations show MLP performs significantly worse than Transformer on the same data, and context length 15 is critical—validating the necessity of temporal information for smooth multi-skill switching.

  3. Systematic handling of high-speed sim-to-real: The authors explicitly identify that default domain randomization fails above 2 m/s and add torso inertia, motor modeling, and joint static friction randomization for jumping/climbing scenarios. This finding has direct guidance for any team pursuing high-speed agile policies, not just for this benchmark.

Experiments and Results

  • Single-obstacle performance: weave poles 100% success (9.27s), A-frame 100% success (7.95s), broad jump only 38% success (2.45s, velocity 1.7 m/s)—broad jump is the clear bottleneck.
  • Hierarchical controller robustness: 6 falls in 71 runs, 40 board touches (score ≈ 0.73), 25 full course completions (average 0.87).
  • Ablations: 1% dataset (≈176 episodes) insufficient; 10% and 100% perform similarly; longer context more effective; larger model size helps but limited by inference latency.
  • Direct multi-task RL training fails: a single policy trained on mixed three terrains cannot climb the A-frame nor complete the broad jump, proving steep slopes and jumps require expert-level training.
  • Best single run: 20.4s completion, score 0.91; peak velocity 2.3 m/s during jump segment.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Baseline methods use privileged information: environment CAD models and robot position from external motion capture systems; purely onboard sensor solutions not explored.
  • Broad jump success rate only 38%, and course-specific policies did not fully clear gaps in real environments, occasionally skipping or missing weave poles.
  • Slope traversal policy performs well in simulation but fails when transferred to the real A-frame, indicating unmodeled differences between simulated terrain and real obstacles (e.g., artificial turf friction, gaps).
  • Authors did not conduct multi-robot platform generalization experiments, nor test policies on unseen obstacle configurations; long-term stability and degradation tests not mentioned.
  • Real dogs never failed and were significantly faster than baselines; authors acknowledge this highlights dual improvement space in hardware (e.g., flexible spine) and algorithms.

Engineering Insights

  • Verify scoring formula and obstacle specifications first: Barkour scores heavily depend on v_target=1.69 m/s and nominal obstacle dimensions; reproduction must precisely follow Appendix B (e.g., A-frame floor-to-ramp gap <20mm, broad jump board flush with ground), otherwise scores are incomparable.
  • High-speed domain randomization is the biggest pitfall: Default parameters work below 1 m/s, but jumping/climbing requires additional randomization of torso inertia (40%–165%) and joint static friction (0–0.7), otherwise sim-to-real gaps directly cause broad jump failures.
  • Transformer distillation is worth prioritizing for reproduction: Locomotion-Transformer performs only slightly below the expert ensemble (0.73 vs 0.77), but requires no explicit obstacle type at deployment, and ablations show 10% data achieves near-full-data performance—a more economical choice for teams with limited compute.
  • Broad jump is the bottleneck; prioritize jump policy optimization: 38% success is the lowest across the course, with most failures from touching the board edge. Start with JP's three-stage curriculum (flat sprint → gaps → fixed gap fine-tuning) and check whether the knee height termination condition (<0.06m) is overly strict.
  • Match inference frequency with hardware: Policy position commands at 50 Hz, PD loop at 1 kHz, PD gains (20 N·m/rad, 0.5 N·m·s/rad); if changing actuators (e.g., T-Motor AK80-6 peak 12 N·m), redo system identification (differentiable optimization of joint gains and damping).
