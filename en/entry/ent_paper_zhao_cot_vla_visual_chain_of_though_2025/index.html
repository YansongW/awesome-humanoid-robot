Summary

CoT-VLA: Visual Chain-of-Thought Reasoning for Vision-Language-Action Models (CoT-VLA), is a 2025 large vision-language-action model for robotic manipulation, introduced by NVIDIA, Stanford University, MIT, and published at CVPR25.

Sources

  • CoT-VLA source

Overview

CoT-VLA is built by the author team based on VILA-U, marking the first time explicit visual chain-of-thought (CoT) is introduced into vision-language-action models (VLAs): it first autoregressively predicts future subgoal images as intermediate reasoning steps, then generates action sequences to achieve that goal, thereby endowing the model with temporal planning capabilities. The core contribution lies in leveraging action-free video data (EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something V2) to enhance visual reasoning, along with a designed hybrid attention mechanism (causal for image/text, full attention for actions) and action chunking (chunk size 10). On the LIBERO benchmark, it achieves an average success rate of 81.13%, an improvement of approximately 6% over OpenVLA (76.5%); on real-world Franka-Tabletop tasks, it outperforms the SOTA by 17%. The pretraining phase yields a 46.7% relative improvement (53.7%→78.8%), but generating 256 image tokens results in an average 7× inference slowdown, and the autoregressive image quality is lower than that of diffusion models.

What It Changes

The real change this work brings is not merely adding another module to VLA, but rather challenging a default assumption within the VLA paradigm that almost no one questions: vision-language-action models should map directly from observations to actions. CoT-VLA decomposes this single-step mapping into two explicit causal chains—first predicting "what the future will look like," then deciding "how to get there." This decomposition may seem simple, but it effectively pulls "planning" out of the latent space and turns it into an intermediate variable that is supervised, intervenable, and verifiable.

Why is this change substantive? Because existing VLAs (OpenVLA, Octo, etc.) essentially perform conditional probability modeling, where language instructions and the current frame directly determine the action distribution. This end-to-end mapping suffices for short-horizon tasks, but once a task requires multi-step reasoning (e.g., "first grasp A, then place it on B, and finally push C away"), the model lacks an explicit "intermediate state" to anchor the reasoning process. CoT-VLA uses subgoal images as this anchor, effectively installing a "self-checkpoint" in the model—it must first reason clearly about what it expects to see next before generating actions. This mechanism directly addresses the systematic shortcomings of VLA in long-horizon tasks (LIBERO long-horizon suite 69.0% vs. OpenVLA's 53.7%) and real-world multi-stage manipulation.

What is even more intriguing is the change at the data level. Subgoal image generation does not require action annotations, meaning CoT-VLA can consume vast amounts of action-free video (EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something V2) to pretrain visual reasoning capabilities. This is a fundamental assault on the VLA data bottleneck—robot demonstration data is scarce and expensive, but the internet is full of videos showing "a person looking at a scene and performing the next action." The authors demonstrate this through pretraining ablations: with pretraining, CoT-VLA achieves a 46.7% relative improvement over directly fine-tuning the base model (53.7%→78.8%). This number indicates that visual CoT capability is not learned from robot data but is "pre-installed" from action-free videos.

However, the change comes at a cost. Generating 256 image tokens results in an average 7× inference slowdown, which is fatal in real robot closed-loop control—high-frequency feedback is the lifeline of operational stability. The authors themselves acknowledge that action chunking (chunk size 10) introduces discontinuities between chunks, and that the primary cause of failure in Bridge-V2 visual generalization tasks is grasping actions rather than visual reasoning. This indicates that CoT-VLA's "thinking" has become stronger, but the precision of "doing" has been sacrificed. It changes the reasoning structure of VLA without simultaneously addressing the temporal coherence issues at the execution layer—this is the true gap left for future work.

Method Breakdown

The core of CoT-VLA is explicitly decomposing "think first, act later" into two autoregressive stages, rather than having the model jump directly from language instructions to action sequences. The overall architecture is based on VILA-U 7B, where input images are encoded into 16×16×4 tokens (residual depth 4), with all images uniformly resized to 256×256 resolution.

Two-Stage Reasoning

  • Stage One (Visual Reasoning): Given the current observation \(s_t\) and language instruction \(l\), the model autoregressively predicts the subgoal image \(\hat{s}_{t+n}\) at a future frame \(n\), serving as an intermediate visual reasoning step. Here, \(n\) is not fixed but uniformly sampled from a dataset-specific range \([n_l, n_u]\) (e.g., Bridge takes [5,10], TOTO takes [20,24]), enabling the model to plan across different temporal scales.
  • Stage Two (Action Generation): Based on the predicted subgoal image tokens and the original context, the model generates a sequence of \(m\) actions (action chunk size 10) to achieve that subgoal state. The action sequence is conditioned not on the original image but on the "imagined future frame"—this is the fundamental difference from direct input-output mapping VLAs.

Key Design Decisions

  • Visual Token Prediction: A deep transformer \(P_\delta\) autoregressively predicts \(D\) residual tokens, trained with a visual loss (Equation 4). Residual tokens are chosen over single-token prediction to improve generated image quality under limited computation—although the authors acknowledge that visual quality remains lower than diffusion-based models.
  • Action Discretization: Each action is represented by 7 tokens (corresponding to 7-DoF end-effector control), with each continuous action dimension independently mapped to 256 discrete bins. Bin widths are determined by uniformly dividing the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training data action distribution, rather than a fixed range—this avoids quantization waste caused by extreme action values. Action bin tokens directly borrow the 256 least frequently used tokens from the text tokenizer vocabulary, eliminating the need for vocabulary expansion.
  • Hybrid Attention Mechanism: Causal attention is used for image/text generation (ensuring autoregressive order), but full attention is used for action generation, allowing all action tokens within the same chunk to interact with each other. This is a deliberate design: action sequences exhibit strong correlations across dimensions (e.g., position and velocity), and full attention enables the model to jointly consider the entire action chunk during decoding, rather than generating each dimension independently.
  • Training Objective: The total loss is the sum of action loss and visual loss (Equation 6), with both stages jointly optimized rather than trained separately. The presence of the visual loss forces the LLM backbone to genuinely learn "imagining the future," rather than degenerating into pure action mapping.

Training Pipeline

  • Pretraining Phase: Joint training on an Open X-Embodiment subset (following OpenVLA preprocessing, selecting third-person views and single-arm 7-DoF control) and action-free video datasets (EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something V2). Action-free videos provide only language descriptions and images, used to strengthen visual reasoning—since subgoal image generation does not require action annotations, the model can learn common sense about "how states evolve" from massive video data. The LLM backbone, projector, and deep transformer are optimized, while the vision tower is frozen. Learning rate 1e-4, cosine decay, global batch size 2048, 10 epochs, consuming 11K A100 GPU hours.
  • Adaptation Phase: Fine-tuning on task-specific demonstrations for the target robot setup, maintaining the same training configuration (LLM backbone, projector, deep transformer trainable, vision tower frozen), but with a constant learning rate of 1e-5 for 150 epochs. Bridge-V2 additionally fine-tunes until the training action prediction accuracy reaches a 95% threshold.
  • Test-Time Closed-Loop Control (Algorithm 1): Iteratively executes "sample subgoal image → sample action sequence → execute actions → update observation." The action chunk size of 10 means observations are updated only every 10 steps, which explains the acknowledged "discontinuous actions between chunks" and "lack of high-frequency feedback" issues.

Ablation Validation

  • Pretraining brings significant gains: CoT-VLA with pretraining achieves a 46.7% relative improvement over directly fine-tuning the base VILA-U (53.7% → 78.8%), demonstrating the critical role of action-free video pretraining for visual reasoning.
  • Visual reasoning effectiveness: Using real target images yields sub-task 1/2 success rates of 60%/40%, while using generated target images drops to 20%/0%—an absolute gap of 40%. This indicates that current generated image quality remains a bottleneck, but the mechanism itself (subgoal as an intermediate) is effective.

Key Innovations

The core innovation of CoT-VLA lies in explicitly separating "thinking" and "doing" at the architectural level, using subgoal image generation as an intermediate reasoning step rather than having the model jump directly from language instructions to action sequences. Three aspects of this design deserve deeper examination:

First, it is the first to introduce subgoal image generation as a chain-of-thought (CoT) component in VLA. Previous VLAs (such as OpenVLA) were essentially end-to-end "input-output" mappings, lacking explicit modeling of intermediate task states. CoT-VLA enables the model to first autoregressively predict the subgoal image ŝ_{t+n} at a future frame n, then generate action sequences based on that image. This decomposition of "first clarify what visual state to achieve, then decide how to move" directly addresses the need for temporal planning in complex manipulation tasks. Key evidence comes from ablations: with generated target images, sub-task success rates are 20%/0%, which jump to 60%/40% when replaced with real target images—an absolute improvement of 40 percentage points—indicating that the visual reasoning component itself is the performance bottleneck, and while the model-generated subgoal images are imperfect, they already carry effective planning information.

Second, subgoal image generation does not require action annotations, opening the door to leveraging massive action-free video data. Traditional VLA training is constrained by robot demonstration data (action annotations are expensive and scarce), whereas CoT-VLA's visual reasoning stage relies only on image sequences, allowing direct pretraining on large-scale human videos such as EPIC-KITCHENS and Something-Something V2. The gains from pretraining are tangible: compared to directly fine-tuning VILA-U, the version with pretraining achieves a 46.7% relative improvement (from 53.7% to 78.8%). This effectively liberates VLA's visual understanding from "robot-exclusive data," first learning to "understand state changes" from richer human manipulation videos, then transferring to robot control.

Third, the hybrid attention mechanism resolves the conflict between multimodal generation and action prediction. Causal attention is used for image/text generation (ensuring autoregressive consistency), while full attention is used for action generation (allowing the 7 action tokens to interact), preventing the loss of coordination information between action dimensions due to causal masking. Combined with action chunking (chunk size 10) and 256 discrete bin action tokenization, the model can output coherent action sequences rather than single-step decisions. Though subtle, this design directly impacts action quality—in Bridge-V2 motion-type tasks, CoT-VLA achieves 60% vs. OpenVLA's 45%, with the gap stemming from coordinated action generation.

It is worth noting that the authors candidly acknowledge that generating 256 image tokens incurs an average 7× inference slowdown, and image quality is inferior to diffusion models—this is an explicit trade-off of computation for planning capability, not a cost-free improvement.

Experiments and Results

CoT-VLA's experimental design spans three levels: simulation benchmarks (LIBERO), real-robot generalization (Bridge-V2), and a self-built real-world tabletop task (Franka-Tabletop), supplemented by two key ablations validating the independent contributions of pretraining and visual reasoning components.

LIBERO Benchmark (Table 1) serves as the primary comparison arena, with 500 trials per task suite and 3 random seeds. CoT-VLA-7B leads all baselines with an average of 81.13±0.6%, with long-horizon tasks (69.0±0.8%) showing an 18.5 percentage point improvement over Diffusion Policy (50.5±1.3%) (calculated from table values 88.4→72.4), directly confirming the value of subgoal images as intermediate reasoning steps for multi-stage tasks. Notably, on object tasks, Diffusion Policy (92.5±0.7%) slightly outperforms CoT-VLA (91.6±0.5%), indicating that in single-step scenarios with clear object attributes, the gains from explicit visual reasoning are limited.

Bridge-V2 Benchmark (Table 2) uses partial credit scoring with 10 trials per category, examining four generalization dimensions. CoT-VLA leads substantially in motion (60%) and semantic (50%) categories, exceeding OpenVLA by 15 and 10 percentage points respectively, indicating that visual reasoning through future frame prediction effectively encodes kinematic and semantic changes. Ablation Experiments provide the most compelling causal evidence. The pretraining ablation shows that adding pretraining on the Open X-Embodiment subset and action-free videos (EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something V2) yields a 46.7% relative improvement over directly fine-tuning the base VILA-U model (53.7%→78.8%), validating the transferable gains of action-free video data for visual reasoning. The visual reasoning ablation (Table 3) is even more decisive: with generated target images, sub-task 1 success is 20% and sub-task 2 is 0%; replacing with real target images raises these to 60% and 40% respectively—an absolute improvement of 40 percentage points. This comparison clearly demonstrates that current autoregressive image generation quality is the performance bottleneck—the distribution shift between generated images and real states directly limits downstream action prediction accuracy.

Franka-Tabletop overall performance improvements (17% relative improvement over state-of-the-art VLA in the real world, 6% on simulation benchmarks) align with the ablation conclusions: CoT-VLA's architectural advantages are fully realized in long-horizon, multi-stage tasks, but are constrained by generated image quality and execution discontinuities from action chunking. The 7× inference slowdown (from generating 256 image tokens) and inter-chunk action discontinuities are explicit costs of the current design in terms of real-time performance and smooth control.

Boundaries and Limitations

The boundaries and limitations of CoT-VLA are first evident in computational cost: generating 256 image tokens during inference incurs an average 7× slowdown (with action chunk size 10), making it difficult to deploy in real-time control scenarios, and the authors acknowledge that current computational constraints limit the ability to achieve visual reasoning generalization for entirely new tasks. Second, the visual quality of autoregressive image generation is lower than diffusion-based SOTA models, meaning the reliability of subgoal images as intermediate reasoning steps has an upper bound—if the intermediate visual representation itself is lossy, downstream action generation will inevitably inherit errors.

A more fundamental limitation lies in the action chunking mechanism: discontinuous actions may occur between chunks, and there is a lack of high-frequency feedback during execution. This cost is already visible in Bridge-V2 visual and language generalization tasks—CoT-VLA success rates (65%, 70%) are lower than OpenVLA (75%, 75%), which the authors attribute to grasping failures rather than visual reasoning errors, but this precisely indicates that the action execution layer is not robust enough in out-of-distribution scenarios. Furthermore, out-of-distribution subgoal generation remains difficult; the pretraining ablation shows that the 46.7% relative improvement depends on large-scale pretraining data (OpenX, EPIC-KITCHENS, Something-Something V2), and if the target domain differs significantly from the pretraining distribution, this gain may shrink considerably.

What the authors did not do is equally critical: they did not validate cross-embodiment generalization on real robots (only Franka and WidowX platforms), did not discuss the sensitivity of the subgoal prediction time step n sampling range (e.g., RoboTurk's u_l=1, u_u=2) to long-horizon task performance, and did not analyze how the weight balance between visual loss and action loss affects the final policy. In the visual reasoning ablation, generated target images achieve only 20%/0% sub-task success rates versus 60%/40% for real images—this 40% absolute gap reveals the chasm between current generative visual reasoning and ideal intermediate representations, and the paper does not provide a mechanistic explanation for bridging this gap.

Engineering Insights

Before reproducing CoT-VLA, verify two things first: the data mixing weight table (Table 4), where per-dataset weights and subgoal ranges [u_l, u_u] are hand-tuned, and blindly copying them will lead to pitfalls—for example, RoboTurk's u_l=1, u_u=2 means predicting very near-future frames, while TOTO's u_l=20, u_u=24 requires the model to look 20 frames ahead; if your downstream task's temporal scale does not match, visual reasoning will fail. Second, the action discretization details: each action dimension is mapped to 256 bins, with bin widths determined by the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training data action distribution, meaning the action distribution tail is sensitive—if your dataset contains abnormally large actions, bin widths will be stretched, causing quantization errors to surge for small actions.

The most common pitfall is inference latency: generating 256 image tokens incurs an average 7× slowdown (with action chunk size 10), significantly reducing closed-loop control frequency. If the downstream task requires high-frequency feedback (e.g., force-controlled insertion), this approach is unsuitable; if the task allows second-level decision-making, sufficient computational headroom must be reserved. Another subtle issue is inter-chunk action discontinuity—with chunk size 10, there is no high-frequency feedback correction at chunk boundaries, and the lower performance on Bridge-V2 visual/language generalization tasks compared to OpenVLA stems precisely from grasping failures, not visual reasoning errors. When reproducing, it is recommended to first run single-task fine-tuning (10-24 hours on a single A100 node), confirm that training action prediction accuracy meets the threshold (95% for Bridge-V2), and then scale up.
