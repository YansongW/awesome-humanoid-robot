Overview

The design of bearing and output interface is a critical process node in the actuator and drive system design for humanoid robot joint modules, numbered P4.2.3. It addresses how the joint output end, when subjected to combined radial, axial, and moment loads, ensures joint life, stiffness, and assembly maintainability through rational bearing arrangement, fit tolerances, and interface definition. What it truly changes is not the selection of individual bearings, but the precision retention capability and predictability of failure modes of the entire joint over long-term service.

Content

What It Is: The "Skeletal Extremity" and "Load-Bearing Throat" of the Joint

Bearing and output interface design is the mechanical interface layer in a joint module that connects the reducer output end to downstream structural components (such as the shank or forearm). It encompasses three core objects: bearing arrangement scheme (determining how loads are transmitted), fit tolerance system (determining assembly precision and interference/clearance states), and output interface geometry (determining the dimensions and tolerances of flanges, keyways, or splines).

Within the process context of P4.2.3, this node receives the joint load spectrum output from upstream dynamics analysis and provides interface constraints to downstream body structure design. It is not an isolated design step but a complete engineering closed loop from input review to document delivery, comprising five third-level subtasks from P4.2.3.1 to P4.2.3.5.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The joint output end of humanoid robots faces a contradiction rarely encountered in industrial robots: load directions are highly variable with frequent impacts, yet joint space is extremely limited. The joint output ends of industrial manipulators typically bear only well-defined radial or axial loads with ample installation space; however, the hip, knee, and ankle joints of humanoid robots endure combined radial forces, axial forces, and overturning moments during walking, jumping, and falling, and the joint diameter is constrained by human body proportions, preventing unlimited enlargement of bearing dimensions.

Improper bearing arrangement leads to two typical failure modes: first, insufficient life—under combined loads, the actual bearing life falls far below the rated life calculated for a single load type; second, insufficient stiffness—bearing clearance or overly loose fits cause radial runout at the output end, manifesting as millimeter-level pose errors at the terminal, which in legged robots directly appears as gait instability. Therefore, the historical positioning of P4.2.3 is: within limited space, use bearing life calculation and fit tolerance analysis to secure long-term joint reliability and dynamic precision.

Principle Breakdown

① Load Decomposition: Equivalent Treatment of Combined Loads

The loads on the joint output end can be decomposed into radial force \( F_r \), axial force \( F_a \), and overturning moment \( M \). For crossed roller bearings or angular contact ball bearing combinations, the overturning moment can be equivalently represented as a pair of axial forces:

\[ F_{a,\text{eq}} = \frac{M}{d_p} \quad \text{（} d_p \text{ is the bearing pitch circle diameter）} \]

The equivalent axial force is superimposed on the original axial force and substituted into the bearing equivalent dynamic load calculation formula:

\[ P = X F_r + Y F_a \]

where \( X \) and \( Y \) are the radial and axial load coefficients, determined by the bearing type and contact angle. This step is the physical foundation of the entire life verification—without equivalently converting the moment to an axial force, the life calculation is incorrect.

② Life Verification: From Rated Life to Modified Life

The basic rated life of a bearing is calculated according to ISO standards:

\[ L_{10} = \left( \frac{C}{P} \right)^p \times 10^6 \text{ (revolutions)} \]

where \( C \) is the basic dynamic load rating and \( p \) is the life exponent (3 for ball bearings, 10/3 for roller bearings). In engineering practice, reliability factors, material factors, and lubrication factors must also be introduced for correction to obtain the modified rated life. For humanoid robot joints, the target life is typically converted from walking distance or joint revolutions, and specific values need to be confirmed independently.

③ Fit Tolerances: The Trade-off Between Interference and Clearance

The fits between the bearing inner ring and shaft, and between the outer ring and housing, determine the bearing's radial clearance and support stiffness. The rotating ring (typically the inner ring) should use an interference fit, while the stationary ring should use a transition or clearance fit. Excessive interference eliminates the bearing's internal clearance, causing heat generation and binding; insufficient interference produces fretting, where reddish-brown powder appears on the mating surfaces after prolonged operation—a common root cause of joint noise and precision degradation.

Horizontal Comparison

Crossed roller bearings and angular contact ball bearing combinations are the two most common solutions for joint output ends, each with its applicable scenarios:

  • Crossed roller bearings: Rollers are arranged at 90° crossing angles; a single bearing can simultaneously carry radial, axial, and overturning moment loads, offering compact structure and high stiffness. Suitable for high-load joints such as hips and knees, but with higher cost and stringent flatness requirements for mounting surfaces.
  • Angular contact ball bearing combinations: Two or three angular contact ball bearings are mounted face-to-face or back-to-back, with preload applied to eliminate clearance and increase stiffness. Suitable for medium-load scenarios such as ankle joints, with lower cost, but requiring greater axial space and precise preload control.

Engineering judgment: if joint diameter is constrained and loads are dominated by overturning moments, crossed roller bearings are preferred; if axial space is ample and cost is sensitive, angular contact combinations are preferred. Both solutions require fit tolerance analysis to verify the actual clearance and preload state after installation.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

P4.2.3 is a standard process node in the joint module design workflow, executed in the following scenarios:

  • Full-size bipedal humanoid robot hip joint: Bears full body weight and walking impacts, using crossed roller bearings with interference fits for shaft and housing to ensure precision retention after prolonged walking.
  • Collaborative manipulator joints: Although loads are smaller, high stiffness and low friction are required; angular contact ball bearing combinations with preload are used to achieve low resistance during backdriving.
  • Exoskeleton robot knee joint: Loads vary dramatically with gait cycles; life verification ensures survival under peak loads, while lightweight thin-section bearings are selected for weight reduction.

At the process execution level, P4.2.3.1 requires listing all upstream input items and confirming versions; P4.2.3.2 requires forming no fewer than 2 candidate solutions and establishing an evaluation matrix; P4.2.3.3 requires producing prototypes and recording key parameters; P4.2.3.4 requires developing a test plan and outputting an issue list; P4.2.3.5 requires updating ICD/BOM/SOP and completing downstream delivery.

Limitations and Boundaries

P4.2.3 has clear boundaries but the following limitations:

  1. Static load assumption: Life calculations are based on rated loads and speeds, whereas humanoid robot joint loads are highly dynamic impact loads. Engineering practice typically uses equivalent load methods for conversion, but impact peaks may far exceed equivalent values, requiring verification through prototype durability testing.
  2. Temperature and lubrication effects: Bearing life is sensitive to lubrication conditions, and joint internal space is limited; grease fill volume and seal design require dedicated consideration. Grease degradation at high temperatures significantly shortens life; temperature ranges need independent confirmation.
  3. Dependence on assembly processes: No matter how well fit tolerances are designed, if press-in forces or heating temperatures are not controlled during assembly, actual clearances may deviate significantly from design values. The prototype fabrication in P4.2.3.3 and verification in P4.2.3.4 are precisely intended to expose such issues.
  4. Does not cover motor-side bearings: P4.2.3 focuses on the output end; bearings inside the motor are the responsibility of the motor supplier and fall outside the scope of this process.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Bigger bearings are more durable"—Incorrect. Bearing dimensions are constrained by joint space, and oversized bearings increase rotational inertia, degrading joint dynamic response. The correct approach is to trade off between space and life, using higher-grade materials or better lubrication when necessary.
  2. "Life calculation only requires substituting the rated load"—Incorrect. Humanoid robot joint loads are combined; the overturning moment must be equivalently converted to an axial force before calculating the equivalent dynamic load. Life calculations that ignore the moment will overestimate life by several times.
  3. "Tighter interference fits are always better"—Incorrect. Excessive interference eliminates the bearing's internal clearance, causing rolling element binding, heat generation, and even seizure. The goal of fit tolerance analysis is to find the window that "provides support stiffness without eliminating clearance."
  4. "Crossed roller bearings do not require preload"—Incorrect. Although crossed roller bearings inherently have high stiffness, preload is still required in joints demanding zero backlash; otherwise, micro-clearance appears during load reversal, manifesting as terminal vibration.
