Overview

The Actuator & Drive System Design is the 4th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, broken down into several Level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. The core task of this stage is to transform the joint requirements output from upstream dynamics analysis into actuator solutions that are manufacturable, controllable, and reliable over long-term operation. It serves as the critical bridge connecting "how much force the robot needs" with "how the motor should spin." What it truly changes is not the selection of a single motor, but the physical ceiling of the entire robot's dynamic performance and energy efficiency.

Content

What It Is: Engineering Translation from Requirements to Hardware

Joint module and drive system design is essentially mapping the joint-level requirements derived from kinematics and dynamics analysis into specific selections and integration schemes for motors, reducers, encoders, drivers, and structural components. In humanoid robots, each joint is a miniature electromechanical system: the motor provides raw power, the reducer amplifies torque and adjusts speed, the encoder feeds back position and velocity, the driver controls current and torque, and the structural components bear loads while ensuring stiffness.

This stage sits at position 4 in WBS V3, receiving the dynamics analysis results from Stage 3 and providing interface constraints for the body structure design in Stage 5. It covers the complete engineering workflow—input review, solution design, implementation/prototyping, verification loops, and documentation delivery—making it a key node that ensures downstream dependents receive qualified inputs.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robot joint design has long faced a fundamental contradiction: humanoid robots require high dynamic response, but traditional industrial robot joints have large reduction ratios, high inertia, and poor back-drivability. Industrial manipulators can tolerate harmonic reduction ratios above 100:1 because their loads are fixed and motion patterns are predictable; however, humanoid robots—when walking, jumping, or being pushed by external forces—require joints that can quickly respond to impacts, sense external forces, and actively comply. If the reduction ratio is too large, motor inertia is amplified by \(G^2\), the joint cannot "feel" external forces, and force control and compliant control become impossible.

Historically, two technical routes have attempted to resolve this contradiction: one is high-torque-density motors + low reduction ratios (quasi-direct drive, QDD), sacrificing some peak torque for transparency; the other is high reduction ratios + torque sensors (e.g., harmonic drives + joint torque sensors), compensating for insufficient transparency with sensors. The former is structurally simple and low-cost, while the latter offers high torque density but significantly increased complexity and cost. The design decisions in the P4 stage are essentially a trade-off between these two routes.

Principle Breakdown

① Quantifying Torque Requirements: Safety Factors and Thermal Margins

The starting point for refining joint performance requirements is the dynamic peak torque output from P3.1.3. Engineering practice dictates that motors should not be selected directly based on peak torque, because peak torque typically lasts only a very short time, while continuous motor torque is limited by the thermal time constant. Therefore:

\[ \tau_{\text{peak, design}} = \tau_{\text{dynamics, peak}} \times (1.5\text{–}2.0) \]

The safety factor of 1.5–2.0 covers uncertainties such as modeling errors, load variations, and impact loads. Continuous torque is determined based on sustained walking conditions—when a humanoid robot walks, the knee joint bears loads close to body weight during the stance phase but is nearly unloaded during the swing phase. Thus, continuous torque is usually far lower than peak torque, but it must ensure the motor does not overheat throughout the entire sustained walking cycle.

② Actuator Topology: Trade-off Between Transparency and Torque Density

Actuator topology selection is the most critical decision in the P4 stage. The four mainstream solutions each have their applicable scenarios:

Topology Type Reduction Ratio Range Back-drive Transparency Torque Density Typical Joint Applications
Direct Drive 1:1 Extremely High Low Small research platforms
QDD (Quasi-Direct Drive) 3–10:1 High Medium High-dynamic joints such as hip and ankle
Planetary + Frameless Motor 10–50:1 Medium Medium-High Medium-load joints such as knee and elbow
Harmonic Drive 50–160:1 Low High High-torque, compact joints such as shoulder and wrist

High-dynamic joints (hip/ankle) tend to favor QDD because impact forces during walking require the joint to be quickly back-drivable to absorb energy; high-torque, compact joints (shoulder/wrist) tend to favor harmonic drives because end-effector loads are relatively fixed, transparency requirements are lower, and volume and weight constraints are stricter.

③ Back-drive Transparency: The Physical Foundation of Force Control

Back-drivability refers to whether an external force can "push back" the motor from the output side. In a joint with high transparency, an external force can directly rotate the motor, the motor's back-EMF generates resistive torque, and the controller can estimate the external force by sensing current—this is the physical basis of "sensorless force control." The relationship between transparency and reduction ratio is:

\[ \tau_{\text{back-drive, friction}} \propto G \cdot \tau_{\text{motor, friction}} + \tau_{\text{gear, friction}} \]

The larger the reduction ratio \(G\), the more the motor-side friction is amplified by \(G\) times at the output, making the joint "stiffer." The core advantage of the QDD approach lies precisely here: a low reduction ratio compresses friction and backlash nonlinearities into an acceptable range, making "force control without torque sensors" possible.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Core parameters for joint module design include:

  • Peak Torque: Determined by dynamic peak torque × 1.5–2.0 safety factor, defining the ultimate capability of the motor and reducer
  • Continuous Torque: Determined by sustained walking conditions, constrained by the motor's thermal time constant and heat dissipation capability
  • Maximum Speed: Determined by the highest joint angular velocity from kinematics analysis, influencing reducer ratio selection
  • Bandwidth Requirement: Force-controlled joints require high control bandwidth (typically > 50 Hz), demanding low motor inertia and high driver sampling rates
  • Back-drive Torque: Measures transparency; QDD solutions typically require back-drive resistance < 5 N·m
  • Motor Thermal Time Constant: Determines the sustainable duration of continuous torque, influencing thermal design

Horizontal Comparison

Comparing the P4 stage with adjacent stages clarifies its boundaries:

  • Relationship with P3 (Dynamics Analysis): P3 outputs "requirements" (torque, speed, bandwidth), while P4 outputs "solutions" (motor models, reduction ratios, drivers). P3 is pure computation; P4 is selection and integration.
  • Relationship with P5 (Body Structure Design): P4 determines the joint module's external dimensions, mounting interfaces, and weight; P5 designs the skeleton and linkages based on these. P4's dimensional constraints directly affect P5's structural layout.
  • Comparison with Industrial Robot Joint Design: Industrial robot joints prioritize repeatable positioning accuracy and stiffness, while humanoid robot joints prioritize transparency and dynamic response. The former can accept large reduction ratios; the latter must keep reduction ratios within a reasonable range.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The design methodology of the P4 stage has been applied in several well-known humanoid robot projects:

  • MIT Cheetah series: Adopted the QDD approach with motors + low-ratio planetary reducers, achieving high-dynamic running and jumping, validating a transparency-first design philosophy.
  • Unitree series: Hip and ankle joints use QDD or low-ratio solutions to ensure impact absorption and force control performance during walking.
  • Tesla Optimus: Shoulder and wrist joints use harmonic drive solutions, achieving high torque output within limited volume for fine manipulation tasks.

The common thread across these cases is: high-dynamic joints (hip, ankle) prioritize transparency, while high-torque joints (shoulder, wrist) prioritize torque density—fully consistent with the design logic of the P4 stage.

Limitations and Boundaries

Design decisions in the P4 stage have clear boundary conditions:

  • A larger safety factor is not always better: 1.5–2.0 is an empirical range; an excessively large safety factor causes motor and reducer volume and weight to balloon, actually degrading dynamic performance. Engineering judgment: take the upper bound for knee joints subject to frequent walking impacts; take the lower bound for wrist joints with relatively stable loads.
  • QDD is not a universal solution: A low reduction ratio means the motor must provide greater raw torque, requiring larger motor volume and higher current, placing greater demands on the battery and driver. In scenarios requiring extremely high torque density (e.g., finger joints), QDD is not suitable.
  • Thermal management is a hidden constraint: Continuous torque is limited by the thermal time constant, but heat dissipation capability depends on structural design. The P4 stage must consider thermal paths during selection; otherwise, discovering overheating issues later will be difficult to remedy.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The smaller the reduction ratio, the better"—Wrong. If the reduction ratio is too small, the motor must operate in a low-efficiency region and requires a larger motor volume. The point is "low enough to preserve transparency," not blindly minimizing.
  2. "Meeting peak torque is sufficient"—Wrong. Insufficient continuous motor torque leads to overheating; a motor that meets peak torque but lacks continuous torque will be derated due to overheating during sustained walking, with actual performance far below the rated value.
  3. "Harmonic reducers are better because they are more precise"—Wrong. The flexspline of a harmonic reducer exhibits elastic deformation, introducing nonlinear stiffness in force-control scenarios and actually increasing control difficulty. High precision does not equal high transparency.
  4. "A safety factor of 2.0 is the safest choice"—Wrong. The safety factor is the result of trade-offs; an excessively large safety factor makes joints bulky and reduces overall dynamic performance. Engineering judgment: the safety factor should match the joint's dynamic requirements.
