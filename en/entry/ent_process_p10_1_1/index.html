Overview

Fusing IMU and joint encoders is a core process node in legged robot state estimation, belonging to the state estimation and perception fusion segment within the Motion Control algorithm development and validation work package. This method fuses the high-frequency attitude data from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) with kinematic information from joint encoders to estimate the robot's center of mass position, velocity, and attitude in real time, providing fundamental state input for balance control. Its essence lies in addressing the complementary limitations of individual sensors—IMU suffers from integration drift, while encoder-based kinematic estimation lacks a global attitude reference—and their fusion achieves complementary advantages.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

IMU and joint encoder fusion is a multi-sensor state estimation method whose core idea is to optimally combine two types of heterogeneous sensing information within a filtering framework. The IMU provides three-axis acceleration and three-axis angular velocity with high sampling rates but suffers from integration drift; joint encoders provide individual joint angles, from which foot positions and body motion increments can be derived via forward kinematics, but lack an absolute attitude reference. The fusion objective is: within each control cycle, output an optimal estimate of the robot's center of mass position, velocity, and attitude, with bounded estimation errors and controllable drift.

This process node corresponds to P10.1.1 in the development workflow and includes five third-level subtasks: input analysis and target quantification (P10.1.1.1), solution/method design (P10.1.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P10.1.1.3), verification and issue closure (P10.1.1.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P10.1.1.5). What it truly changes is not the sensors themselves, but rather transforming "two individually imperfect sensors" into "one sufficiently reliable state source"—this is the watershed determining whether balance control can transition from simulation to physical hardware.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In the early development of legged robots, state estimation primarily relied on single-IMU integration. This approach was acceptable over short durations (seconds), but over time, double integration of accelerometer noise causes position estimates to diverge with cubic growth, and attitude estimates gradually deviate from true values due to gyroscope bias accumulation. Pure kinematic estimation (using only joint encoders) faces another issue: when feet slip, the kinematic assumption of "no relative sliding between foot and ground" no longer holds, leading to unpredictable position estimation errors.

The emergence of fusion methods directly addresses both pain points. It combines the IMU's high-frequency dynamic response with the encoder's low-frequency but drift-free kinematic constraints, achieving complementarity within a filtering framework. From a historical perspective, this technology is a key technical milestone for legged robots transitioning from "being able to walk" to "walking stably"—without reliable state estimation, any high-performance balance controller is impossible. What it truly changes is not algorithmic complexity, but the way of answering the fundamental question of "what state is the robot currently in": from "guessing" to "computing."

Principle Breakdown

① Sensor Models and Complementary Characteristics

The IMU measurement model can be expressed as:

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{b}_g + \mathbf{n}_g, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{R}^{\top}(\mathbf{g} + \mathbf{a}) + \mathbf{b}_a + \mathbf{n}_a \]

where \(\mathbf{b}_g\), \(\mathbf{b}_a\) are the gyroscope and accelerometer biases, and \(\mathbf{n}_g\), \(\mathbf{n}_a\) are Gaussian white noise. The IMU's advantage lies in its high frequency (typically 100–1000 Hz) and its inclusion of an absolute gravity reference (the accelerometer can sense the direction of gravity), but position information requires double integration, making drift inevitable.

Joint encoders, on the other hand, compute body velocity through forward kinematics:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{\text{base}} = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}} \]

where \(\mathbf{J}\) is the Jacobian matrix and \(\mathbf{q}\) is the joint angles. This method has no integration drift but relies on the accuracy of the kinematic model and cannot provide absolute attitude.

② Filtering Fusion Framework

The most commonly used fusion framework is the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). Its core idea is: use the IMU as the state prediction source (high-frequency propagation) and encoder-based kinematic estimation as the measurement update source (low-frequency correction). The state vector typically includes position, velocity, attitude quaternion, gyroscope bias, and accelerometer bias. The prediction step is driven by the IMU, and the update step is driven by kinematic constraints.

The key design point lies in the setting of covariance matrices: the IMU noise covariance determines prediction uncertainty, and the encoder measurement noise covariance determines the weight of corrections. Covariance tuning is the most critical aspect in engineering—setting it too small causes filter divergence, while setting it too large renders the fusion meaningless. Engineering judgment: initial covariances are typically set based on nominal noise densities from sensor datasheets, then iteratively adjusted using experimental data.

③ Zero Velocity Update and Contact Constraints

When the robot is standing or in the support phase, the foot velocity is theoretically zero. This constraint can be injected into the filter as a virtual measurement, significantly suppressing drift. The Zero Velocity Update (ZUPT) implementation detects the support phase and uses zero foot velocity as a measurement update. More advanced contact-aided methods (such as Contact-Aided InEKF) model contact events as state constraints, leveraging Lie group theory to achieve invariance of error states and enhance estimation robustness.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

This fusion method is widely applied across various legged robot platforms. Boston Dynamics' Atlas employs IMU and joint encoder fusion for state estimation in its balance control; domestic robotics companies such as Unitree and Yushu use similar frameworks in their bipedal/quadrupedal products. In academic research, platforms such as MIT Cheetah and ETH ANYmal have publicly released state estimation implementations based on EKF or InEKF.

In the development workflow, the output of this process node (the state estimator) directly serves downstream balance control, gait planning, and navigation modules. Specifically, the P10.1.1.4 verification phase requires "measured trajectory error < target, standing drift controllable," which necessitates validating the estimator's accuracy and stability through actual walking experiments.

Limitations and Boundaries

This fusion method has several inherent limitations. First, IMU and encoder fusion is fundamentally a relative positioning method that cannot provide global absolute position—position drift over extended operation is inevitable, requiring external sensors such as vision, LiDAR, or GPS for absolute correction. Second, foot slip is the primary failure mode for kinematic measurements; on slippery or loose terrain, encoder-derived velocity information becomes unreliable and may mislead the filter. Third, covariance tuning is highly experience-dependent, requiring re-tuning across different platforms and operating conditions, lacking adaptability. Fourth, EKF linearization errors may increase during strongly nonlinear motions (such as rapid turns or jumps), potentially causing estimation divergence.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Fusion is simply weighted averaging"—Wrong. True fusion is optimal estimation within a filtering framework that accounts for sensor noise statistics and motion models; simple weighting cannot handle time-varying noise and state coupling.

  2. "More expensive IMUs are always better"—Not entirely. In fusion frameworks, noise matching between the IMU and encoders matters more than absolute accuracy. A high-performance IMU paired with low-precision encoders may actually degrade filtering performance due to improper covariance settings.

  3. "Covariance tuning is a one-time effort"—Unrealistic. In actual operation, ground conditions, load variations, and temperature drift all affect sensor noise characteristics, requiring online adaptation or periodic re-tuning of covariances.

  4. "Zero velocity update only works when stationary"—Inaccurate. The constraint of zero foot velocity during the support phase also holds during walking; the key lies in accurately detecting the transition between support and swing phases.
