Overview

The Thevenin terminal voltage equation is a fundamental equation describing the port characteristics of a linear active two-terminal network. It states that the terminal voltage equals the open-circuit voltage minus the voltage drop across the equivalent resistance. This equation compresses the external behavior of any arbitrarily complex linear network into two parameters, serving as the cornerstone of battery modeling, power supply design, and signal conditioning circuit analysis.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Thevenin terminal voltage equation (Thevenin Terminal Voltage Equation) is the algebraic expression of Thevenin's equivalent theorem. For any linear time-invariant active two-terminal network, viewed from the port, the terminal voltage \( V_{ab} \) satisfies:

\[ V_{ab} = V_{oc} - R_{th} \, I_{ab} \]

where \( V_{oc} \) is the open-circuit voltage, \( R_{th} \) is the equivalent resistance (Thevenin Equivalent Resistance), and \( I_{ab} \) is the current flowing out of the port. This equation corresponds one-to-one with the Thevenin equivalent circuit (ent_formalism_thevenin_equivalent_circuit): the ideal voltage source in the equivalent circuit equals \( V_{oc} \), and the series resistance equals \( R_{th} \). What it truly changes is not the computational burden of circuit analysis, but the engineer's way of thinking about complex networks—reducing a black box to two measurable parameters.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Before Thevenin's theorem was proposed (published by Léon Charles Thévenin in 1883), analyzing the impact of a complex linear network on a specific branch required solving the entire network's node or mesh equations. As network size grew, the computational load increased exponentially, and every change of the load branch necessitated a complete re-solution. The Thevenin terminal voltage equation addressed this pain point: it fully parameterizes the network's external behavior at the port as \( V_{oc} \) and \( R_{th} \), so that load changes only require recalculating a first-order equation. This idea became the theoretical foundation for power supply design, signal attenuation analysis, and impedance matching throughout 20th-century electronics development. In the field of humanoid robots, it directly supports terminal voltage estimation in battery management systems (BMS) and voltage drop budgeting in power distribution networks.

Principle Breakdown

① Open-Circuit Voltage: The Network's "Intrinsic Driving Force" When the port current \( I_{ab} = 0 \), the terminal voltage equals the open-circuit voltage \( V_{oc} \). It is determined jointly by all independent sources inside the network and is independent of the external load. For networks containing controlled sources, calculating \( V_{oc} \) requires preserving the control relationships of the controlled sources.

② Equivalent Resistance: The Network's "Intrinsic Impedance" Short-circuit all independent voltage sources, open-circuit all independent current sources, and preserve controlled sources within the network; the equivalent resistance seen from the port is \( R_{th} \). It characterizes the network's resistance to port current and determines the slope at which terminal voltage drops with load current.

③ Manifestation of the Superposition Principle For linear networks with multiple independent sources, \( V_{oc} \) equals the algebraic superposition of port voltages when each independent source acts alone. This property allows the equation to be analyzed source by source, simplifying the debugging of complex power systems.

④ Corollary for Power Transfer From the terminal voltage equation, the maximum power transfer theorem can be derived: when the load resistance \( R_L = R_{th} \), the load receives maximum power \( P_{max} = V_{oc}^2 / (4R_{th}) \). In humanoid robot power supply design, this corollary is used to match battery internal resistance with the input impedance of power converters.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Parameter Symbol Physical Meaning Typical Acquisition Method
Open-circuit voltage \( V_{oc} \) Terminal voltage when the port is open Direct measurement or nodal analysis
Equivalent resistance \( R_{th} \) Port resistance after zeroing independent sources Measure short-circuit current \( I_{sc} \), then compute \( R_{th} = V_{oc}/I_{sc} \)
Terminal voltage \( V_{ab} \) Actual port voltage under load Computed from the equation or measured online
Port current \( I_{ab} \) Current flowing out of the positive port terminal Determined by the load; positive direction is out of the positive terminal

Among the above parameters, \( V_{oc} \) and \( R_{th} \) are intrinsic properties of the network, independent of the load; \( V_{ab} \) and \( I_{ab} \) are operating-point variables. For battery models, \( V_{oc} \) varies with state of charge (SOC), and \( R_{th} \) varies with temperature and aging state, requiring experimental calibration (engineering judgment).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Terminal Voltage Estimation in Battery Management Systems (BMS) Humanoid robots (e.g., Unitree H1, released in 2024, entry-level price around $16,000) are powered by lithium-ion battery packs. The BMS uses the terminal voltage equation to build an equivalent circuit model of the battery: the open-circuit voltage \( V_{oc} \) is obtained by table lookup from the SOC-OCV curve, the equivalent resistance \( R_{th} \) is given by the internal resistance model, and the real-time terminal voltage \( V_{ab} \) is used for state-of-charge estimation and over-discharge protection.

② Voltage Drop Budgeting in Power Distribution Networks The peak power of joint motors in humanoid robots can reach hundreds of watts, making voltage drops across power cables non-negligible. Designers use the terminal voltage equation to calculate the worst-case voltage drop from the battery to the driver input: \( V_{ab} = V_{oc} - R_{th}I_{peak} \), ensuring sufficient margin for the driver's minimum operating voltage.

③ Signal Conditioning Circuit Design Sensor signals (e.g., from joint torque sensors) are attenuated by voltage dividers before being fed into ADCs. Voltage divider design is essentially a special case of the terminal voltage equation with \( R_{th}=0 \); engineers use this equation to calculate the effects of division ratio and input impedance on measurement accuracy.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Applicable only to linear networks: Networks containing nonlinear elements such as diodes and transistors cannot directly use the terminal voltage equation; small-signal linearization around the operating point is required.
  2. Time-varying networks require caution: For networks with switched capacitors or time-varying resistances, \( R_{th} \) may change over time, and the equation holds only approximately within each switching cycle.
  3. Controlled source handling is complex: When calculating \( R_{th} \) for networks with controlled sources, controlled sources cannot simply be zeroed; the external excitation method must be used, increasing computational complexity.
  4. Frequency-dependent characteristics are not captured: The terminal voltage equation is a DC or low-frequency model and does not include energy storage effects of capacitors and inductors. At high frequencies, \( R_{th} \) must be replaced by impedance \( Z_{th}(j\omega) \).
  5. Power limitations: The equation itself does not limit power, but actual networks have maximum output power limits; beyond these, voltage drop no longer follows a linear relationship.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Open-circuit voltage is the battery's electromotive force"—Inaccurate. Electromotive force is an intrinsic property of an ideal voltage source, while open-circuit voltage is the measured terminal voltage at \( I=0 \); they are numerically equal but physically distinct. When battery internal resistance is nonzero, the terminal voltage under load is necessarily lower than the open-circuit voltage.
  2. "Equivalent resistance equals the series-parallel combination of all resistances in the network"—Incorrect. Independent sources must be zeroed (voltage sources short-circuited, current sources open-circuited) before calculating the equivalent resistance, and controlled sources cannot be zeroed.
  3. "The terminal voltage equation applies only to circuit analysis"—Limited view. It also applies to force-velocity analogies in mechanical systems (force corresponds to voltage, velocity to current) and has broad applications in electromechanical coupling analysis.
  4. "Maximum power transfer corresponds to highest efficiency"—Incorrect. When \( R_L = R_{th} \), the load receives maximum power, but efficiency is only 50%; practical power systems pursue high efficiency, so load resistance is typically much greater than \( R_{th} \).
