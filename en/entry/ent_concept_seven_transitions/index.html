Overview

The Seven Transitions from 0 to 1 is a framework outlining the seven critical thresholds that humanoid robots must cross to move from laboratory prototypes to mass-producible products, spanning seven dimensions: technology, system, supply chain, manufacturing, cost, validation, and market. What it truly changes is not a single technical metric, but the entire industry's evaluation system and engineering methodology—from "demonstration success" to "product reliability."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Seven Transitions from 0 to 1 belongs to the concept type, within the domains of applications and markets, policy, regulation, and ethics, positioned at the validation and market layer of the value chain. Its English name is Seven Transitions from 0 to 1, and its Korean name is 0에서 1로의 7가지 전환.

The core proposition of this framework is that a systemic gap exists between humanoid robots "capable of demonstrations" and those "deliverable as products." Crossing this gap requires simultaneous qualitative changes across seven dimensions, not isolated breakthroughs. These seven transitions are:

  1. Technology Transition: From single-task success rates to long-term stability (MTBF, task success rate ≥99%)
  2. System Transition: From prototype integration to hierarchical design of the entire system architecture
  3. Supply Chain Transition: From custom parts to reuse of mature automotive-grade and consumer-electronics-grade supply chains
  4. Manufacturing Transition: From manual assembly to vertically integrated mass-production lines
  5. Cost Transition: From million-level BOM to a range acceptable for industrial customers' TCO
  6. Validation Transition: From official demonstrations to continuous deployment records in third-party customer scenarios
  7. Market Transition: From CapEx buyout to RaaS (Robot-as-a-Service) subscription models

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

This framework emerged from the long-standing "disconnect between demonstration and product" dilemma in the humanoid robotics industry. The knowledge graph concept entity ent_concept_demo_to_product_gap explicitly identifies a systemic gap between single-task success rates in demonstration videos and ideal-environment motion performance, versus the Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) required in real scenarios, stability with task success rates ≥99%, and the ability to operate continuously for several hours daily.

Historically, ASIMO and early Atlas represented the pinnacle of "technical demonstrations" but never truly entered customer scenarios. The new wave of 2025–2026 emphasizes long-term deployment in real scenarios and mass-production feasibility—this shift is the historical backdrop that validates the Seven Transitions framework. What it truly changes is not what robots can do, but the industry's standard for evaluating a robot: from "watching videos" to "reviewing deployment records."

Principle Breakdown

① Technology Transition: From Demonstration Metrics to Product Metrics

The watershed between demonstration and product lies in reliability metrics. Product-level requirements demand task success rates ≥99% and continuous operation for several hours daily, while demonstrations only require a single success. The mathematical essence of this transition is:

\[ P_{\text{product}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad P_{\text{product}} \geq 0.99 \;(\text{example threshold, engineering judgment}) \]

where \(p_i\) is the single-task success rate of the \(i\)-th subtask, and \(n\) is the task chain length. When \(n=10\), even with each subtask success rate as high as 99.5%, the overall chain success rate is only 95.1% (calculated from corpus data)—this is why "narrowing the task domain" becomes the necessary path to cross the gap.

② Cost Transition: From BOM to TCO

The core of the cost transition is bringing the entire robot's BOM into a range acceptable for industrial customers' TCO. The RaaS model converts the acquisition cost \(C_{robot}\) in the TCO formula into predictable annualized operating expenses while transferring availability risk to the manufacturer. This transition is expressed as:

\[ \text{TCO}_{\text{RaaS}} = C_{\text{opex}} + C_{\text{maintenance}} + C_{\text{fleet\_mgmt}} < C_{\text{labor}}^{\text{eff}} \times T \]

where \(C_{\text{labor}}^{\text{eff}}\) is the effective labor cost (including thresholds raised by recruitment difficulties and aging populations), and \(T\) is the operation duration. Only when this inequality holds is the economic closure line crossed.

③ Validation Transition: Third-Party Deployment as the Watershed

Among the seven transitions, the most discriminative is the validation transition—"whether there are continuous deployment records in third-party customer scenarios" serves as the key criterion distinguishing demonstration from product. This criterion shifts the evaluation subject from the manufacturer itself to an independent third party, fundamentally changing the information asymmetry landscape.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The key evaluation metrics corresponding to the seven transitions are shown in the table below:

Transition Dimension Key Metric Engineering Significance
Technology Task success rate ≥99%, MTBF Distinguishes "demonstration" from "product"
System Degrees of freedom structure, perception-computation configuration Determines task coverage scope and cost
Supply Chain Cost reduction curve for core components Determines whether BOM enters the TCO-acceptable range
Manufacturing Degree of vertical integration, production line transformation Determines mass-production consistency and capacity ceiling
Cost Entire robot BOM, annualized TCO operating expenses Determines whether economic closure is achieved
Validation Runtime duration and scale in third-party customer scenarios The ultimate criterion distinguishing "demonstration" from "product"
Market RaaS subscription penetration rate, CapEx→OpEx conversion Determines alignment with customer cash-flow sensitivity

Horizontal Comparison

The difference between the Seven Transitions framework and the adjacent concept ent_concept_demo_to_product_gap is that the latter is a diagnosis—identifying the existence and causes of the gap; the former is a prescription—providing seven action dimensions to cross the gap. Together, they form a complementary "problem-solution" relationship.

Regarding its relationship with ent_concept_robot_as_a_service: RaaS is one specific implementation path for the market transition. It converts customers' CapEx into OpEx and bundles maintenance, software updates, and fleet management, lowering the initial procurement barrier.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Case 1: Figure 02's Validation Transition. Figure 02 completed an 11-month deployment at BMW's Spartanburg plant, moving over 90,000 parts and participating in the production of over 30,000 BMW X3 vehicles—this is a typical example of the validation transition, possessing continuous deployment records in third-party customer scenarios.

Case 2: Agility Digit's Market Transition. Digit's warehouse deployments adopt the RaaS model, with real warehouse operation records from third-party customers such as Amazon and GXO, making it one of the few samples crossing the "demonstration-product gap."

Case 3: UBTECH Walker S2's Manufacturing Transition. The Walker S2 has 52 degrees of freedom, a dual-arm maximum payload of 15 kg, a fourth-generation five-finger dexterous hand, and ±162° waist rotation. Its autonomous battery hot-swap system can complete battery replacement within 3 minutes, enabling nearly 24-hour continuous operation—this is an engineering practice of the system and manufacturing transitions.

Case 4: Tesla Optimus's Cost Transition. Tesla self-develops linear/rotary actuators, battery packs, and power management, while reusing automotive-grade supply chains—this underpins its long-term price target of 20,000–30,000 USD.

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary conditions of the Seven Transitions framework must be clarified:

  1. Scenario focus precedes generality: Digit (logistics boxes), Galbot G1 (pharmacies and convenience stores), and GR-3 (elderly care companionship) all narrow their task domains to achieve reliability metrics. The framework's validity is built on the premise of "narrowed scenarios," and its applicability to general-purpose humanoid robots requires independent confirmation.

  2. Deployment maturity is negatively correlated with openness: The most mature deployments—Digit and Walker S2—offer closed turnkey solutions; the most open ecosystems—G1/H1—primarily serve research. This negative correlation means the framework does not address the division of labor between "general platforms + third-party applications."

  3. Data flywheel's scenario dependence: The data flywheel loop—deployment generates data, data improves models, models enhance performance and unlock more deployments—implies that scenario selection has strategic compounding effects, but the framework does not provide a quantitative method for scenario selection.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The seven transitions are executed sequentially"—Wrong. The seven transitions converge in parallel, not linearly. The corpus notes that "why now" can be decomposed into four simultaneously converging forces: AI capability convergence, cost curve convergence, labor structure convergence, and capital convergence. Breakthroughs in any single dimension are insufficient to cross the gap.

  2. "Validation transition equals securing orders"—Wrong. The criterion for the validation transition is "continuous deployment records in third-party customer scenarios," not order value. As of mid-2026, Optimus has no continuous deployment records in third-party customer scenarios; most performance figures come from official demonstrations and third-party analyses—this is precisely a sign that the validation transition remains incomplete.

  3. "RaaS is just leasing"—Wrong. RaaS replaces buyout with leasing or subscription and bundles maintenance, software updates, and fleet management. Its essence is transferring availability risk to the manufacturer, matching customers who are cash-flow sensitive and lack robot operations and maintenance capabilities.
