Summary

A visuomotor policy that represents action distributions as denoising diffusion models, enabling multimodal and smooth robot action generation.

Sources

  • Wiki extraction
  • Diffusion Policy

Overview

Diffusion Policy is an imitation learning method proposed by Chi et al. in 2023: it models the conditional distribution of action chunks \(p(a_{t:t+k}\mid o_t)\) as a denoising diffusion process, iteratively generating a smooth sequence of actions starting from noise. Its core advantage is the natural representation of multimodal action distributions (facing demonstrations where "going left or right are both acceptable" does not result in learning a compromise), combined with receding-horizon execution to balance smoothness and closed-loop responsiveness. It has become the mainstream policy form for complex manipulation tasks (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 18 §18.4; arXiv:2303.04137).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Diffusion Policy = conditional denoising diffusion model + action chunk generation + receding-horizon execution. Given encoded visual observations, the policy starts from a pure-noise action sequence and, after several denoising steps, obtains the next k steps of actions, executing only the first h steps (h < k) before re-observing and replanning (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 18 §18.4.2).

It shares the "action chunk" interface with ACT, but the generation mechanism differs: ACT uses a CVAE for a single forward pass, while Diffusion Policy uses multi-step iterative denoising sampling—the former is fast but weakly multimodal, the latter is slower but preserves multimodality.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The regression loss in behavior cloning has a mathematically unavoidable flaw: it takes the mean of multimodal distributions. When "same observation, multiple reasonable actions" is common in demonstration data (bypassing an obstacle from the left or right, pressing a key with any finger), the regression policy outputs a compromise point between the two modes—colliding with both (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 18 §18.2.3). MDN and discretization were early patches, but the number of components/resolution requires manual trade-offs.

The key property of diffusion models precisely fills this gap: they can express arbitrarily complex multimodal distributions (Wiki §18.4.1). Diffusion Policy introduces this into the action space, representing the convergence of the "generative AI wave" and "robot imitation learning." What it truly changes is not the success-rate numbers but the family of action distributions the policy can express—jumping from unimodal Gaussians to arbitrary multimodality.

Principle Breakdown

① DDPM Basics

The forward process gradually adds noise to corrupt data into Gaussian noise, and the network is trained to predict the added noise (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 18 §18.4.1):

\[ q(x^k \mid x^{k-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1-\beta^k}\, x^{k-1},\, \beta^k I), \qquad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\left[ \|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x^k, k)\|^2 \right] \]

Generation starts from pure noise and iteratively denoises. Diffusion Policy is thus a conditional diffusion model "conditioned on observation o and instantiated in the action space."

② Structural Differences from ACT (Wiki §18.4.2)

  • Multimodality: Samples a complete action from one of the modes, rather than a compromise;
  • Receding horizon: Generates the full chunk each time but executes only the first few steps—balancing open-loop smoothness and closed-loop responsiveness;
  • Training stability: Noise prediction regression target, no KL balancing as in CVAE or adversarial training as in GAN.

③ Two Additional Issues for Humanoid Adaptation

High-dimensional whole-body action space + locomotion-manipulation coupling (Wiki §18.4.3). Engineering conclusion: "Diffusion Policy is responsible for what actions to generate, and the whole-body controller is responsible for how to execute them stably," decoupled via an action interface (end-effector poses or whole-body joint targets). A frequently overlooked interface issue is action chunk feasibility: demonstration data naturally lies within the feasible region, and generated actions are statistically close to feasible but not individually feasible; under observation distribution shift, joint limits or self-collision constraints may be violated. Protective measures include feasibility projection layers or negative-sample-guided sampling (Wiki §18.4.3).

Who Uses It / Application Cases

  • Original benchmark: Consistent improvements over prior methods on pushing, flipping, precision assembly, etc. (Wiki §18.4.2, arXiv:2303.04137);
  • 3D variant: DP3 (3D Diffusion Policy) uses point clouds as observations; Generalizable Humanoid Manipulation with Improved 3D Diffusion Policies (2024) drives humanoid upper-body manipulation (Wiki §18.4.3);
  • Humanoid whole-body: H-RDT (2025) extends the diffusion action head to whole-body degrees of freedom; Coordinated Humanoid Manipulation with Choice Policies (2025) uses diffusion/flow matching for bimanual coordination mode selection (Wiki §18.4.3);
  • VLA action head: RDT-1B, DexVLA adopt diffusion action experts (Wiki §19.2.3).

Limitations and Boundaries

  • Inference latency: Multi-step denoising conflicts with high-frequency control; requires DDIM/distillation/consistency model acceleration (Wiki §18.4.2/18.4.5);
  • Cross-modal mixed trajectories: Sampling occasionally produces actions between two modes; low-level safety checks are needed as a fallback (Wiki §18.4.5);
  • No hard feasibility guarantee: Statistically feasible ≠ individually feasible (see Principle Breakdown ③);
  • Data requirements increase with task complexity, and it does not solve the demonstration coverage problem itself (Wiki §18.4.4).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Diffusion Policy = image generation applied to robots"—it diffuses action chunks, not pixels; the conditioning mechanism and receding-horizon execution are policy-specific designs.
  2. "The number of steps must follow the paper's 100 steps"—training uses 50–100 steps, but deployment can be reduced to single digits via DDIM/distillation; copying the training configuration for deployment is a common beginner mistake.
  3. "Multimodal generation means no collisions"—sampling can still draw mixed trajectories; low-level safety clipping is indispensable.
  4. "Diffusion is always better than ACT"—for unimodal, real-time-critical, and data-limited scenarios, ACT's single forward pass remains a more engineering-friendly choice.
