Overview

Structural topology and force transmission path design is a core process node of mechanical overall design within the system architecture and electromechanical overall design phase. It is responsible for determining the robot structure's topological form, load transmission paths, and modular interface partitioning before the concept scheme is frozen. What it truly changes is not the shape of parts, but the global engineering discipline of "where forces enter, where they travel, and where they are absorbed"—all stiffness, strength, and weight targets must be demonstrated at the topological level first, rather than left to detailed design to remedy through local thickening. This process uses topology optimization, force path sketches, and modular partitioning as its primary tools, and outputs structural topology schemes, force transmission path diagrams, and module interface definitions, providing traceable inputs for downstream detailed design and manufacturing.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Structural topology and force transmission path design is a third-level subtask under mechanical overall design (P3.1), numbered P3.1.4, with the English equivalent Structural topology and force transmission path design. It addresses the fundamental question of "what the robot skeleton should look like and how loads should be transmitted from the end effector to the base." Specifically, this process determines the material distribution topology of structural components, the routing of primary load-bearing beams/shells, and the mechanical interface forms between modules, given the constraints of the established joint layout, actuator selection, and overall machine weight budget.

Within the WBS V3 framework, this process follows a unified five-step approach: input review and target quantification (P3.1.4.1), conceptual and detailed design (P3.1.4.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P3.1.4.3), verification and issue closure (P3.1.4.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P3.1.4.5). Each subtask has clearly defined four-level key actions, ensuring traceability and verifiability throughout the entire process from requirements to delivery.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early robot development, structural design often started from "drawing a human-like appearance," defining the exterior first and adding strength later. The consequences of this approach were: discontinuous load paths with stress concentrations at bolted connections; uncontrolled weight gain from local thickening to compensate for insufficient stiffness; and vague module interface definitions leading to assembly interference or maintenance difficulties. These issues were tolerable during single-unit debugging, but once entering mass production or long-duration operation, they evolved into systemic defects such as fatigue fractures, excessive resonance, and skyrocketing maintenance costs.

Structural topology and force transmission path design exists precisely to establish the "skeleton of forces" before detailed design. It requires engineers to first answer three questions: where forces come from (end-effector loads, inertial forces, gravity), which path forces take (through which structural members to the base), and where forces are balanced (joint torques, support reactions). Only after these three questions are quantitatively answered can wall thicknesses, fillets, and bolt specifications in the detailed design phase be justified. What it truly changes is not the number of drawings, but the engineering discipline of "from requirements to verifiable metrics"—all design decisions must undergo quantitative demonstration and scheme freezing before entering detailed design.

Principle Breakdown

① Topology Optimization: Letting Material "Grow" Optimal Paths

Topology optimization is the core mathematical tool for structural topology design. Its basic idea is to solve for the optimal material layout within a given design domain, using material density distribution as the design variable, minimizing structural compliance (i.e., maximizing stiffness) as the objective, and volume fraction as the constraint. Its mathematical model can be expressed as:

\[ \min_{\rho} \; C(\rho) = \mathbf{F}^T \mathbf{U}(\rho) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \frac{V(\rho)}{V_0} \leq f, \quad 0 \leq \rho_e \leq 1 \]

where ρ is the element density, C is the structural compliance, F is the external load vector, U is the displacement vector, V/V₀ is the volume ratio, and f is the volume fraction upper limit. The solution typically appears as "truss-like" or "branch-like" material distributions, from which engineers extract the primary load transmission paths and then perform geometric reconstruction considering manufacturing processes (casting, machining, 3D printing). It should be emphasized that topology optimization provides the "shortest path for forces," not the final part shape—engineers must judge which branches can be merged and which holes should be retained to avoid wire harnesses and pipelines.

② Force Path Sketches: Translating Topology into Engineering Structures

Topology optimization results are often highly organic in form, and direct manufacturing is neither economical nor aesthetically pleasing. The role of force path sketches is to "translate" the optimization results into engineering structures that are manufacturable and assemblable. The specific approach is: identify primary load paths (e.g., hip joint to pelvis, shoulder joint to thorax, end effector to forearm), arrange primary load-bearing members (beams, plates/shells, casting ribs) along the paths, add stiffening ribs or transition fillets at path intersections, and reduce weight or open holes in non-load-bearing areas. This step relies on the engineer's understanding of load cases and is an experiential step that topology optimization cannot replace.

③ Modular Partitioning: Making Assembly and Maintenance Design Constraints

The core idea of modular partitioning is "central skeleton + detachable limb modules." The central skeleton bears primary loads and integrates wire harnesses and electronic components, while limb modules connect to the skeleton through standard interfaces (flanges, locating pins, quick-release mechanisms). This partitioning approach brings three direct benefits: during assembly, the skeleton can be installed first and limbs attached afterward, reducing lifting difficulty; during maintenance, only the faulty module needs to be removed without disassembling the entire machine; during upgrades, a single module can be replaced without affecting other subsystems. Module interface definition (location, dimensions, tolerances, fastening methods) is the core output of modular design and must be frozen during the conceptual design phase; otherwise, subsequent detailed design will fall into a quagmire of repeated interface modifications.

Horizontal Comparison

Within the spectrum of robot structural design methodologies, three typical approaches exist. The first is the "experience-driven" approach, relying entirely on structural forms from the engineer's past projects. Its advantage is rapid development; its disadvantage is difficulty in exceeding the performance limits of existing solutions. The second is the "simulation-driven" approach, centered on topology optimization and finite element analysis, optimizing first and verifying later. Its advantage is the ability to find non-intuitive optimal topologies; its disadvantage is high demands on the engineer's simulation capabilities and longer iteration cycles. The third is the "modular platform" approach, centered on standardized interfaces and reusable modules, prioritizing assembly and maintenance convenience first, then improving performance through local optimization.

This process (P3.1.4) belongs to a fusion of the second and third approaches: performance-driven through topology optimization and force path sketches, and maintainability-driven through modular partitioning. Compared to the purely experience-driven approach, it is more systematic and quantifiable; compared to the purely simulation-driven approach, it is closer to manufacturing and assembly realities. This fused positioning is highly consistent with the bridging role of the P3 phase in "connecting conceptual design with detailed engineering implementation."

Who Uses It · Application Cases

This process is applied in the development of industrial robots, collaborative robots, and humanoid robots. In the humanoid robot field, typical application scenarios include: structural topology design of the hip joint and pelvic region—this area bears impact loads during walking and static loads during standing, and topology optimization helps achieve significant weight reduction while maintaining stiffness; connection structures between the shoulder joint and thorax—these require both a large range of motion and sufficient structural stiffness, where force path sketches play a critical role; and interface design between limb modules and the central skeleton—modular partitioning here manifests as standardized flanges and quick-release mechanisms, directly determining assembly and maintenance efficiency.

At the specific execution level, P3.1.4.1 requires listing all upstream input items and confirming versions, converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establishing task owners, timelines, and risk registers. P3.1.4.2 requires forming no fewer than 2 candidate schemes, establishing an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organizing reviews, and freezing the scheme. P3.1.4.3 requires establishing models/prototypes and recording key parameters, executing simulation or prototype verification, and recording anomalies and deviations. P3.1.4.4 requires developing test/review plans and pass criteria, executing tests and recording raw data, and outputting issue lists and improvement measures. P3.1.4.5 requires writing documentation per templates with references to raw data, completing internal reviews and version control, and publishing and notifying downstream dependents.

Limitations and Boundaries

This process has clear boundaries and limitations. First, topology optimization results depend on the accuracy of load cases—if the input load spectrum (magnitude, direction, application point) deviates significantly from actual operating conditions, the optimization results lose their reference value. Second, topology optimization does not consider manufacturing constraints (such as minimum wall thickness, draft angles, tool accessibility), and optimization results must undergo geometric reconstruction before manufacturing, a process that may sacrifice some performance. Third, modular partitioning, while improving maintainability, may introduce additional interface weight and connection compliance—the more interfaces, the greater the risk of loosening and fatigue. Fourth, the output of this process is a "scheme" rather than "final parts"; the detailed design phase still requires strength verification and fatigue analysis, and the topology scheme cannot directly replace detailed calculations.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Topology optimization results can be directly manufactured"—Wrong. Topology optimization outputs material density distributions, typically appearing as organic forms that must undergo geometric reconstruction (smoothing, shelling, adding fillets) to meet manufacturing process requirements, and this reconstruction requires engineering judgment.

  2. "The shorter the force path, the better"—Not entirely true. Shorter force paths do favor stiffness and efficiency, but paths must avoid functional areas such as wire harness channels, battery compartments, and sensor mounting spaces. The actual path is often the "shortest feasible path" rather than the geometrically shortest.

  3. "The more modular interfaces, the more flexible"—Wrong. An increased number of interfaces brings added weight, accumulated assembly tolerances, and higher loosening risks. Modular partitioning should balance "maintainability benefits" against "interface costs," rather than blindly pursuing module count.

  4. "The higher the stiffness, the better"—Wrong. Excessive stiffness leads to uncontrolled weight and higher costs, and in certain scenarios (such as at joint torque sensor mounting locations), some flexibility is needed to avoid over-constraint. Stiffness design should target meeting dynamic performance metrics, not pursuing absolute maximums.
