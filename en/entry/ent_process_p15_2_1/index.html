Overview

Safety function testing is a critical process node in the whole-machine integration and verification testing phase (P15) that verifies the robot's safety mechanisms operate as designed, corresponding to WBS number P15.2.1. It validates that the robot can safely shut down or enter a protective posture under any abnormal condition through means such as emergency stop, fall protection, collision detection, limit switches, and safety zones. Its completion criterion is the issuance of a safety test report and confirmation that all safety functions pass. What it truly changes is not the troubleshooting action of "detecting faults," but the qualification process of "proving that the safety design holds through quantifiable acceptance criteria."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Safety function testing is the first third-level subtask under the P15.2 Functional Safety and Performance Verification work package within the P15 whole-machine integration and verification testing phase, numbered P15.2.1 in WBS V3. It belongs to one of the core test categories in the DV (Design Verification) matrix, alongside performance baseline testing (P15.2.2) and environmental adaptability testing (P15.2.3), collectively forming a complete closed loop for whole-machine functional and performance verification.

The core objects of this test are the robot's safety-related functions, including the emergency stop chain, safety MCU monitoring, power-loss braking hold, and collision detection. Its methods/tools are explicitly defined as: emergency stop, fall protection, collision detection, limit switches, and safety zones. The design rationale is: any abnormality can result in a safe shutdown or entry into a protective posture. The key constraint is safety standards and personnel protection.

Among the 17 phase entities in the knowledge graph, P15 is positioned after P14 software integration and before P16 small-batch trial production and mass production preparation. As a component of P15, safety function testing receives upstream inputs from the deliverables of subsystem integration (P15.1), and its downstream outputs serve performance baseline testing (P15.2.2), durability testing (P15.3.1), and certification preparation and pre-audit (P15.3.3).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before safety function testing was systematized, safety verification for humanoid robots often relied on "trial and error": prototypes exposed problems during debugging, and engineers would patch them in a targeted manner. This model had two fundamental flaws.

First, the discovery of safety defects was lagging. When a robot operates in whole-machine form, an emergency stop failure might only be discovered after a collision accident or personnel injury. At that point, the repair cost is extremely high—not only involving hardware rework, but also potentially facing regulatory compliance risks.

Second, safety verification lacked traceability. Without clear acceptance criteria, test results could not be quantified, making it impossible to determine whether "safety functions truly passed." This directly left downstream certification preparation (P15.3.3) without a foundation.

The positioning of safety function testing is precisely to address these two pain points. It shifts safety verification from "post-hoc remediation" to "pre-hoc proof," and by converting completion criteria into quantifiable KPIs, it enables every safety function to be traced back to specific test cases and acceptance data. In the DV matrix, the orchestration logic of safety function testing uses failure modes identified by FMEA as an index, allowing every test to be traced back to a specific risk—this is the essential characteristic that distinguishes it from general functional testing.

Principle Breakdown

① Hierarchical Architecture of the Safety Chain The underlying logic of safety function testing is "defense in depth." The safety mechanisms of a humanoid robot are typically divided into three levels:

  • Level 1: Active Avoidance—Identifying hazards through perception systems (e.g., collision detection, safety zone monitoring) and proactively adjusting behavior before an accident occurs;
  • Level 2: Passive Protection—When active avoidance fails, limiting harm through physical means such as mechanical limit switches and power-loss braking hold;
  • Level 3: Emergency Response—When the above levels fail to prevent an accident, cutting off power through the emergency stop chain to bring the robot into a safe posture.

Safety function testing must verify that each level operates as designed and that the switching logic between levels is correct.

② Integrity Verification of the Emergency Stop Chain Emergency stop is the most critical element of safety functions. Its verification points include:

  • Chain Integrity: The entire electrical circuit from the emergency stop button to the motor driver must have no open circuits;
  • Response Time: The time from pressing the emergency stop to the robot coming to a complete stop must meet safety standards;
  • Redundant Design: The safety MCU monitoring and the main control MCU are independent of each other; even if the main controller fails, the safety MCU can still trigger braking.

③ Sensitivity Calibration of Collision Detection The principle of collision detection is to determine a collision based on abnormal changes in joint current or torque. The verification challenge lies in selecting the sensitivity threshold—too high a threshold leads to missed detections, while too low a threshold causes false triggers. During testing, collisions of varying force are injected to calibrate a threshold range that reliably detects collisions without frequent false alarms.

④ Logic Verification of Safety Zones Safety zones are delineated using LiDAR or vision systems. When personnel or objects enter a hazardous zone, the robot should decelerate or stop. Verification content includes the accuracy of zone delineation, the real-time performance of detection, and the correctness of response actions.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The acceptance criteria for safety function testing must be converted into quantifiable KPIs during the P15.2.1.1 input review and target quantification phase. Based on the corpus, typical safety function test items and their verification purposes are as follows:

Test Category Typical Item Verification Purpose WBS Anchor
Safety function testing Emergency stop chain Verify that power is completely cut off after the emergency stop button is pressed P15.2.1
Safety function testing Safety MCU monitoring Verify that the safety MCU independently triggers braking when the main controller fails P15.2.1
Safety function testing Power-loss braking hold Verify that joints maintain position without falling after power loss P15.2.1
Safety function testing Collision detection Verify that the robot stops or retreats promptly after a collision occurs P15.2.1

Specific numerical indicators (such as emergency stop response time, collision detection sensitivity thresholds, braking hold torque, etc.) must be determined based on the selected safety standards (e.g., ISO 13482, ISO 10218) and the product specification sheet. The corpus does not provide specific values, so these must be confirmed independently.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Safety function testing has mature applications in the industrial robotics field. Taking collaborative robots as an example, in the collaborative assembly scenario of the KUKA LBR iiwa 7 R800 robotic arm with the SCHUNK Co-act EGP-C 64 gripper, the robot must coexist, collaborate, and operate jointly with humans. Its safety functions (collision detection, torque limiting) must undergo rigorous verification before deployment on the production line.

In the humanoid robotics field, safety function testing runs through both the DV and PV phases. The DV phase uses engineering prototypes to verify design specifications, corresponding to the WBS P15.2 and P15.3 task series; the PV phase conducts safety sampling inspections on production parts before small-batch trial production (P16.3.1). Test results directly support certification preparation and pre-audit (P15.3.3), providing the basis for market access approval.

Limitations and Boundaries

Safety function testing has the following boundaries:

  1. Cannot cover all failure modes: Testing can only verify identified risks (indexed by FMEA); unidentified failure modes fall outside the test scope;
  2. Differences between test environment and real scenarios: Laboratory testing cannot fully simulate complex operating conditions in real use (e.g., extreme weather, unintended operations);
  3. Lag in safety standards: Humanoid robots are an emerging product category, and existing safety standards (such as ISO 10218 for industrial robots) may not fully cover their novel safety risks;
  4. Constraints of test cost and time: A complete safety test matrix requires substantial prototypes and testing hours, and under project schedule pressure, test items may be forced to be trimmed.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Safety function testing is just pressing an emergency stop button"—Wrong. Emergency stop is only one of the safety functions; a complete test also includes safety MCU monitoring, power-loss braking hold, collision detection, limit switches, safety zones, and more, each requiring quantifiable acceptance criteria.

  2. "Passing safety testing = the product is absolutely safe"—Wrong. Safety testing can only prove that identified risks are controlled; it cannot guarantee absolute safety. What it truly changes is not the promise of "eliminating all risks," but the engineering fact of "proving through traceable evidence that risks have been reasonably managed."

  3. "Safety testing can be conducted after performance testing"—Wrong. Safety function testing must be performed first because performance testing (such as walking speed and payload tests) itself carries safety risks; it can only be conducted safely after safety functions have been verified.

  4. "Safety testing only needs to be done once"—Wrong. The DV phase uses engineering prototypes to verify design specifications, the PV phase requires sampling inspections on production parts, and regression testing is needed after design changes.
