Overview

Dexterous hand selection/design and integration is the 11th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. The core task of this stage is to start from task requirements, complete hand DOF definition, actuation and transmission scheme selection, and output the "Hand Specification" as input for downstream development. What it truly changes is not "what the hand looks like," but "what the hand can do"—the leap from gripper to dexterous hand is essentially a transition in degrees of freedom and adaptability.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Dexterous hand selection/design and integration is the 11th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. This stage covers complete engineering actions such as input review, scheme design, implementation/prototyping, validation closed-loop, and documentation delivery, serving as a key node to ensure downstream dependents receive qualified inputs.

In WBS V3, stage P11 governs two level-2 work packages: "Hand Architecture and Actuation" (P11.1) and "Finger Impedance and Force Control" (P11.2). P11.1 is further expanded into two level-3 subtasks: "Hand DOF and Function Definition" (P11.1.1) and "Actuation and Transmission Scheme" (P11.1.2). Each level-3 subtask follows a unified five-step method: input review and target quantification, scheme/method design, implementation/prototype/sample fabrication, validation and issue closure, and documentation output and downstream delivery.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before dexterous hands emerged, robot end effectors were long dominated by two-finger/three-finger grippers. Grippers are simple in structure and low in cost, but can only perform limited grasp types. As humanoid robots move from laboratories to real-world scenarios, task complexity has risen sharply—unscrewing bottle caps, holding pens, pinching fragile objects—these operations pose fundamental challenges to the degrees of freedom and adaptability of end effectors.

The dexterous hand was created precisely to resolve this contradiction. It provides multi-finger, multi-DOF end effector capabilities for complex manipulation, but at the cost of control complexity and high expense. The significance of stage P11 is to find a balance between "capability ceiling" and "engineering feasibility": it must satisfy task requirements for hand functionality while keeping weight, volume, cable count, and cost within acceptable ranges.

From a historical positioning perspective, P11 comes after system architecture and electromechanical overall design (P3) and before full-machine integration and validation testing (P15). Stage P3 determines the hand DOF configuration range of 11–22 (estimated from corpus data), while P11 must translate this macro configuration into a concrete hand architecture and actuation scheme. What it truly changes is not "the number of hand DOFs," but "how each DOF is actuated and controlled."

Principle Breakdown

① Fully Actuated vs. Underactuated: The Trade-off Between DOF and Actuators The core design decision for a dexterous hand lies in the choice of actuation method. Fully actuated means each DOF is driven by an independent actuator, offering high dexterity but complex structure and high cost; underactuated means fewer actuators than DOFs, transmitting motion through mechanical coupling to achieve adaptive grasp—the hand automatically envelops the object's shape.

\[ \text{DOF}_{\text{hand}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{DOF}_{\text{finger}_i} + \text{DOF}_{\text{thumb}} \]

where n is the number of fingers. Fully actuated dexterous hands typically have 16–24 DOFs, underactuated dexterous hands have 8–16, and two-finger grippers have only 1–2 (estimated from corpus data). Designers must decide between full-DOF or underactuated schemes based on target objects.

② Actuation and Transmission: Trade-offs Among Tendon, Direct Drive, and Linkage The choice of actuation and transmission scheme directly determines the hand's volume, weight, and reliability. Tendon transmission is compact and allows remote motor placement, but is complex and difficult to maintain; miniature motor direct drive is easy to maintain and control directly, but is bulky and heavy; linkage transmission offers good rigidity and low backlash, but limits design freedom; hydraulic/pneumatic schemes provide high output force but require additional power sources.

Key constraints include reliability, maintainability, backlash, and force control precision. For scenarios requiring fine force control (e.g., grasping fragile objects), the backlash and friction characteristics of the transmission scheme are critical.

③ Five-Step Method: An Engineering Closed Loop from Requirements to Delivery Each level-3 subtask follows a unified five-step process: 1. Input Review and Target Quantification: List all upstream input items and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task owner, timeline, and risk register. 2. Scheme/Method Design: Generate no fewer than 2 candidate schemes, build an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize review, and freeze the scheme. 3. Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulation or prototype validation, and document anomalies and deviations. 4. Validation and Issue Closure: Develop test/review plans and pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output issue lists and improvement measures. 5. Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Write documents per templates with references to raw data, complete internal review and version control, and publish and notify downstream dependents.

Actuation Motor/tendon/direct drive Tendon/linkage/differential Motor + leadscrew

(All data above are from the corpus.) The table shows a clear "capability-cost" gradient among the three. The task of stage P11 is not to choose the "best" scheme, but to choose the scheme that "best matches task requirements."

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The engineering methods of stage P11 have been applied in several real-world projects:

  • Shadow Hand: A representative fully actuated dexterous hand, using tendon transmission with up to 24 DOFs, used for research-grade dexterous manipulation.
  • Robotiq 3F: A typical underactuated dexterous hand, using linkage and differential mechanisms to achieve adaptive grasping, widely used in industrial collaborative scenarios.
  • HIT Hand: A dexterous hand developed by Harbin Institute of Technology, exploring multiple actuation schemes.
  • SVH (Schunk SVH): An underactuated dexterous hand emphasizing lightweight design and adaptability.

At the application level, P11's output directly serves downstream full-machine integration and validation testing (P15). The accuracy of the hand specification directly affects the efficiency of full-machine integration and the performance of the final product.

Limitations and Boundaries

Stage P11 has clear boundaries and limitations:

  1. The Contradiction Between DOF and Control: Fully actuated dexterous hands have up to 16–24 DOFs, but control complexity rises sharply. High DOF does not necessarily lead to higher task success rates and may instead introduce real-time control and stability issues.
  2. Precision Bottleneck of Underactuation: Underactuated dexterous hands achieve adaptability through mechanical coupling, but have low control precision and struggle with tasks requiring precise force control.
  3. Trade-off Between Cost and Reliability: Tendon transmission is compact but complex and difficult to maintain; direct drive is easy to maintain but bulky. There is a fundamental tension among reliability, maintainability, and performance.
  4. Static Nature of the Specification: The "Hand Specification" is frozen at stage P11, but downstream integration may reveal new requirements or constraints requiring retroactive changes—this demands that P11's output possess traceability and change management capability.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "More DOFs is always better"—Wrong. Increasing DOFs brings nonlinear growth in control complexity and cost. The key to design is matching task requirements, not pursuing parameter accumulation.
  2. "A dexterous hand is just an upgraded gripper"—Wrong. The two differ fundamentally in DOF, actuation methods, and control strategies. A gripper is a "tool"; a dexterous hand is an "organ."
  3. "Underactuation is a low-end solution"—Wrong. Underactuation achieves adaptive grasping through mechanical coupling and can be more robust than full actuation in specific scenarios (e.g., unknown object shapes).
  4. "Once the specification is released, it cannot be changed"—Wrong. P11's output must support downstream change requests; a requirements traceability chain and change management mechanism should be established.
