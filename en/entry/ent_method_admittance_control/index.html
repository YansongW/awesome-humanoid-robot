Overview

Admittance Control is the "dual" method of impedance control: it computes the desired motion trajectory based on measured contact forces, which is then tracked by a position/velocity controller. Its fundamental equation is \(\mathbf{M}_d (\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \ddot{\mathbf{x}}_c) + \mathbf{D}_d (\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{x}}_c) + \mathbf{K}_d (\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}_c) = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\). What it truly changes is not the way force is controlled, but rather the transfer of the "force-motion" coupling relationship from the torque level to the trajectory level—allowing existing high-precision position control hardware to directly participate in compliant interaction.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Admittance control is a control strategy that generates desired motion based on external forces. It measures the external contact force \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\) via a force sensor, feeds it into an admittance controller, outputs a corrected command trajectory \(\mathbf{x}_c\), and then an inner-loop position controller tracks this trajectory. Its core equation (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11):

\[ \mathbf{M}_d (\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \ddot{\mathbf{x}}_c) + \mathbf{D}_d (\dot{\mathbf{x}}_d - \dot{\mathbf{x}}_c) + \mathbf{K}_d (\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}_c) = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} \]

where \(\mathbf{x}_d\) is the desired trajectory, \(\mathbf{x}_c\) is the admittance-corrected command trajectory, \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\) is the external contact force, and \(\mathbf{M}_d\), \(\mathbf{D}_d\), \(\mathbf{K}_d\) are the desired inertia, damping, and stiffness matrices, respectively. The input is force, and the output is motion correction—this is exactly the opposite of impedance control (which takes motion deviation as input and outputs force), and the two form a force-motion dual relationship.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Pure position control has fundamental flaws in contact scenarios: geometric errors (inaccurate models, unknown object poses) translate into uncontrolled contact forces—at best causing task failure, and at worst damaging the robot or injuring humans. Although impedance control solves this problem, it requires the robot to possess torque-level control capability, meaning it can directly send torque commands to the current loop. However, the vast majority of industrial robots and traditional position-controlled platforms do not have torque interfaces, and their high-precision position loops actually become an obstacle to achieving compliant control.

The historical positioning of admittance control lies precisely here: it does not alter the underlying position control architecture, but rather superimposes an "admittance layer" outside the position loop, translating force signals into position corrections. This enables:

  • Direct use of existing high-precision position control hardware;
  • A certain filtering effect on external force sensor noise;
  • Suitability for interaction between high-stiffness industrial robots and the environment.

What it truly changes is not the control architecture, but the deployment method of compliance capability—allowing robots without torque interfaces to interact with the environment safely.

Principle Breakdown

① Admittance Equation: Mapping from Force to Trajectory

The core of admittance control is a second-order differential equation that specifies the dynamic relationship between external forces and trajectory corrections. When an external force \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\) acts on the end-effector, the admittance controller computes a corrected trajectory \(\mathbf{x}_c\), causing the virtual mass-damper-spring system to respond according to the desired dynamic characteristics. At steady state, the spring term dominates:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} \approx \mathbf{K}_d (\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}_c) \]

This means that the larger the external force, the farther the trajectory deviates from the desired position—the robot appears more "compliant."

② Inner/Outer Loop Architecture: Collaboration of Two Control Layers

Admittance control employs a typical inner/outer loop architecture:

  • Outer loop (admittance loop): Reads force sensor signals and generates a corrected trajectory \(\mathbf{x}_c\) via the admittance equation;
  • Inner loop (position loop): Tracks \(\mathbf{x}_c\) and drives the actuators.

The advantage of this hierarchical structure is that the high bandwidth of the inner position loop ensures tracking accuracy, while the lower bandwidth of the outer admittance loop naturally filters out high-frequency noise from the force sensor. What it truly changes is not control precision, but the hierarchical level of force-position coupling—moving it from the torque level up to the trajectory level.

③ Essential Differences from Impedance Control

Dimension Impedance Control Admittance Control

Key Parameters and Specifications

Key parameters of admittance control include (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11):

  • Desired inertia \(\mathbf{M}_d\) (kg): Determines acceleration response, affecting dynamic behavior at the moment of contact;
  • Desired damping \(\mathbf{D}_d\) (N·s/m): Determines the rate of energy dissipation, affecting stability and oscillation decay;
  • Desired stiffness \(\mathbf{K}_d\) (N/m): Determines the relationship between steady-state position deviation and restoring force;
  • Force sensor range and noise: Directly affects the signal-to-noise ratio of the admittance output;
  • Position inner-loop bandwidth: Determines the upper limit of achievable force response speed for admittance control.

Engineering judgment: The bandwidth of the position inner loop in admittance control typically needs to be 5–10 times higher than that of the admittance outer loop; otherwise, coupling between the two loops may lead to instability.

Horizontal Comparison

Comparison with impedance control: Impedance control directly generates torque commands, offering fast response but requiring a torque interface; admittance control generates position commands, offering better compatibility but limited by position-loop bandwidth. What it truly changes is not the compliance effect, but the assumption about hardware prerequisites—impedance assumes "a torque interface exists," while admittance assumes "a force sensor plus position loop exists."

Comparison with hybrid force/position control: Hybrid control uses a selection matrix to determine which directions operate under position control and which under force control—this is "division of labor by direction"; admittance control establishes a force-motion dynamic relationship in all directions—this is "coupling in dynamics."

Comparison with variable stiffness control: Variable stiffness control adjusts joint stiffness according to task phases—this is "parameter scheduling"; admittance control can achieve similar effects by adjusting \(\mathbf{K}_d\), but its essence is "dynamic relationship design."

Who Uses It: Application Cases

TALOS humanoid robot (PAL Robotics): Approximately 175 cm tall, approximately 95 kg in mass, 32 DOF, equipped with force/torque sensors at all joints, supporting impedance/admittance control, and using the ROS software stack (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11). TALOS is a full-size torque-controllable humanoid robot designed for research, and its admittance control capability enables safe human-robot interaction and compliant manipulation.

Collision safety response: After detecting a collision, the robot can switch to compliant yielding mode—that is, switching to zero-force/admittance control mode, allowing the robot to move freely in response to external forces (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11). This is a typical application of admittance control in humanoid robot safety strategies.

Landing impact absorption and uneven terrain compliance: In landing impact absorption, uneven terrain compliance, and fall protection, humanoid robots often combine admittance control with compliant actuator technologies such as SEA/VSA (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited by position control bandwidth: Admittance control struggles to achieve fast force responses. The bandwidth of the inner position loop determines the upper limit of force response, and for tasks requiring high-frequency force tracking (e.g., grinding, polishing), admittance control may be inadequate.

  2. Stability issues under high environmental stiffness: If the environmental stiffness is extremely high, the admittance controller may become unstable. This is because in high-stiffness environments, tiny position errors produce large contact force variations, easily exciting oscillations.

  3. Dependence on force sensor quality: Admittance control directly uses force sensor signals, and sensor noise directly affects the quality of the corrected trajectory. Although the admittance equation itself provides some filtering, additional filtering is still needed when noise is excessive.

  4. Not suitable for pure torque control scenarios: For tasks requiring precise torque output (such as force tracking in precision assembly), the trajectory-correction approach of admittance control may be insufficiently direct.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Admittance control is impedance control"—Wrong. The two are force-motion duals: impedance control takes motion deviation as input and outputs force; admittance control takes force as input and outputs motion corrections. Although their mathematical forms are similar, their physical meanings and control architectures are completely different.

  2. "Admittance control is more advanced than impedance control"—Wrong. Each has its own applicable scenarios: admittance control suits industrial robots with position loops but no torque interfaces; impedance control suits advanced platforms with torque interfaces. The choice depends on hardware conditions and task requirements.

  3. "Admittance control does not require a force sensor"—Wrong. The core of admittance control is "computing the desired motion trajectory based on measured contact forces," and the force sensor is a necessary hardware prerequisite.

  4. "Admittance control is only used for human-robot interaction"—Wrong. In humanoid robots, admittance control is used not only for human-robot interaction but also for landing impact absorption, uneven terrain compliance, and fall protection (source: Project Wiki Chapter 8 §8.4.11).
