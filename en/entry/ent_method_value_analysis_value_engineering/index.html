Overview

Value Analysis / Value Engineering (VA/VE) is a structured method for systematically reducing costs while preserving product functionality, or enhancing functionality within an acceptable cost range, thereby increasing product value. What it truly changes is not the material cost of a specific part, but the "function-cost" decision logic throughout the entire product lifecycle from design to mass production—ensuring that every cost reduction first answers the question, "Is this function truly necessary?"

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Value Analysis / Value Engineering (VA/VE) belongs to the method type, within the domain of mass production manufacturing, and sits at the midstream level of the value chain. Its core definition is: a structured method that reduces costs while preserving functionality, or enhances functionality at an acceptable cost, to increase product value. The English name is Value Analysis / Value Engineering (VA/VE), and the Korean name is 가치 분석/가치 공학(VA/VE).

In the taxonomy of entity types, VA/VE is classified as an Information Entity, alongside paper, algorithm, and standard, as objects existing in symbolic or data form. Its theoretical depth is method, i.e., the "method or technique" level, situated above formalism (formalized methods) and below system (systems or products).

The core formula of VA/VE is the fundamental equation of value engineering:

\[ V = \frac{F}{C} \]

where \(V\) is Value, \(F\) is Function, and \(C\) is Cost. This formula may seem simple, but it reveals the entire methodology of VA/VE: there are five basic paths to increase value—reduce cost while keeping function unchanged, enhance function while keeping cost unchanged, enhance function and reduce cost simultaneously, significantly enhance function with a slight cost increase, or slightly reduce function with a substantial cost decrease. The last path is often the most controversial in engineering practice, as it touches on the boundary judgment between "functional redundancy" and "necessary functions."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

VA/VE was born out of a structural contradiction: the design phase determines 70%–80% of a product's cost, yet design teams are often the least informed about cost composition. In highly complex products like humanoid robots, this contradiction is dramatically amplified—a single choice in joint module selection can cause cost fluctuations of thousands of yuan, while design engineers typically focus their attention on performance metrics.

The historical positioning of VA/VE can be understood from two dimensions:

  1. Temporal dimension: VE (Value Engineering) originated during World War II under conditions of material shortages, proposed by Lawrence Miles at General Electric (specific year to be confirmed independently). It was initially used to find substitute materials without sacrificing functionality; VA (Value Analysis) is the extension of VE applied after a product enters mass production. The difference lies in the timing of intervention—VE intervenes during the design phase, while VA intervenes during the mass production phase, but the methodological core is identical.

  2. Industry chain dimension: In the humanoid robot industry chain, VA/VE sits at the midstream position, connecting upstream component selection with downstream mass production of complete units. Unlike DFM (Design for Manufacturing), which focuses on the manufacturability of individual parts, or BOM cost engineering, which concentrates on item-by-item accounting of the bill of materials, VA/VE stands at the intersection of "function-cost" and questions the product definition itself.

Principle Breakdown

① Functional Analysis: The First Principle of VA/VE

The starting point of VA/VE is not cost but function. Each function is described in a "verb + noun" format, such as "transmit torque," "support loads," or "dissipate heat." The core of functional analysis is distinguishing between basic functions (the reason the product exists) and secondary functions (supporting functions that enable the basic functions). In a humanoid robot, the basic function of a harmonic reducer is "reduce speed and increase torque," while secondary functions include "provide stiffness," "withstand axial forces," "seal and lubricate," etc.

② Functional Cost Allocation: Attributing Costs to Functions

Traditional cost accounting aggregates costs by part, whereas VA/VE requires aggregating costs by function. A single part may serve multiple functions, and a single function may be realized by multiple parts. Establishing this "function-cost" matrix often reveals surprising facts: certain secondary functions consume a disproportionate share of costs.

③ Value Index and Improvement Directions

\[ V_i = \frac{F_i}{C_i} \]

where \(V_i\) is the value index of the \(i\)-th function, \(F_i\) is the function importance coefficient (typically obtained by normalizing team scoring), and \(C_i\) is the function cost coefficient (the proportion of total cost attributed to that function). When \(V_i < 1\), it indicates that the function has "high cost, low value" and is a key target for improvement; when \(V_i > 1\), it indicates that the function has "low cost, high value," and consideration may be given to increasing investment to enhance overall performance.

④ Five Paths for Creative Stimulation

After identifying low-value functions, VA/VE generates alternative solutions through methods such as brainstorming and TRIZ. Typical paths include: eliminate (is this function truly necessary?), combine (can it share components with other functions?), substitute (is there a cheaper way to achieve it?), simplify (can the number of parts realizing this function be reduced?), and standardize (can standard parts replace custom parts?).

Key Parameters and Specifications

The effectiveness of VA/VE implementation is typically measured using the following metrics:

Metric Definition Typical Target Value (Engineering Judgment)
Cost reduction rate (Original cost - New cost) / Original cost 10%–30% (depending on product maturity)
Function retention rate Number of retained basic functions / Total original functions ≥95%
Implementation cycle Time from proposal to mass production introduction 3–6 months
Return on investment Savings amount / VA/VE project investment ≥5:1

It should be noted that the typical target values above are based on engineering judgment; actual projects may vary significantly due to product complexity, team experience, and supply chain conditions, and should be confirmed independently.

Horizontal Comparison

The core differences between VA/VE and adjacent methods lie in the analysis object and timing of intervention:

Method Analysis Object Timing of Intervention Core Question
VA/VE Function-cost Design phase (VE) / Mass production phase (VA) Is this function worth this price?
DFM Part manufacturability Design phase Can this part be manufactured economically?
DFA Assembly efficiency Design phase Can these parts be assembled efficiently?
BOM cost engineering Bill of materials Mass production phase Is the cost of each material item optimal?

The complementary relationship between VA/VE and DFM/DFA is particularly evident: DFM/DFA answers "how to make the design easier to manufacture and assemble," while VA/VE answers "whether this design is worth manufacturing"—the former optimizes means, the latter questions ends. In humanoid robot mass production practice, VA/VE typically determines "what to do," followed by DFM/DFA determining "how to do it."

Who Uses It: Application Cases

In the humanoid robot field, typical application scenarios for VA/VE include:

  1. Joint module selection: Comparing harmonic reducer + frameless torque motor solutions from different suppliers, evaluating cost differences while meeting basic functional requirements such as torque density, backlash, and lifespan. A VA/VE team might discover that the "high precision" feature of a premium reducer is not a basic function in bipedal walking scenarios, thereby selecting a lower-cost alternative.

  2. Structural component lightweighting: Applying topology optimization and material substitution to structural parts such as linkages and housings. Topology optimization can automatically form load-bearing skeletons under given load paths, removing material that does not participate in load transmission—this characteristic of "material existing only where needed" makes it a powerful tool for lightweighting humanoid robot linkages. VA/VE evaluates the value balance between the "weight reduction" function and the "increased cost" during this process.

  3. Sensor configuration optimization: Humanoid robots require numerous sensors for perception and state estimation. VA/VE teams review the functional necessity of each sensor item by item—whether the "backup function" provided by certain redundant sensors justifies their cost and weight penalties.

Limitations and Boundaries

VA/VE is not a universal tool; its limitations include:

  1. Difficulty in quantifying functions: The function importance coefficient \(F_i\) relies on subjective team scoring, and different stakeholders (design, manufacturing, procurement, after-sales) may have vastly different weight judgments for the same function. This subjectivity can lead to repeated revisions of solutions in the absence of a strong decision-maker.

  2. Insensitivity to hidden costs: VA/VE focuses on explicit manufacturing costs but has limited capability to assess hidden costs (e.g., after-sales maintenance costs, brand image damage, supply chain risks). A cost-reducing design may increase after-sales failure rates, and such long-term costs are difficult to fully capture within the VA/VE framework.

  3. Not applicable to disruptive innovation: VA/VE optimizes within an existing functional framework; it does not question the fundamental issue of "whether the product needs to exist." For disruptive innovation from 0 to 1, VA/VE has little to offer.

  4. High data requirements: VA/VE requires accurate cost data to support functional cost allocation. In the emerging field of humanoid robots, the supply chain is not yet mature, and cost data often fluctuates significantly with low transparency, limiting the credibility of analysis results.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "VA/VE is just cutting costs"—Wrong. The first principle of VA/VE is "preserve function"; cost reduction must be carried out without compromising basic functions. Cutting necessary functions for short-term cost savings is the most typical misuse of VA/VE.

  2. "VA/VE is a mass production phase concern"—Wrong. VE (Value Engineering) intervention during the design phase yields far better results than VA (Value Analysis) remediation during the mass production phase. After design freeze, 70%–80% of costs are already locked in, leaving VA with only marginal optimization.

  3. "VA/VE is the same as DFM/DFA"—Wrong. DFM/DFA optimizes "how to manufacture," while VA/VE questions "whether it is worth manufacturing." The former is means optimization, the latter is ends examination. In practice, they are used together, but their analysis objects and intervention logic are fundamentally different.

  4. "Functional analysis is a waste of time"—Wrong. Functional analysis is the core that distinguishes VA/VE from ordinary cost-reduction activities. Cost reduction without functional analysis is merely "negotiating prices item by item"; with functional analysis, it becomes possible to identify "which costs are necessary and which are wasteful."
