Overview

Market Opportunities and the Scenario Canvas is the first process node in the commercial and strategic validation work package (P0.1) of the P0 phase (project initiation and commercial baseline) in the humanoid robot product development process, numbered ent_process_p0_1_1. Through methods such as TAM/SAM/SOM analysis, user personas, scenario storyboards, and competitive benchmarking (Boston Matrix), it clarifies in which scenarios robots create quantifiable economic value, and outputs the Business Model Canvas along with at least 3 prioritized scenarios and corresponding KPIs. What it truly changes is not the technical fantasy of "what this robot can do," but the commercial decision-making framework of "who this robot should serve, at what price, and which problems it should solve first."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Market Opportunities and the Scenario Canvas belongs to the method type, covering the domains of applications and markets, as well as policy, regulation, and ethics. Its value chain level is the validation and market layer. Within the three parallel work packages of the P0 phase, it belongs to commercial and strategic validation (P0.1), precedes intellectual property and compliance strategy (P0.3), and is the first process node initiated in the P0 phase.

This process node is internally structured according to a unified five-level task hierarchy: P0.1.1.1 Input Review and Target Quantification, P0.1.1.2 Solution/Method Design, P0.1.1.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Production, P0.1.1.4 Validation and Issue Closure, P0.1.1.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery. Each subtask is further broken down into 3 level-4 key actions, forming a complete execution loop.

From a process dependency perspective, P0 is a critical prerequisite for P1 (requirements definition)—"the commercial and regulatory baseline determines the requirements boundary." This means that the Business Model Canvas, prioritized scenarios, and KPIs output by this process node will directly determine the design space for the subsequent P1 requirements phase. Scenario selection determines the robot's form factor, price range, and performance priorities, which in turn affects the DOF and dynamics requirements of P4 joints, the packaging approach of P5 structures, and the architecture scheme of P13 electronics and electrical systems.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the history of humanoid robot product development, a recurring failure mode is "technology first, market later"—teams spend years overcoming technical challenges such as motion control and dexterous manipulation, only to discover at product launch that the target scenario lacks sufficient willingness to pay, or that cheaper alternatives already exist. The essence of this failure is not inadequate technology, but incorrect scenario selection.

The Market Opportunities and the Scenario Canvas process node exists precisely to answer three questions systematically before technical investment begins: for whom (user personas), what to do (scenario storyboards), and why win (competitive benchmarking and Boston Matrix). It transforms "market opportunity" from intuitive judgment into a quantifiable analytical framework, ensuring that investments across the subsequent 16 phases (P1 to P16) all point toward economically valuable scenarios.

From a process positioning perspective, this process node sits at the very front of the P0 phase, and its output is one of the core deliverables of the P0.1 commercial and strategic validation. Without scenario assumptions validated by this process node, subsequent requirements definition, system architecture, and joint selection may all be built on incorrect premises.

Principle Breakdown

① Input Review and Target Quantification: Turning Vague Business Intentions into Verifiable Metrics

The core actions of P0.1.1.1 are "list all upstream input items and confirm versions," "convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs," and "establish task owners, timelines, and risk registers." Upstream inputs here include: corporate strategic planning, market research reports, technical feasibility assessments, and regulatory policy analyses. The key action is confirming the versions of these inputs—because different versions of market data can lead to completely different scenario judgments.

A typical approach to converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs is:

\[ \text{KPI}_{\text{Scenario}} = f(\text{TAM}, \text{SAM}, \text{SOM}, \text{Willingness to Pay}, \text{Competitive Barriers}) \]

Where TAM (Total Addressable Market) is the theoretical maximum market, SAM (Serviceable Available Market) is the serviceable market, and SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is the obtainable market. These three narrow progressively, forming the quantitative basis for scenario prioritization.

② Solution/Method Design: Freezing Methodology with Candidate Solutions and Evaluation Matrices

P0.1.1.2 requires "forming no fewer than 2 candidate solutions" and "establishing an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring." The candidate solutions here are not robot design proposals, but rather market entry strategy options—for example: Option A focuses on the warehousing and logistics scenario, Option B focuses on the home service scenario, and Option C focuses on the special operations scenario. Each option must be validated using four tools: TAM/SAM/SOM analysis, user personas, scenario storyboards, and competitive benchmarking (Boston Matrix).

Typical dimensions of the evaluation matrix include: market size, growth rate, competitive intensity, technical feasibility, regulatory risk, and customer willingness to pay. Each dimension is assigned a weight, candidate solutions are scored quantitatively, and ultimately "a review is organized and the solution is frozen."

③ Implementation/Prototype/Sample Production: Turning Scenario Storyboards into Testable Hypotheses

The "prototype" in P0.1.1.3 is not a mechanical mock-up, but rather a scenario prototype—including user journey maps, scenario storyboards, and value proposition canvases. Key actions are "build models/prototypes and record key parameters," "execute simulation or prototype validation," and "record anomalies and deviations." The "simulation" here can be role-playing, user interviews, focus groups, or simple financial model calculations.

④ Validation and Issue Closure: Testing Scenario Hypotheses with Data

P0.1.1.4 requires "developing test/review plans and pass criteria," "executing tests and recording raw data," and "outputting issue lists and improvement measures." The validation targets are whether KPIs are achievable, whether scenario assumptions hold, and whether competitive barriers genuinely exist. Pass criteria typically include: SOM reaching target values, customer interviews validating willingness to pay, and regulatory obstacles being resolvable.

⑤ Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery: Making the Commercial Baseline Traceable

P0.1.1.5 outputs the Business Model Canvas and at least 3 prioritized scenarios with corresponding KPIs, and "updates the ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chain." Here, the ICD (Interface Control Document) ensures consistency between scenario requirements and subsequent technical requirements; the BOM (Bill of Materials) provides preliminary cost estimates; the SOP (Standard Operating Procedure) standardizes processes; and the requirements traceability chain ensures that every subsequent phase's requirements can be traced back to this process node's scenario decisions.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Typical users of this process node include: product managers (responsible for scenario definition and user personas), market analysts (responsible for TAM/SAM/SOM calculations), strategic planning teams (responsible for Boston Matrix and competitive benchmarking), and financial analysts (responsible for KPI quantification and financial modeling).

Application Case: Project Initiation of a Humanoid Robot for the Warehousing and Logistics Scenario

Assume a company plans to develop a humanoid robot. The execution process of the P0.1.1 process node is as follows:

  1. Input Review: Confirm that market research report V2.1, technical feasibility report V1.0, and corporate strategic plan V3.0 are the valid versions.
  2. Target Quantification: Set KPIs—SOM reaching 500 units/year within 3 years, customer willingness to pay ≥ RMB 150,000/unit, and payback period ≤ 3 years.
  3. Solution Design: Form 3 candidate solutions—warehousing and logistics, home service, and special operations. Evaluation matrix scoring results: warehousing and logistics 82 points, special operations 75 points, home service 61 points (example values, engineering judgment).
  4. Scenario Prototype: Create a warehousing and logistics scenario storyboard containing three sub-scenarios—picking, transportation, and sorting—and record key parameters (payload 10 kg, battery life 4 hours, positioning accuracy ±5 cm).
  5. Validation: Validate willingness to pay through customer interviews and confirm that regulatory obstacles (safety standards) are resolvable.
  6. Delivery: Output the Business Model Canvas and KPIs for 3 prioritized scenarios, and update the requirements traceability chain.

Limitations and Boundaries

The limitations and boundaries of this process node include:

  1. Timeliness of market data: TAM/SAM/SOM calculations rely on market research data, and the humanoid robot market is in a period of rapid change—data may become obsolete within 6-12 months. The KPIs output by this process node need to be periodically reviewed in subsequent phases.
  2. Uncertainty of scenario assumptions: Scenario storyboards are constructed based on current technical capabilities and market understanding, and cannot fully predict scenario restructuring caused by technological breakthroughs or market disruptions.
  3. Inherent errors in quantitative methods: KPI quantification involves numerous assumptions (such as market growth rates and penetration rates), and deviations in these assumptions propagate to decisions in all subsequent phases.
  4. No technical feasibility validation: This process node only screens scenarios from a commercial perspective and does not validate technical feasibility—technical feasibility is the responsibility of the P1 requirements phase and subsequent phases.
  5. Limited depth of regulatory restriction identification: This process node only identifies the existence of regulatory restrictions and does not develop specific compliance solutions—the latter is the responsibility of P0.1.3 Intellectual Property and Compliance Strategy.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The bigger the TAM, the better"—Wrong. TAM is the theoretical maximum market, but what truly matters is SOM (obtainable market). A scenario with a TAM of hundreds of billions but an SOM of only tens of millions is inferior to a scenario with a TAM of tens of billions but an SOM of one billion. Engineering judgment: SOM is the basis for decision-making.

  2. "The more scenarios, the better"—Wrong. The completion criteria require "at least 3 prioritized scenarios," but prioritization implies ranking. If the KPIs of 3 scenarios are all vague, that is equivalent to having no priorities at all. Engineering judgment: better to have clear KPIs for 2 scenarios than vague KPIs for 5.

  3. "The Boston Matrix is just drawing a four-quadrant chart"—Wrong. The value of the Boston Matrix lies in using the two dimensions of market growth rate and relative market share to screen scenarios, but the judgment criteria need to be adjusted to account for the unique characteristics of the humanoid robot industry (high R&D investment, long return cycles).

  4. "KPI quantification is just making up numbers"—Wrong. KPIs must come from upstream inputs (market data, competitive benchmarking, customer interviews) and must be tested through the validation loop of P0.1.1.4. Arbitrarily set KPIs will trigger cascading deviations in subsequent phases.

  5. "Once this process node is done, it's done forever"—Wrong. Market conditions, competitive landscapes, and regulatory policies are all changing. The Business Model Canvas and scenario KPIs output by this process node need to be reviewed before the end of the P0 phase and at the start of the P1 requirements phase. Engineering judgment: it is recommended to set up a mid-phase review point within the P0 phase.
