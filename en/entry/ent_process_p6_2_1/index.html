Overview

Verification of forward and inverse kinematics solution is the first third-level subtask in the URDF modeling and kinematics calibration phase (P6.2). Its core objective is to confirm the consistency between the robot kinematics model (URDF) and the CAD design through systematic testing and quantitative metrics, while also validating the accuracy and reliability of the inverse kinematics (IK) solver. This task uses "random 1000 pose IK success rate > 99%, position error < 1 mm" as its completion criterion. What it truly changes is not merely "running a simulation once," but rather transforming "model correctness" from a qualitative judgment into a quantifiable, traceable engineering acceptance process.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Verification of forward and inverse kinematics solution is a structured engineering validation process comprising five fourth-level subtasks: input organization and target quantification (P6.2.1.1), scheme/method design (P6.2.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P6.2.1.3), test execution and result analysis (P6.2.1.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P6.2.1.5). Its technical means include analytical/numerical inverse solutions, random pose sampling, and singularity analysis. The validation targets are the consistency of joint limits and link lengths in the URDF model with CAD, as well as the success rate and accuracy of the IK solver.

From a mathematical perspective, forward kinematics defines the mapping \(x = f(q)\) (from joint space to task space), while inverse kinematics solves its inverse mapping \(q = f^{-1}(x)\). The core of the validation task lies in confirming that \(f(q)\) is consistent with the physical prototype (forward solution verification) and that \(f^{-1}(x)\) converges reliably within the target workspace (inverse solution verification).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the robot development pipeline, the URDF model serves as the common input to the simulation, control, and planning toolchain. If the model is inconsistent with CAD—for example, a link length deviation of 0.5 mm (example value, engineering judgment) or incorrect joint limit settings—simulation results will systematically diverge from actual robot behavior, and all downstream algorithms relying on this model (motion planning, dynamics control, state estimation) will inherit this error. Historically, such issues often surface only during prototype debugging, where the cost of correction is extremely high.

Verification of forward and inverse kinematics solution exists precisely to detect and eliminate these deviations during the "design phase" rather than the "debugging phase." It quantifies the acceptance criteria as "random 1000 pose IK success rate > 99%, position error < 1 mm," giving model correctness a clear, testable criterion. What it truly changes is not the "validation method" itself, but rather making "model credibility" an auditable, traceable engineering milestone in the development process.

Principle Breakdown

① Forward Kinematics Verification: URDF-CAD Consistency Check

The core of forward kinematics verification is confirming that the kinematic parameters in the URDF (link lengths, joint origins, rotation axis directions, joint limits) match the CAD model. The specific approach is to export key dimensions from CAD and compare them item by item against the <origin>, <axis>, and <limit> fields in the URDF. For high-degree-of-freedom systems like humanoid robots (typically 30+ DOF), manual comparison is impractical; automated scripts are needed to extract URDF parameters and perform numerical comparison against the CAD-exported parameter table, with tolerances typically set at the sub-millimeter level (engineering judgment).

② Inverse Kinematics Verification: Random Sampling and Success Rate Statistics

Inverse kinematics verification employs a random pose sampling strategy: 1000 end-effector poses are randomly generated within the workspace, and the IK solver is invoked to solve each one, with success rate and position error being recorded. The mathematical foundation of this method lies in the multi-solution nature and singularity of the IK problem:

\[ q^* = \arg\min_{q} \| f(q) - x_d \|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad q_{\min} \le q \le q_{\max} \]

where \(x_d\) is the desired end-effector pose, and \(q_{\min}\), \(q_{\max}\) are the joint limits. Numerical solvers (such as damped least squares, Levenberg-Marquardt) approximate \(q^*\) through iterative minimization of pose error. A success rate below the threshold typically indicates: incorrect URDF parameters, unreasonable joint limit settings, or unhandled singular regions within the workspace.

③ Singularity Analysis: Identifying Unreachable Regions

Singularity refers to configurations where the Jacobian matrix \(J(q)\) is rank-deficient, potentially causing the IK solver to diverge or produce abrupt joint velocity changes. The validation process must identify singular regions within the workspace and confirm whether these regions fall outside actual task requirements. For humanoid robots, singular configurations commonly occur in poses such as fully extended arms or nearly upright legs.

Horizontal Comparison

Compared with IK verification for general robotic arms, verification of forward and inverse kinematics solution for humanoid robots exhibits significant differences. General 6-DOF arms (satisfying the Pieper condition) admit analytical closed-form solutions, with the verification focus being multi-solution selection strategies; humanoid robots, by contrast, typically include 7-DOF redundant arms and 6-DOF legs, where numerical solving is the mainstream approach, shifting the verification focus to solver convergence and singularity robustness. Furthermore, the humanoid robot workspace contains numerous self-collision constraints and joint limit constraints, and random sampling must account for the combined effects of these constraints (engineering judgment).

Compared with academic IK algorithm evaluations (such as the TRAC-IK benchmark), this task is positioned as engineering acceptance rather than algorithm research: it does not pursue upper-bound algorithm performance but rather ensures that the currently selected solver meets established thresholds within the target workspace.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

This validation process is a standard procedure for kinematics model delivery in robot product development, with primary users including:

  1. Kinematics engineers: Execute URDF parameter extraction, CAD comparison, IK solver configuration, and testing;
  2. Simulation engineers: Receive validated URDF models for dynamics simulation and motion planning;
  3. Control algorithm engineers: Rely on validated kinematics models to develop WBC (whole-body control) and task-space control strategies;
  4. System integration engineers: Compare simulation models against actual robot behavior during prototype debugging.

Typical application scenario: In bipedal humanoid robot development, this validation ensures that gait planning results from simulation can be directly transferred to the physical robot without extensive on-site tuning. If validation fails, all downstream work dependent on the kinematics model is blocked.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limitations of static validation: This validation covers only the kinematics level and does not involve identification or validation of dynamics parameters (mass, inertia, friction), which belong to subsequent subtasks of P6.2 or a separate phase;
  2. Blind spots in sampling coverage: Although random 1000 poses carry statistical significance, they cannot guarantee coverage of all boundary conditions (such as extreme poses near joint limits), necessitating supplementary targeted boundary testing (engineering judgment);
  3. Solver dependency: Validation results are strongly correlated with the chosen IK solver; switching solvers requires re-validation;
  4. Accuracy of the CAD baseline: Validation uses the CAD model as the reference; if the CAD itself deviates from the physical prototype, the validation cannot detect this deviation;
  5. No dynamic performance coverage: The position error < 1 mm metric applies only to static poses and does not include tracking errors during motion.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Validation passed = model is completely correct"—Wrong. Validation covers only the sampled pose space and uses CAD as the reference, so absolute correctness cannot be guaranteed;
  2. "The higher the IK success rate, the better"—Not necessarily. An excessively high success rate may indicate an overly relaxed solver (e.g., allowing larger pose errors); the position error metric must also be checked;
  3. "Random sampling can replace boundary testing"—Wrong. Random sampling has low coverage probability for boundary conditions; targeted testing near joint limits and singular configurations must be supplemented;
  4. "Validation is a one-time effort"—Wrong. The URDF model undergoes iterative updates during development (e.g., structural optimization, joint limit adjustments), and validation must be re-executed after each change;
  5. "Numerical IK is always worse than analytical IK"—Not necessarily. Modern numerical solvers (such as TRAC-IK) often outperform analytical methods in robustness, especially for redundant-degree-of-freedom systems.
