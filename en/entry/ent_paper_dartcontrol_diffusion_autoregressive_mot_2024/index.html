Summary

Text-conditioned human motion generation, which allows for user interaction through natural language, has become increasingly popular. Existing methods typically generate short, isolated motions based on a single input sentence. Institutions per source list: ETH Zürich.

Overview

DartControl proposes a unified diffusion autoregressive motion primitive model (DART) that achieves real-time text-driven long-sequence motion generation at over 300 frames per second on a single RTX 4090, while supporting precise spatial control through latent noise optimization and reinforcement learning. The method uses overlapping motion primitives as its representation, performs diffusion denoising in latent space with only 10 steps, and ensures online autoregressive stability through scheduled training. It outperforms existing baselines in text-conditioned temporal composition, motion interpolation, and human-scene interaction tasks.

What It Changes

Existing text-conditioned motion generation methods (T2M-GPT, MDM, etc.) are essentially "single-sentence, single-segment" offline generators that cannot handle long sequences composed of multiple action description segments. FlowMDM supports temporal composition but requires prior knowledge of the complete action timeline (oracle information), generates at only 31 frames per second with a latency of up to 161 seconds, making it completely unsuitable for interactive applications. What DART truly changes is advancing text-driven motion generation from the "offline batch processing" paradigm to a "real-time online streaming" paradigm—it no longer requires pre-planning the entire timeline but instead generates autoregressively segment by segment based on text prompts arriving online, with a first-frame latency of only 0.02 seconds.

More importantly, DART unifies "text semantic control" and "spatial constraint control" within the same latent motion primitive space: it achieves motion interpolation and human-scene interaction through latent noise optimization (DDIM deterministic mapping + gradient descent), and text-conditioned goal reaching through reinforcement learning (using latent noise as the action space). Previously, these tasks were handled separately by specialized methods such as DNO, OmniControl, and GAMMA, which were isolated from each other and lacked text-conditioning support. DART demonstrates that a single foundation model can simultaneously serve generation, optimization, and decision-making tasks—an architectural paradigm shift.

Method Breakdown

Motion Primitive Representation

  • Long-term motion is modeled as a sequence of overlapping primitives: each primitive \( P^i = [H^i, X^i] \), where \( H \) frames of history (overlapping with the previous primitive) and \( F \) frames of future; in experiments \( H=2, F=8 \)
  • Each frame uses an over-parameterized SMPL-X representation with dimension \( D=276 \), including root translation \( t \), root orientation \( R \), local joint rotations \( \theta \), joint positions \( J \), and temporal difference features for position and rotation (\( dt, dR, dJ \))
  • All frames are normalized in a local coordinate system (centered at the pelvis of the primitive's first frame), and global motion is recovered via inverse transformation after generation

Latent Motion Primitive Space (VAE)

  • A Transformer-based VAE (encoder/decoder with 7 layers each, hidden dimension 256, 4 attention heads, dropout 0.1) compresses motion primitives into a 256-dimensional latent space
  • The encoder takes history \( H \), future \( X \), and learnable distribution tokens \( T_\mu, T_\sigma \) as input, and samples \( z \) via reparameterization; the decoder predicts \( \hat{X} \) conditioned on \( z \) and \( H \)
  • Loss: \( L_{VAE} = L_{rec} + 10^{-6} L_{KL} + 100 L_{aux} \), where \( L_{rec} \) is a smooth L1 reconstruction loss and \( L_{aux} \) regularizes temporal difference features

Latent Diffusion Denoiser

  • A Transformer-based denoiser \( G(z_t, t, H, c) \) predicts the clean latent variable \( \hat{z}_0 \), taking as input the noisy latent \( z_t \), diffusion step \( t \) (embedded via a small MLP), history \( H \), and CLIP text embedding \( c \)
  • Uses DDPM with only 10 diffusion steps for both training and inference, with a cosine noise schedule; text is randomly masked with probability 0.1 during training to support classifier-free guidance (with \( w=5 \) at inference)
  • Loss: \( L_{denoiser} = L_{simple} + L_{rec} + 10000 L_{aux} \)

Scheduled Training (Key Design)

  • Three-stage training progressively introduces the test-time distribution: Stage 1 uses only real history for 100K iterations; Stage 2 mixes real and rolled-out history with a linear probability (0→1) for 100K iterations; Stage 3 always uses rolled-out history (maximum rollouts \( N=4 \)) for 100K iterations
  • Purpose: to address long-term instability and text controllability degradation caused by distribution shift in history frames during autoregressive generation

Spatial Control

  • Motion control is formulated as \( M^* = \arg\min_M F(\Pi(M), g) + cons(M) \), where \( \Pi(\cdot) \) is a task-dependent projection
  • Latent Noise Optimization: Uses a deterministic DDIM sampler, treating DART sampling as a deterministic mapping from \( Z_T \) to motion, and directly optimizes the latent noise via the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.05, 100-300 steps, gradient normalization)
  • Reinforcement Learning Control: Uses latent noise \( z_T \) as the action space, training an actor-critic policy with PPO (4-layer MLP, hidden dimension 512, residual connections), with input state \( [H^i, g^i, s^i, c^i] \)

Key Innovations

  1. Latent-space autoregression + extremely few diffusion steps: After compressing motion primitives into a 256-dimensional latent space, high-quality motion can be generated with only 10 diffusion steps (ablation shows FID degrades significantly at 2 steps, but performance is nearly identical from 5 to 100 steps). This enables real-time generation at 334 frames per second, approximately 10× faster than FlowMDM, while maintaining the best Segment FID (3.79) and Transition FID (1.86).

  2. Scheduled training addresses distribution shift in online autoregression: This is the key to DART's stable long-sequence generation. Ablation shows that removing scheduled training (DART-schedule) degrades Segment FID from 3.79 to 8.08 and R-prec from 0.62 to 0.39—demonstrating that standard training alone cannot handle the distribution difference between rolled-out history at test time and real history at training time.

  3. Unified framework covering generation-optimization-decision tasks: The same latent motion primitive space and denoiser achieve motion interpolation (Goal error 0.54cm vs. DNO 5.52cm) and human-scene interaction via latent noise optimization, and text-conditioned goal reaching via RL (100% success rate vs. GAMMA 95%). Previously, these tasks required completely different specialized models; DART proves that a single foundation model can serve three types of downstream tasks simultaneously.

Key Ablation Findings

  • Removing the VAE (DART-VAE): Transition FID degrades from 1.86 to 1.79 (PJ increases from 0.06 to 0.20), with significantly increased jitter
  • Removing scheduled training (DART-schedule): Segment FID degrades to 8.08, R-prec drops to 0.39
  • Diffusion steps: 10 steps perform nearly identically to 100 steps (Segment FID 3.79 vs 4.16), with significant degradation at 2 steps (4.44)

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper explicitly acknowledges limitations including: the latent noise optimization method has high computational cost (approximately 74 seconds for a 60-frame target sequence with 100 optimization steps), making it unsuitable for real-time control scenarios; and DDIM step skipping is not used because it introduces artifacts in generated motion. A more critical implicit limitation is that DART relies on datasets with frame-level aligned text annotations (such as BABEL); when trained on coarse-grained sentence-level labels from HML3D, text-motion alignment degrades significantly—motion switches between described actions in random order because each short motion primitive essentially matches only part of the sequence semantics. Additionally, the HumanAct12 subset and left-right mirrored sequences in HML3D provide only joint positions rather than SMPL parameters and are therefore excluded, limiting data coverage. The paper does not address generalization to unseen action categories, multi-person interactions, or complex scenes (non-flat terrain).

Engineering Insights

When reproducing DART, the highest priority is verifying the implementation details of scheduled training—the three-stage training (100K+100K+100K iterations) and maximum rollout count \( N=4 \) are central to ensuring long-sequence stability; skipping or simplifying this component will directly more than double the Segment FID. Second is the VAE's KL weight: the paper's ablation shows that with \( w_{KL}=1 \), reconstruction error degrades from 0.08 to 0.44, failing to accurately reconstruct complex actions like cartwheels; a value on the order of \( 10^{-6} \) is essential. The auxiliary loss weights \( w_{aux}=100 \) (VAE) and 10000 (denoiser) are critical for suppressing jitter; removing them increases jerk from 2.45 to 3.67.

The most common engineering pitfall is normalization of the over-parameterized motion representation: all frames must be processed in a local coordinate system centered at the pelvis of the primitive's first frame, and the inverse transformation must be stored and applied after generation to recover global motion; otherwise, cumulative drift will occur in long sequences. Text encoding must use CLIP rather than other text encoders, and the 0.1 masking probability during training directly affects the effectiveness of classifier-free guidance. For downstream teams, if real-time spatial control is needed, latent noise optimization (74 seconds/segment) is unsuitable for online scenarios and the RL policy (240 frames/s) should be used instead; if coarse-grained text labels need to be handled, the semantic alignment of DART must first be validated against requirements, otherwise extensions such as hierarchical latent spaces should be introduced.
