Overview

Software stability and regression testing is a critical verification process in the humanoid robot full-system integration and validation testing phase (P15). Its core task is to ensure that the robot does not crash or experience functional degradation under long-duration operation, anomaly injection, and version iteration. This method employs long-duration operation, stress testing, fault injection, and version regression as its primary tools, with "no crashes during long-duration operation and successful recovery from critical fault injections" as the completion criteria. It serves as the quality gate connecting durability testing and certification preparation.

Content

What It Is: From "Functionally Correct" to "Continuously Correct"

Software stability and regression testing refers to systematic testing activities conducted after the full-system hardware and software integration of a humanoid robot, involving prolonged continuous operation, extreme load application, deliberate fault injection, and version iteration regression verification of the robot software system. It does not verify "whether a specific function works at a specific moment," but rather "whether the entire software system remains reliable under the triple pressures of continuous operation, abnormal interference, and code changes."

What it truly changes is not the precision of a specific algorithm or module, but the granularity of reliability verification—advancing from "functional tests passed" to "stability across the time dimension, robustness across the anomaly dimension, and non-degradation across the change dimension." In joint and full-system durability testing (P15.3.1), the verification target is the fatigue life of mechanical structures and hardware; in P15.3.2, the verification target shifts to the software system's memory leaks, resource exhaustion, deadlocks, anomaly recovery capabilities during prolonged operation, and behavioral consistency after version upgrades.

In WBS V3, this process is located in the P15 full-system integration and validation testing phase, alongside joint and full-system durability testing (P15.3.1) and certification preparation and pre-audit (P15.3.3), collectively forming the third sub-task family of the P15 phase—durability and certification verification. Its upstream inputs are safety function testing (P15.2.1), performance benchmark testing (P15.2.2), and environmental adaptability testing (P15.2.3), while its downstream connects to P16 small-batch trial production and mass production preparation.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Software failures are the primary source of robot field issues—this is the fundamental reason for this process's existence. In real-world deployment of humanoid robots, mechanical failures often have precursors (abnormal noises, temperature rise, wear), while software failures are frequently sudden, intermittent, and difficult to reproduce. A system that runs normally for 10 minutes in the lab may crash after 8 hours of continuous operation due to memory leaks; a behavior verified on an older version may silently degrade after a new version merge.

From a historical perspective, software stability and regression testing is an essential gateway for robot products to transition from "prototype validation" to "reliable delivery." In early R&D stages, software verification focused primarily on functional correctness; however, by the P15 full-system integration stage, the robot is already a complete physical-information system, and any software instability directly manifests as dangerous full-system behavior—such as sudden power loss during walking, planner deadlocks causing joint loss of control, or communication timeouts triggering false safety protection activations. Therefore, P15.3.2 exists essentially to provide a safety net for the full system's safety and reliability.

Principle Breakdown

① Long-Duration Operation Testing: Reliability Verification Across the Time Dimension

The core principle of long-duration operation testing is the cumulative failure model. Many software defects (such as memory leaks, file descriptor exhaustion, timer drift) accumulate over runtime and cannot be exposed by single short-duration tests. Its mathematical foundation can be expressed as:

\[ R(t) = e^{-\lambda t} \]

where R(t) is the probability of the system operating without failure over time t, and λ is the failure rate. The essence of long-duration operation testing is to expose defects with low λ by extending t—a defect that does not appear in a 1-hour test may appear with high probability in a 100-hour test (based on corpus data calculations, if the failure rate is 0.01/hour, the probability of no failure over 100 hours is approximately 36.8%). In engineering practice, "continuous operation for N hours without crashes" is typically used as the acceptance criterion, where N must cover multiples of typical mission cycles and worst-case operating conditions.

② Stress Testing: Limit Verification Across the Load Dimension

The core principle of stress testing is resource contention and bottleneck exposure. During high-dynamic motion of a humanoid robot, the perception, planning, control, and communication modules operate simultaneously under high load, pushing CPU, memory, bus bandwidth, and real-time budgets to their limits. Stress testing deliberately creates abnormal loads (such as high-frequency perception inputs, concurrent multi-tasking, extreme motion commands) to force the system to expose resource contention, scheduling delays, and priority inversion issues that remain hidden under normal loads.

③ Fault Injection: Robustness Verification Across the Anomaly Dimension

The core principle of fault injection is deliberately creating faults and verifying recovery mechanisms. Key fault scenarios for robot systems include: sensor disconnection, communication timeouts, actuator power loss, watchdog triggers, and memory allocation failures. Fault injection testing actively introduces these faults to verify whether the system's fault detection, isolation, and recovery (FDIR) mechanisms work as designed. The acceptance criterion of "successful recovery from critical fault injections" means that after a fault occurs, the system must detect the anomaly, enter a safe state, and resume normal operation once the fault is cleared—rather than crashing or entering an unknown state.

④ Version Regression: Consistency Verification Across the Change Dimension

The core principle of version regression is change impact analysis. Every software iteration (new feature merges, bug fixes, parameter adjustments) may introduce new defects or break existing behaviors. Regression testing re-executes historical test case suites to verify that changes have not caused functional degradation. Its key constraint is test coverage—the effectiveness of regression testing depends on the degree to which the test case suite covers system behavior; insufficient coverage renders regression testing ineffective.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Within the WBS V3 framework, the executing entities of P15.3.2 are the full-system integration and validation testing team, with specific roles including: software test engineers (responsible for test plan design and execution), system integration engineers (responsible for test environment setup and fault injection implementation), and log analysis engineers (responsible for runtime data collection and root cause analysis).

Typical application scenarios include:

  • Full-system continuous operation verification: The robot operates continuously for hours to days in typical mission modes, with monitoring of memory, CPU, communication latency, and other metrics to verify no crashes or performance degradation.
  • Critical fault injection drills: Deliberately disconnecting sensors, injecting communication timeouts, and simulating actuator power loss to verify whether the system can detect anomalies and enter a safe state.
  • Version upgrade regression: After each software version update, re-executing the core test case suite to ensure the new version has not broken existing functionality.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Test coverage cannot be exhaustive: The state space of a software system is extremely large, and no test case suite can cover all possible execution paths and input combinations. Test coverage is one of the key constraints of P15.3.2, but coverage improvement follows the law of diminishing returns—after reaching a certain threshold, the benefit of adding test cases drops sharply. Engineering judgment: coverage priorities should be risk-oriented rather than pursuing absolute full coverage.

  2. Log analysis capability is a bottleneck: Long-duration operation testing generates massive amounts of log data, and identifying root causes requires strong log analysis capabilities. If log recording is incomplete, formats are inconsistent, or analysis tools are insufficient, even if tests detect anomalies, root causes may be difficult to identify, preventing issue closure.

  3. Fault injection fidelity is limited: Deliberately injected faults may differ in characteristics from real faults, leading to inconsistencies between test results and field performance. For example, injected communication timeouts may not fully simulate signal distortion under real electromagnetic interference.

  4. Regression testing maintenance costs: As system functionality grows, regression test case suites expand continuously, increasing execution time and maintenance costs. A balance must be struck between coverage and cost.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Stability testing just means running for a long time"—Wrong. Long-duration operation is only one dimension of stability testing; stress testing, fault injection, and version regression are equally indispensable. A system may run continuously for 100 hours without crashing, yet degrade severely in performance under high load, or crash outright after sensor disconnection.

  2. "Regression testing just means re-running old test cases"—Wrong. Effective regression testing requires change impact analysis to determine which test cases need re-running, which can be skipped, and which need to be added. Blindly re-running all test cases wastes resources and may miss new risks introduced by changes.

  3. "Fault injection will damage hardware"—Partially correct. Fault injection does carry risks, but through carefully designed injection scenarios, pre-validation in simulation environments, and safety protection mechanisms, risks can be controlled within acceptable ranges. Engineering judgment: fault injection should be validated in simulation environments first, then executed on real systems.

  4. "Passing tests means the software is reliable"—Wrong. Passing tests only proves that "no defects were found in the scenarios covered by the tests," not that "the software has no defects." Test coverage is limited, and defects may still exist in uncovered scenarios.
