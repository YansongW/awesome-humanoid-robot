Overview

HumanoidUMI, proposed by the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence team, is a robot-free data collection and policy training pipeline for full-body humanoid manipulation. It uses PICO 4 VR tracking and UMI-style handheld grippers to collect human demonstrations, and generates deployable full-body control policies for the Unitree G1 through Spatial Keypoint Retargeting (SKR) and diffusion policies in a sparse task space. The core contribution lies in extending the UMI paradigm from tabletop manipulation to full-body tasks requiring balance and gait coordination.

What It Changes

Humanoid robot skill learning has long been bottlenecked by data collection: teleoperation requires target robot hardware, skilled operators, and safety supervision, making it extremely inefficient and difficult to scale. While the UMI paradigm has proven the value of robot-free demonstrations in tabletop manipulation, directly transplanting it to full-body humanoid tasks fails—because humanoid robots must simultaneously handle manipulation, balance, posture adjustment, and gait, and these morphology-dependent full-body movements cannot be directly mapped from human demonstrations.

What HumanoidUMI truly changes is the possibility boundary of "collecting deployable full-body policy data without the target robot." It does not attempt to solve all challenges of full-body control but instead cleverly decomposes the problem: the high-level policy reasons about manipulation intent only in a sparse task space (keypoint poses), while a learned motion tracking controller handles morphology-related full-body coordination at the low level. This decoupling makes robot-free demonstrations possible while maintaining the deployability of the final policy on physical robots.

Method Breakdown

Data Collection System

  • Hardware: PICO 4 VR (headset + handheld controllers + three lower-body trackers) + two custom UMI-style grippers (fisheye cameras + motor-driven rack-and-pinion mechanism)
  • Tracking: Fuses inertial, geomagnetic, and active infrared data, outputting SMPL-format 6-DoF body motion estimates
  • Recorded modalities: wrist fisheye images, controller 6-DoF poses, pelvis/foot poses, gripper widths (motor encoders)
  • Key design: SKR module runs online, recording lower-body DoF positions q_low_t as policy conditioning input

High-Level Policy (Diffusion Policy)

  • Action space: default 47-dimensional (5 keypoints × 9 + 2 gripper widths), augmented 65-dimensional (7 keypoints × 9 + 2)
  • Keypoint poses expressed in the pelvis coordinate frame, future poses relative to query-time pose: T_rel_k,τ = (T_pel_k,t)^(-1) T_pel_k,τ
  • Normalization: translation and gripper width min-max scaled to [-1,1], 6D rotations unscaled
  • Observations: 224×224 left/right wrist RGB image pairs + 15-dimensional lower-body proprioception vector with three-frame history
  • Prediction horizon H = 48, receding-horizon action chunks

Spatial Keypoint Retargeting (SKR)

  • Morphology-aware anisotropic adjustment: only leg keypoints undergo pelvis-local vertical scaling, d̄_i = [d_i,x, d_i,y, λ_i·d_i,z], λ_leg = 0.75 to compensate for human-G1 leg length differences
  • Constraint-weighted IK: q*t = argmin Σ w_i‖e_i(q_t)‖²₂ + λ_q‖q_t − q‖²₂, two-stage optimization (foot support first, then pelvis/TCP)

Low-Level Controller

  • Motion tracking controller trained in MJLab, running at 50 Hz, outputting 29-dimensional residual joint positions
  • Desired joint positions: q_des_t = q⁰ + s ⊙ clip(a_t, −a_max, a_max), executed via joint-space PD control

Key Innovations

  1. Robot-free full-body data collection: First to extend the UMI paradigm to humanoid tasks requiring full-body coordination, replacing teleoperation with consumer-grade VR devices + handheld grippers, achieving 2-61× higher data collection throughput, with novices approaching experienced-user efficiency after 10 minutes of instruction.

  2. Sparse task space + hierarchical control architecture: The high-level policy reasons only in the task space of 5-7 keypoints without directly predicting joint commands; the low-level learned motion tracking controller handles morphology-related full-body coordination. This decoupling avoids the data explosion of end-to-end policies in full-body tasks while maintaining physical deployability.

  3. Morphology-aware retargeting in SKR: Not simple full-body IK, but anisotropic scaling (vertical only, λ=0.75) targeting humanoid leg length differences, combined with two-stage weighted IK that prioritizes foot support and orientation consistency. This design decision directly addresses the core contradiction in mapping human demonstrations to humanoid robot kinematics.

Experiments and Results

Five real-world tasks (Unitree G1), 20 trials each:

Task Key Result
Cluttered tabletop pick-and-place Replacing SKR with GMR significantly reduces success rate

Under-table trash disposal Seven keypoints (with knees) outperform five keypoints

Key implications: Walking-related tasks show the largest speedups (12-61×), indicating teleoperation is extremely inefficient in mobile manipulation scenarios; novices using HumanoidUMI achieve throughput close to experienced users across all tasks, demonstrating the system's low entry barrier.

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly mention a limitations section. Boundaries inferable from factual points: validated only on Unitree G1, not tested on other robot platforms; no large-scale data collection or data scaling validation; no evaluation of long-horizon/multi-step complex tasks; no sim-to-real transfer validation; no quantitative comparison with end-to-end joint prediction policies; no robustness testing under varying lighting, backgrounds, or object configurations. Key engineering parameters such as training data volume, training configuration (epochs/batch size/learning rate), inference frequency, and GPU model are not mentioned.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, prioritize checking the three most error-prone points: Delay matching—the paper explicitly states that removing delay matching degrades performance in the dynamic ball throwing task, indicating that relative delay calibration between wrist observations, gripper state, policy inference, and robot control is system-critical; it is recommended to strictly follow UMI's delay matching procedure. SKR λ_leg calibration—0.75 is an empirical value for human-G1 leg length differences; changing robots or operator body types requires recalibration, otherwise leg retargeting will fail. Two-stage weighted IK weight settings—the optimization order of foot support first, then pelvis/TCP, is key to ensuring balance; directly switching to single-stage IK will likely cause falls during deployment.

Practical advice for downstream teams: if the goal is rapid accumulation of humanoid manipulation data, this system is indeed an order of magnitude more efficient than teleoperation, but note that the policies it produces are hierarchical structures of "task-space keypoints + low-level tracking controller," not end-to-end joint command policies—if your downstream requires direct joint-level control, an additional adaptation layer is needed. Additionally, the seven-keypoint variant (adding knees) significantly outperforms the five-keypoint version in tasks requiring squatting or lower-body coordination, so enabling it by default is recommended.
