Overview

The Unitree H1 is a full-size high-dynamic humanoid robot launched by Unitree Robotics, positioned as a research platform for scientific research, embodied intelligence studies, and high-dynamic motion validation. It utilizes the self-developed M107 permanent magnet synchronous motor, with a peak knee joint torque of 360 N·m and a peak torque density of approximately 189 N·m/kg, setting a record of 3.3 m/s running speed for full-size humanoid robots. What the H1 truly changes is not the appearance of humanoid robots, but the intersection of "full-size + high-dynamic + mass-producible"—previously, only two of these three could typically be achieved simultaneously.

What It Is: Precise Definition of the Unitree H1

The Unitree H1 is a full-size universal humanoid robot released by Unitree Robotics in 2023, belonging to the robot system (robot_system) category, with its value chain covering midstream complete machine design and upstream core components. The H1 base version has 26 DOF in total, while the H1-2 upgraded version has 27 DOF; the base version stands approximately 180 cm tall and weighs about 47 kg, whereas the H1-2 is approximately 178 cm tall and weighs about 70 kg.

The core characteristics of the H1 can be summarized in three points: high-power-density powertrain (M107 joint motors + industrial-grade crossed roller bearings), 360° depth perception (3D LiDAR + depth camera), and continuous OTA software upgrades. It supports ROS2 and the Unitree SDK, features Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and hot-swappable batteries, and is suitable for university laboratories, AI research institutions, and industrial prototyping.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the history of humanoid robot development, there has long been an "impossible triangle": full-size (height above 170 cm), high-dynamic (running speed exceeding 3 m/s), and mass-producible (price and supply chain supporting commercialization). Early humanoid robots like ASIMO achieved full-size and walking capabilities but had limited speed and extremely high costs; research platforms such as the MIT Cheetah series achieved high dynamics but were not full-size humanoid forms.

The H1's positioning is precisely to break this triangle. Through a quasi-direct drive architecture and high-torque-density motors, it achieves a running speed of 3.3 m/s (officially marked as a world record) while maintaining a lightweight design of approximately 47 kg, and enters the market at a price of approximately 90,000 USD (current pricing to be confirmed). What it truly changes is not the locomotion capability of humanoid robots, but the cost curve of full-size humanoid robots transitioning from "laboratory one-offs" to "batch-deliverable research tools".

Principle Breakdown

① High Torque Density: Core Contribution of the M107 Joint Motor

The H1's knee joint peak torque reaches 360 N·m, hip joint approximately 220 N·m, and ankle joint approximately 59 N·m (H1 base version); the H1-2 has a knee joint of 360 N·m, hip joint of 220 N·m, waist of 220 N·m, and ankle joints of 75×2 N·m. Behind these figures lies Unitree's self-developed M107 permanent magnet synchronous motor (PMSM), featuring a low-inertia high-speed inner-rotor structure, which the company claims outperforms mainstream international joint motors in response speed and heat dissipation.

The peak torque density of 189 N·m/kg means: in the integrated joint unit combining motor and reducer, each kilogram of mass can output 189 N·m of peak torque. This metric directly determines the "explosive power-to-weight ratio" of the robot's legs—for high-dynamic actions such as running and jumping, torque density is more important than absolute torque.

② Quasi-Direct Drive Architecture: Balance Between Transparency and Explosive Power

The H1's joints adopt a low-reduction-ratio transmission scheme (specific reduction ratio to be confirmed), differing from traditional high-reduction-ratio harmonic drives. The core advantage of quasi-direct drive lies in backdrivability: the motor-side torque can be reflected relatively directly to the joint output, allowing force control algorithms to achieve good torque estimation without relying on expensive joint torque sensors. Its physical basis can be expressed as:

\[ \tau_{\text{joint}} \approx G \, k_t \, I_m - J_{\text{eq}} \ddot{\theta}_m \]

where \(G\) is the reduction ratio, \(k_t\) is the motor torque constant, \(I_m\) is the motor current, \(J_{\text{eq}}\) is the equivalent moment of inertia, and \(\ddot{\theta}_m\) is the motor angular acceleration. The second term is the inertia compensation term. Due to the small reduction ratio, friction and backlash nonlinearities are also small, making torque estimation sufficiently accurate in engineering practice—this is the key to the H1 achieving high-dynamic motion control without relying on expensive force sensors.

③ Perception and Computing: 360° Depth Perception Loop

The H1 is equipped with 3D LiDAR + depth camera, achieving 360° depth perception and real-time acquisition of high-precision spatial data for panoramic scanning. The computing platform can be optionally equipped with Intel Core or NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX (specific model configuration to be confirmed), supporting ROS2 and the Unitree SDK, providing software and hardware interfaces for upper-level perception, planning, and reinforcement learning algorithms.

Horizontal Comparison

Comparison with Tesla Optimus: Optimus is also positioned as a full-size universal humanoid robot, but the Unitree H1 places greater emphasis on the single metric of "high-dynamic locomotion capability," while Optimus focuses more on "general task manipulation" and mass-production cost control. The H1's 3.3 m/s running speed is significantly higher than Optimus's announced walking speed (specific value to be confirmed), but Optimus has deeper deployment in dexterous hands and task manipulation.

Comparison with AgiBot Yuanzheng A1: The Yuanzheng A1 also targets the scientific research and embodied intelligence market, but the two differ in joint solutions and perception configurations (specific parameters to be confirmed). The H1's core differentiator lies in its 189 N·m/kg peak torque density and 3.3 m/s running record, giving it a unique position in high-dynamic motion research.

Comparison with Berkeley Humanoid: The Berkeley Humanoid is a mid-sized research platform built internally by the Berkeley team, emphasizing low cost, lightweight design, and high impact resistance and reliability; the H1 is a commercial full-size platform, comprehensively surpassing the Berkeley Humanoid in size, torque, and speed metrics, but also costing several orders of magnitude more (specific comparison to be confirmed).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The primary user groups of the H1 include:

  • University robotics laboratories: Used for locomotion algorithm research, reinforcement learning sim-to-real transfer validation, and whole-body motion control (WBC) algorithm development.
  • AI research institutions: Serving as the physical carrier for embodied AI, deploying VLA models, diffusion policies, and other visual-motion policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy can be deployed on the H1).
  • Industrial prototyping: Validating the feasibility of humanoid robots in scenarios such as logistics and inspection, though the H1 is currently more of a research platform than a vertical-scenario-specific device.

A typical research pipeline is: training control policies in simulation environments (e.g., Isaac Sim, MuJoCo) → deploying to the physical H1 via the Unitree SDK → iterating policies using 360° depth perception and OTA upgrades. The H1's ROS2 support enables seamless integration into existing robotics software ecosystems.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Insufficient arm DOF: The H1 base version has only 4 DOF per arm, making complex manipulation tasks difficult; upgrading to the H1-2's 7 DOF solution (shoulder×3 + elbow×1 + wrist×3) is required to support fine manipulation research.
  2. Limited endurance: The 0.864 kWh battery supports approximately 1.5–2 hours of operation, requiring frequent hot-swappable battery changes during extended experiments.
  3. Payload capacity: The H1-2's total weight increases to approximately 70 kg, but specific payload parameters need to be confirmed; the base H1's payload capacity has not been officially disclosed.
  4. Price barrier: The approximately 90,000 USD price tag (current pricing to be confirmed) makes it primarily accessible to institutional users rather than individual developers.
  5. Running speed trade-offs: The H1's 3.3 m/s is a running record, but the H1-2's mobility speed drops to < 2 m/s, indicating a trade-off between speed and payload/DOF.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The H1 is a direct-drive robot"—Incorrect. The H1 adopts a quasi-direct drive architecture, i.e., low reduction ratio + high-torque-density motor, rather than a pure direct drive without reducers. Without the reducer, the M107 motor's torque would be far from sufficient to support the 360 N·m joint peak torque.
  2. "The H1-2 is just an enhanced version of the H1"—Inaccurate. The H1-2 features structural changes in total DOF (26→27), leg DOF (5→6), arm DOF (4→7), and ankle torque (59→75×2 N·m), and its mobility speed drops from 3.3 m/s to < 2 m/s, indicating a design shift from "extreme running" to "manipulation and payload capability."
  3. "3.3 m/s is the H1's sustained running speed"—Needs caution. The official specification states "Moving speed of 3.3 m/s (world record)" alongside "Potential mobility > 5 m/s," suggesting this is more likely a peak/short-duration speed rather than a sustained cruising speed (engineering judgment).
  4. "189 N·m/kg is the torque density of the bare motor"—Inaccurate. This metric refers to the peak torque density of the integrated joint unit (motor + reducer + bearings, etc.), not the bare motor.
