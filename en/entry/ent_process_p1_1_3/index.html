Overview

The mapping of regulations, standards, and safety requirements is a critical process node in the P1 requirements definition and system concept phase of the humanoid robot product development process. Its core task is to translate standards requirements such as ISO 10218-1/2, ISO/TS 15066, IEC 61508, and IEC 62368, as well as market access regulations like CE/FCC/UL, into quantifiable engineering constraints early in the design phase. What it truly changes is not the volume of compliance documentation, but the decision-making approach where "safety is a hard constraint from day one of design"—safety requirements must become hard constraints early in the design phase, rather than being addressed retroactively.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The mapping of regulations, standards, and safety requirements (ent_process_p1_1_3) is a Level 3 subtask under the P1 phase (requirements definition and system concept) of the humanoid robot development process in the knowledge graph, belonging to the P1.1 requirements engineering work package. The core deliverables of this process include regulatory requirements matrix, compliance roadmap, and safety integrity levels (SIL/PL). Its method/tool combination includes: ISO 10218-1/2, ISO/TS 15066, IEC 61508, IEC 62368, and CE/FCC/UL gap analysis.

In the WBS V3, this process is uniformly expanded into five Level 4 key actions: input review and target quantification (P1.1.3.1), solution/method design (P1.1.3.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P1.1.3.3), verification and issue closure (P1.1.3.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P1.1.3.5). This "five-step" structure ensures that each subtask has clear inputs, methods, outputs, and acceptance criteria.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In the early days of the humanoid robot industry, teams often deferred compliance work until after the product prototype was completed. The result: mechanical engineers selected actuators based on performance metrics, software engineers defined control logic based on functional requirements, and only during the certification testing phase did they discover missing safety circuits, non-compliant force limits, and electromagnetic compatibility failures—rework costs escalated exponentially, and certification timelines stretched several times longer.

The mapping of regulations, standards, and safety requirements exists precisely to eliminate this pain point. It requires the team to answer three questions during the concept design phase: In which regions will the product be sold (regional differences)? How long is the certification cycle (certification cycle)? What is the testing budget (testing costs)? These three key constraints directly determine the direction of the product architecture—for example, if the target market is the EU, the Machinery Directive and EMC Directive under the CE mark must be incorporated into design constraints from day one.

Principle Breakdown

① Hierarchical Structure of the Standards System

Safety compliance for humanoid robots is not covered by a single standard but by a multi-layered standards system:

  • ISO 10218-1/2: Safety requirements for industrial robots, covering the robot itself and robot systems/integration, respectively. This is the foundational threshold for humanoid robots entering industrial scenarios.
  • ISO/TS 15066: Technical specification for collaborative robots, defining force and pressure limits for human-robot collaboration. This is the key basis for human-robot coexistence in humanoid robots.
  • IEC 61508: The foundational functional safety standard, defining Safety Integrity Levels (SIL1 to SIL4). Safety-related control systems in humanoid robots must meet functional safety requirements: in the event of a fault, the system must still be able to bring the robot to a safe state.
  • IEC 62368: Safety standard for audio/video, information, and communication technology equipment, covering electrical safety and protection against energy hazards.
  • CE/FCC/UL: Market access regulatory frameworks, corresponding to EU, US FCC, and UL certification requirements, respectively.

② Core Logic of Gap Analysis

Gap analysis is the core tool of this method. Its logic is: compare the product's current design state against the requirements of the target standard item by item, identify "gap items," and then develop corrective actions and priorities for each gap item. The results of the gap analysis feed directly into the regulatory requirements matrix, forming a traceable requirements chain.

③ Quantification of Safety Integrity Levels

Functional safety levels are typically quantified using the SIL (Safety Integrity Level) from IEC 61508 or the PL (Performance Level) from ISO 13849. The PL levels from ISO 13849 correspond to the probability of dangerous failure per hour (\(PFH_d\)):

PL \(PFH_d\) (per hour) Typical Application Scenarios
PL a \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-5}\) Low risk, acceptable minor injury
PL b \(3 \times 10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-5}\) Medium risk
PL c \(10^{-5}\) to \(3 \times 10^{-6}\) Higher risk
PL d \(3 \times 10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-6}\) High risk, requires redundant architecture
PL e \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-7}\) Extremely high risk, requires highest safety integrity

(Based on corpus data: PL d corresponds to a probability of dangerous failure per hour between \(3 \times 10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-6}\), meaning a dangerous failure is permitted only once every 11.4 to 114 years on average.)

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Application Scenario 1: Industrial Humanoid Robots Entering Automotive Factories

A team planned to deploy humanoid robots on an automotive assembly line to work collaboratively with workers. In the P1.1.3 process, the team first confirmed the target market was the EU, making the Machinery Directive (2006/42/EC) under the CE mark mandatory. The team then applied ISO 10218-1/2 to define safety requirements for the robot itself and system integration, and ISO/TS 15066 to quantify force and pressure limits for collaborative scenarios. The gap analysis revealed that the current design's peak joint force exceeded the ISO/TS 15066 limits, requiring the addition of force sensors or adjustments to the control strategy. Ultimately, the team set the safety integrity level to PL d and designed redundant safety circuits accordingly.

Application Scenario 2: Service Humanoid Robots Entering Home Environments

A team developed a home service humanoid robot targeting both North American and EU markets. In the P1.1.3 process, the team needed to simultaneously satisfy UL certification (North America) and CE marking (EU) requirements. The gap analysis found differences between North American electrical safety requirements (UL 62368-1) and the EU's IEC 62368, requiring separate power module and insulation designs. Additionally, the human-robot interaction scenarios in home environments required compliance with ISO/TS 15066 force limits, leading the team to adjust joint reduction ratios and control bandwidth accordingly.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Standards Lag Behind Technological Development

Humanoid robots are an emerging technology, and existing standards (such as ISO 10218-1/2) are primarily designed for traditional industrial robots, with insufficient coverage of humanoid-specific characteristics such as bipedal locomotion, whole-body coordination, and dynamic interaction. Engineering judgment: in areas where standards are lacking, teams should adopt the "closest standard" principle and proactively communicate with certification bodies.

② Regional Differences Increase Compliance Costs

Regulatory requirements vary significantly across markets: the EU's CE mark covers multiple directives including machinery, EMC, and radio; the US has distinct FCC and UL certifications; and China's CR certification system is still evolving. This means the same product may require multiple rounds of certification testing, making testing costs and certification cycles key constraints.

③ Trade-off Between Safety Levels and Performance

Higher SIL/PL levels imply stricter architectural requirements (such as redundancy and diagnostic coverage), which increase cost, weight, and power consumption. Engineering judgment: teams need to find a balance between safety levels and product performance to avoid over-engineering.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Compliance is a certification-phase issue"—Wrong. Safety requirements must become hard constraints early in the design phase, not be addressed retroactively. Conducting gap analysis only after the prototype is complete leads to exponentially increasing rework costs.

  2. "One standard covers all markets"—Wrong. Regulatory requirements vary significantly across regions; CE, FCC, and UL each have different emphases and must be analyzed separately.

  3. "Higher SIL levels are always better"—Wrong. Higher SIL levels mean more complex architectures and higher costs; the appropriate level should be selected based on risk assessment results.

  4. "Gap analysis only needs to be done once"—Wrong. As design iterations and standards updates occur, gap analysis must be continuously updated, and the regulatory requirements matrix should be maintained as a living document.
