Summary

A unified whole-body reinforcement-learning controller enables a 21-DoF humanoid to play badminton in simulation and on hardware, achieving 21-hit rallies in simulation and outgoing shuttle speeds up to 19.1 m/s in real-world tests.

Sources

  • Humanoid Whole-Body Badminton via Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning

Overview

This paper presents the first humanoid robot system that plays badminton in the real world, driven by a unified whole-body reinforcement learning policy. Through a three-stage curriculum that coordinates footwork and racket swings, it achieves zero-shot transfer to the custom-built Phybot C1 hardware. The core contribution lies in advancing agile, contact-rich dynamic interaction from simulation to real-world deployment, validating the feasibility of multi-stage RL in sub-second perception-action loops.

What It Changes

The badminton task exposes a fundamental gap in existing robot learning paradigms: quasi-static interaction assumptions fail in real dynamic environments, while existing testbeds such as table tennis avoid the core challenge through 2D simplifications or lack of whole-body perturbations. What this paper truly changes is pushing the physical constraint that "footwork and striking must co-evolve" from theoretical discussion to engineering practice—lower-limb motion not only repositions the base but directly determines striking accuracy through support and momentum transfer, forcing the policy to simultaneously handle balance maintenance, rapid arm swings, and precise timing under a unified optimization objective, rather than layering independent controllers.

Another key shift is abandoning reference motions or expert demonstrations. The authors show that human-like backswing-swing-recovery action chains can emerge purely through reinforcement learning, along with adaptive behaviors such as short corrective steps, long strides with parallel backswings, and tiptoe extensions tailored to different time windows. This suggests that for time-critical, contact-rich whole-body tasks, curriculum-based reward shaping may be more scalable than imitation learning, as the latter struggles to cover the continuous space of action variants.

Method Breakdown

Three-Stage Curriculum Training

  • S1 Footwork Acquisition: Optimizes only locomotion rewards, learning stable, forward-facing gaits among six sampled strike positions. The primary shaping term is r_track = exp(−4·max(d−0.3, 0)), where d is the horizontal distance between the base and the target, with success defined as approaching within 0.3 m. Converges in approximately 1k iterations.
  • S2 Precision-Guided Swinging: Building on S1, introduces a sparse reward activated only at the predetermined strike time t*: r_swing = exp(−e_ee_pos²/σ_p)·exp(−e_ee_ori/σ_r) + 0.3·r_v, where r_v = 1 − exp(−max(0, v_ee·n*)/σ_v). The key design is the multiplicative coupling of position and orientation errors, preventing the policy from over-optimizing a single metric. Precision scheduling tightens from σ_p=2.0, σ_r=8.0 to σ_p=0.1, σ_r=1.0, allowing full swings to emerge first and then refine. Smooth action chains appear after approximately 20k iterations.
  • S3 Task-Focused Refinement: Removes S1's target-approach rewards and most gait regularizations (feet_orientation, feet_height, feet_distance, sym_contact_forces, sym_step, no_fly) to avoid gradient interference with strike objectives. Retains safety, energy, and hardware constraint terms, and enables domain randomization and observation noise. Strike rewards improve by 3–5%, while energy and torque costs decrease by approximately 20%.

Network and Training Configuration

  • Actor observes 87-dimensional base features plus short/long histories (5 frames + 20 frames), totaling 1,557 dimensions; Critic uses 98-dimensional privileged observations, additionally receiving the next two strike times, next target position and orientation, and remaining target count.
  • Action space comprises joint position targets for all 21 DoFs, uniformly scaled by 0.25, tracked by a 500 Hz low-level PD controller.
  • PPO, single RTX 4090, 4096 parallel environments, MLP 512–256–128, ELU activation, total training time 12.4 hours across three stages.

Deployment Prediction

  • EKF state vector [x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z]^T, using the same badminton dynamics model as training: m·dv/dt = mg − m·‖v‖·v/L (L=3.4). When the predicted height first falls below the strike plane, linear interpolation yields the strike point and time.
  • The no-prediction variant only changes the observation space, replacing explicit targets with a sliding window of 6 badminton positions, randomizing aerodynamic parameters during training, with minimal performance degradation in simulation.

Key Innovations

  1. First Real-World Humanoid Badminton System: Previous work stopped at simulation or simplified hardware; this paper achieves zero-shot deployment on the 1.28 m, 30 kg, 21 DoF Phybot C1 without system identification or manual tuning, validating domain randomization for dynamic contact tasks.

  2. Coupled Position-Orientation Strike Reward: Unlike [6], which handles position and orientation errors separately, this work multiplies them together, preventing the policy from over-optimizing either. This design choice shows superior precision convergence in ablations and better matches the physical essence of badminton's "brief window of aligned position, velocity, and orientation."

  3. Three-Stage Curriculum Eliminates Gradient Interference: Removing lower-limb regularizations in S3 is a key innovation—the authors find that gait shaping terms conflict with strike objectives, and their removal continues to improve strike metrics while reducing energy consumption. This suggests that for whole-body dynamic tasks, motor skills acquired in early stages may require actively "forgetting" certain shaping constraints to unlock full performance.

Experiments and Results

Experiment Key Results
Simulated dual-robot rally Best consecutive returns: 21
Simulated strike precision Position error ≤ 0.10 m, orientation error ≤ 0.2 rad
Real virtual-target swings 71 tests, mean error 24.29 mm, racket speed 5.32 m/s
Repeatability test 20 independent swings, mean error 23.21 mm, std 10.55 mm
Real ball-machine strikes 24/25 successes (1 hit on racket frame edge), average return distance 3.5 m
EKF prediction accuracy Position error < 100 mm at 0.6 s before impact, < 10 mm at 0.3 s; time error approximately 20 ms

In real-world tests, the interception zone was 98 cm × 42 cm, interception height approximately 1.5 m (robot height only 1.28 m), outgoing ball speeds up to 10 m/s, swing speed approximately 5.3 m/s, completing the perception-decision-execution loop within a sub-second time window. Ablation studies show that removing S1 leads to training divergence, S1–S2 is deployable, and S3 breaks the plateau.

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly specify the generalization boundary for target positions outside the training distribution (e.g., farther distances, lower heights). Current tests are limited to machine-served balls within a Mocap arena, without real rallies against human or robot opponents; the no-prediction variant was not deployed to hardware. The Mocap optical volume loses the badminton above 2.8 m, limiting trajectory arc heights; real tests narrowed the interception zone due to safety and field-of-view constraints. Additional latency was not modeled, actuator network models were not fitted, system identification was not performed, and no force or tactile sensors were used. The policy may fail when consecutive target spacing is too large, exceeding available maneuver time.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, prioritize checking three aspects: first, PD gain configuration—ankle roll and shoulder yaw stiffness is only 5 N·m/rad, far lower than the 50–100 of other joints, a critical trade-off between balance maintenance and swing flexibility; second, the six regularization terms removed in S3 must be fully excluded, otherwise gradient interference significantly reduces strike precision; third, domain randomization ranges (friction 0.5–1.0, push interval 5, base CoM offset ±0.08) are essential for zero-shot transfer and cannot be omitted.

The most common pitfall is calibrating the badminton dynamics parameter L=3.4—EKF prediction accuracy heavily depends on this value, and Mocap-measured trajectories must be used for validation before deployment. The training data filtering criteria (interception points x∈[−0.8,0.8], y∈[−1,0.2], z∈[1.5,1.6]) directly determine policy coverage; if downstream tasks require a larger interception zone, the trajectory corpus must be regenerated and the σ schedule adjusted. Action scaling of 0.25 means policy outputs must be multiplied by this factor before feeding the PD controller; using raw outputs directly causes joint position overshoot.
