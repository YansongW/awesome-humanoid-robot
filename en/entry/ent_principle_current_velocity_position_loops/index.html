Overview

The current loop, velocity loop, and position loop form a three-level cascaded feedback control structure in servo drive systems: the innermost current loop (torque loop) directly controls motor torque with millisecond-level response, the middle velocity loop regulates rotational speed, and the outermost position loop tracks joint angles. Each layer's bandwidth is approximately 5-10 times that of the next layer to ensure the stability of the cascaded system. What this truly changes is not the motor itself, but a qualitative leap from "being able to spin" to "being controllable."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The current loop, velocity loop, and position loop are a three-level cascaded feedback control structure in servo drive systems, arranged from highest to lowest bandwidth. Practical joint drives typically employ three-level cascaded control: the innermost current loop (torque loop), the middle velocity loop, and the outermost position loop. Each layer's bandwidth is approximately 5-10 times that of the next layer to ensure stability.

  • Current loop: The fastest inner loop controlling motor phase current as the controlled variable, directly determining output torque.
  • Velocity loop: Controls motor rotational speed as the controlled variable, outputting current commands.
  • Position loop: Controls joint angle as the controlled variable, outputting velocity commands.

These three form a nested structure: the output of the outer loop serves as the command for the inner loop, and the execution speed of the inner loop is far faster than the command update rate of the outer loop, ensuring each layer "sees" a relatively stable controlled plant.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

① Single-loop control cannot simultaneously satisfy rapidity and precision. If only a position loop directly controls the motor, the position error signal must pass through the entire mechanical transmission chain (reducer, links, load inertia) to be converted into torque. The inertia, friction, and elasticity of the mechanical system introduce significant phase lag, causing the system to either respond sluggishly or oscillate and diverge. The essence of the cascaded structure is layering the control problem: the inner loop first solves "is the torque accurate," the middle loop solves "is the speed stable," and only then does the outer loop handle "does the position track."

② The current loop is the physical foundation of force control. The output torque of a permanent magnet synchronous motor is proportional to the q-axis current, so the current loop is essentially the torque loop. Without the current loop, precise torque control is impossible, and advanced algorithms requiring force interaction such as impedance control and admittance control cannot be supported. The current loop bandwidth directly determines the range of impedance (Z-width) that a joint can render, which is a hard constraint for safe interaction in humanoid robots.

③ Historical context: evolution from analog to digital. Early servo systems implemented the current loop with analog circuits, achieving high bandwidth but suffering from severe parameter drift. After digital control became widespread, the three-loop structure runs at fixed sampling rates in DSP/MCU, with bandwidth limited by sampling rate and computational latency. Modern servo drives commonly use FOC (Field-Oriented Control) for the current loop, combined with PI controllers and feedforward compensation, making three-loop cascading the standard architecture in industry and robotics.

Principle Breakdown

① Cascaded bandwidth principle: why the inner loop must be 5-10 times faster

The core constraint for cascaded control stability is bandwidth separation: each layer's bandwidth is approximately 5-10 times that of the next layer. Let the inner loop closed-loop transfer function be \(G_i(s)\) and the outer loop controller be \(C_o(s)\). During outer loop design, the inner loop can be approximated as a first-order inertial element:

\[ G_i(s) \approx \frac{1}{\tau_i s + 1}, \quad \tau_i = \frac{1}{2\pi f_i} \]

where \(f_i\) is the inner loop bandwidth. When the outer loop bandwidth \(f_o \ll f_i\), the inner loop dynamics can be neglected, and the outer loop sees an "almost ideal torque source." If the bandwidth ratio is insufficient, the inner loop's phase lag enters the vicinity of the outer loop's gain crossover frequency, causing instability. The empirical 5-10 times ratio is precisely the minimum safe distance that guarantees stability margins in engineering practice.

② Typical bandwidth ranges for the three loops

Control Loop Typical Bandwidth Controlled Variable Output
Current loop 1-5 kHz Motor phase current PWM duty cycle
Velocity loop 50-500 Hz Motor rotational speed Current command
Position loop 5-100 Hz Joint angle Velocity command

The current loop bandwidth typically reaches 1-5 kHz; the velocity loop 50-500 Hz; the position loop 5-100 Hz, depending on load inertia, stiffness, and sampling rate. Bandwidth decreases by approximately one order of magnitude from top to bottom, forming a pyramid structure of "fast inside, slow outside."

③ Coordination of PI controllers and feedforward

The controller for each loop typically adopts a PI structure: the proportional term provides immediate correction, and the integral term eliminates steady-state error. However, pure feedback PI has inherent lag when tracking rapidly changing trajectories, so feedforward is commonly added in engineering—precomputing and injecting control effort based on the desired trajectory. Taking the position loop as an example:

\[ u_{pos}(t) = K_{p,p} e_p(t) + K_{i,p} \int_0^t e_p(s)\, ds + K_{ff} \dot{\theta}_{des}(t) \]

where the third term is velocity feedforward, which drives the motor in advance without waiting for error to develop, significantly improving tracking performance. The ratio of feedforward to feedback is the core trade-off in engineering tuning: excessive feedforward amplifies model errors, while insufficient feedforward degenerates into pure feedback.

④ Anti-windup: the overlooked stability killer

When the motor reaches current limits or voltage saturation, the integrator continuously accumulates error, leading to large overshoot or even oscillation after exiting saturation. The anti-windup mechanism freezes or rolls back the integral accumulation when the integral term reaches its limit, preventing excessive accumulation during saturation and avoiding large overshoot after desaturation. This is one of the most critical differences between textbook PID and production-ready PID.

Horizontal Comparison

① Comparison with single-loop PID

Single-loop PID directly outputs torque commands based on position error, offering simple structure but poor dynamic performance: it cannot distinguish between "large position error but zero velocity" and "large position error with rapid approach." Three-level cascading, through the intermediate buffering of the velocity loop, allows the position loop to face only the question of "can the velocity command be tracked," greatly reducing tuning difficulty.

② Comparison with Direct Torque Control (DTC)

DTC directly controls flux linkage and torque without the PI regulation of the current loop, achieving faster dynamic response, but suffers from large torque ripple and poor low-speed performance. The current loop in three-level cascading is based on FOC, offering smooth torque output and better suitability for joint drives requiring precise torque control.

③ Comparison with Model Predictive Control (MPC)

MPC handles position, velocity, and torque constraints simultaneously within a single optimization problem and could theoretically replace three-level cascading. However, MPC's computational load grows rapidly with prediction horizon and the number of constraints, making real-time execution difficult at joint control cycles above 1 kHz. Three-level cascading, with its fixed structure and fixed sampling rate PI control, remains the only pragmatic choice on computationally constrained embedded platforms.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Humanoid robot joint modules

Nearly all commercial and research humanoid robots employ three-level cascaded control for joint drives. Taking Unitree Robotics as an example, its self-developed M107 permanent magnet synchronous motor paired with a custom driver runs cascaded current, velocity, and position loop control internally, supporting dynamic walking and jumping in models such as H1 and G1.

② Prerequisite for impedance control

Research by Focchi et al. (2016) explicitly demonstrates that inner-loop torque bandwidth, velocity filtering, and sampling frequency have decisive impacts on impedance control stability. Insufficient inner-loop bandwidth shrinks the feasible range of stable stiffness and damping (Z-width), while velocity filtering introduces phase lag that further compresses the stable region. The bandwidth design of three-level cascading directly determines the physical interaction characteristics that impedance control can render.

③ Industrial servos and collaborative robots

Servo drive products from manufacturers such as Inovance Technology, Infineon, and TI all use three-level cascading as their core, paired with real-time buses like EtherCAT for multi-axis synchronization. Torque sensors in collaborative robots are typically installed outside the current loop, fusing current-loop torque estimation with external sensor data to achieve collision detection and force limiting.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Bandwidth is physically constrained and cannot be increased indefinitely. Current loop bandwidth is limited by inverter switching frequency (typically 10-20 kHz) and current sampling rate; the velocity loop is limited by encoder resolution and the phase lag of velocity filtering; the position loop is limited by mechanical resonance frequency. Blindly increasing the gain of any loop will excite mechanical resonance or amplify noise.

② The cascaded structure is sensitive to parameter variations. Changes in load inertia alter the equivalent controlled plant for the velocity and position loops, causing bandwidth and stability margin drift. During humanoid robot walking, load inertia varies significantly with posture, making fixed-parameter PI controllers unable to maintain optimal performance across all operating conditions, requiring gain scheduling or adaptive control (engineering judgment).

③ Torque constraints cannot be directly handled. The outputs of the position and velocity loops are velocity/current commands; torque constraints can only be indirectly implemented through current loop limiting. When a joint requires precise torque control (e.g., compliant manipulation), it must switch to torque mode or adopt impedance control; three-level cascading itself does not provide torque closed-loop control.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Higher bandwidth is always better"—Wrong. Research by Focchi et al. shows that maximizing inner-loop bandwidth is not suitable for applications requiring rendering of specific impedance parameter ranges, as it significantly affects the feasible range of stable stiffness and damping. Bandwidth design must match application requirements rather than blindly pursuing high bandwidth.

  2. "Three-level cascading is just three independent PIDs"—Wrong. Strong coupling exists among the three loops: the inner loop's phase lag enters the outer loop's stability analysis, and outer loop command changes excite inner loop transient responses. The correct approach is to treat the cascaded system as a whole for bandwidth allocation and stability margin verification.

  3. "Feedforward can replace feedback"—Wrong. Feedforward relies on accurate models (inertia, friction, back-EMF constant), and model errors directly translate into tracking errors. The role of feedforward is to "reduce the burden on feedback," not to "replace feedback."

  4. "Anti-windup is just a nice-to-have"—Wrong. In scenarios of high-speed joint motion or collision, current commands easily reach limits. Without anti-windup, the integrator accumulates significant error, leading to large overshoot or even oscillation after desaturation. Anti-windup is a necessary mechanism for ensuring system stability under extreme operating conditions.
