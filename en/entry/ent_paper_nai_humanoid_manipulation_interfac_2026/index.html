Summary

HuMI is a portable, robot-free data-collection and hierarchical-learning framework that translates human whole-body manipulation demonstrations into autonomous humanoid skills. Using handheld sensorized grippers and wearable trackers, it trains a high-level Diffusion Policy to predict keypoint trajectories and a low-level RL whole-body controller to execute them, achieving 3× teleoperation throughput and 70% success in unseen environments on a Unitree G1.

Overview

HuMI is a data collection and hierarchical policy learning system for full-body manipulation of humanoid robots, consisting of a robot-free demonstration hardware setup (handheld grippers + Vive Trackers) and a two-level architecture of "high-level Diffusion Policy + low-level RL whole-body controller." The core contribution lies in using human-in-the-loop IK preview to address the kinematic adaptation problem from human demonstrations to humanoid robots, and through mechanisms such as adaptive end-effector rewards, variable-speed augmentation, and relative pose tracking, achieving high success rates across five whole-body tasks on the Unitree G1 along with significant improvements in data collection efficiency.

What It Changes

Full-body manipulation of humanoid robots has long been constrained by two approaches: teleoperation requires operators to simultaneously manage balance and controller errors, making it labor-intensive and difficult to scale; visual sim-to-real reinforcement learning is limited by reward engineering and hardware logistics, demonstrating only a small number of tasks confined to controlled laboratory settings. More fundamentally, existing robot-free data systems (such as UMI) only record end-effector trajectories, which are completely insufficient for specifying whole-body actions like "squatting, kneeling, and bending" that require coordinated movement of the waist, legs, and feet—for the same low-reaching motion, the configuration of the hip, knee, and ankle determines task success.

What HuMI truly changes is the "morphological gap" problem in data collection. Rather than attempting to scale or retarget human demonstrations to robot kinematics (which typically results in self-collision or reachability failures), it uses real-time IK preview to allow human operators to actively adjust their motions during collection, ensuring demonstrations are robot-feasible at the source. Simultaneously, it uses "whole-body trajectories" rather than "end-effector trajectories" as the policy interface, enabling the low-level controller to learn tracking of non-visually-anchored keypoints such as the pelvis and feet, thereby unlocking tasks like deep squats, kneeling, and walking-based cleaning that truly leverage the flexibility of humanoid platforms. This effectively shifts the constraint of "what the robot can do" from the post-processing stage to the data collection stage, changing the data contract of the entire imitation learning pipeline.

Method Breakdown

Data Collection: Robot-Free Demonstration Hardware

  • Two 3D-printed handheld grippers (wrist-mounted GoPro cameras + ArUco markers for tracking gripper width), with five HTC Vive Ultimate Trackers mounted on the grippers, waist, and feet.
  • Vive Ultimate Trackers are chosen over head-mounted systems because the latter suffer from degraded tracking performance due to occlusion when squatting and interacting with ground-level objects.
  • Five key operational frames are tracked: pelvis (floating base), both hands, and both feet; the head is excluded (the target robot Unitree G1 has no actuated neck).
  • The entire setup fits into a single backpack, requires no base stations, and only 1–2 minutes are needed to build an environment tracking map for each new scene.

Human-in-the-Loop Kinematic Adaptation

  • Human trajectories are not scaled; original unscaled poses are preserved to maintain visual-spatial alignment and object interaction geometry; only the pelvis tracker height is scaled.
  • A real-time IK preview interface (Mink solver + Viser visualization) allows operators to adjust demonstrations in real time to satisfy feasibility and task constraints.
  • IK subtasks: track translation/rotation of five keypoints, avoid self-collision, and regularize poses to maintain natural configurations.
  • Key design: controlling a virtual robot subject only to kinematic constraints imposes far less cognitive load than teleoperating a physical robot.

Hierarchical Policy Framework

  • High-level policy: Diffusion Policy (5 Hz), taking camera images + proprioception as input and outputting recursive-horizon task-space trajectories (action chunks: 10 waypoints covering the next 2 seconds).
  • Low-level controller: RL-trained whole-body controller (50 Hz) using a teacher-student framework (teacher accesses privileged states, DAgger distillation to student policy), with student observations including 25-step state history and past actions.

Adaptive End-Effector Tracking Reward

  • Whole-body tracking reward: r_body = Σ_{χ∈{p,θ,v,w}} r(ē_χ^body, σ_χ), where r(ē_χ, σ_χ) = exp(−ē_χ/σ_χ²).
  • Adaptive end-effector reward: r_EE = 𝕀(‖v_base^ref‖ < δ) · Σ_{χ∈{p,θ}} r(ē_χ^EE, σ_χ(v_EE^ref)).
  • σ_χ is linearly interpolated within [σ_χ^min, σ_χ^max] based on reference end-effector velocity (position: 0.01–0.1 m; rotation: 5°–20°; v_max=0.1 m/s, v_min=0.05 m/s).
  • When base velocity exceeds δ, end-effector tracking is deactivated via the 𝕀(·) gate to prioritize stability during fast motion.
  • Curriculum learning: w_EE increases linearly from 0.0 to 0.5, and σ_p^min anneals from 0.1 m to 0.01 m between training steps 10k–15k.

Variable-Speed Augmentation

  • Every 0.02 s, a speed scaling factor s ~ N_[0.25, 1.25] (1.0, σ²) is sampled, with σ increasing linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 1.0 between training steps 10k–15k.
  • Slow-speed variations give the policy sufficient time to correct small errors, promoting learning of high-precision motions.

Policy Interface Improvements

  • Target pose as high-level action reference: Instead of using the actually executed pose as the action chunk reference frame (which causes sudden trajectory reversals), the previously planned target pose is used, naturally connecting the current chunk to the previous one and maintaining smooth momentum for dynamic tasks (e.g., throwing).
  • Relative pose tracking for non-visually-anchored keypoints: The pelvis and feet lack direct visual anchoring, and inference-time errors accumulate irrecoverably (>5 cm drift); the tracking target is modified to relative transformations within the current action chunk rather than absolute transformations. Default is 3 keypoints (grippers + base), extendable to 5 keypoints (adding feet).

Key Innovations

  1. Human-in-the-loop IK preview replacing kinematic retargeting: This is a fundamental restructuring of the "human demonstration → robot policy" paradigm. Traditional methods either scale trajectories (physical scenes are not scalable) or use post-processing retargeting (breaking visual-spatial alignment). HuMI allows humans to actively adjust motions during collection through real-time IK feedback, ensuring both robot feasibility and preservation of original spatial geometry. Ablation shows that removing this component collapses the proposal task success rate from 85% to 10%, proving it is a prerequisite for the system rather than a bonus.

  2. Adaptive end-effector reward + variable-speed augmentation curriculum: The low-level controller faces an inherent conflict between "stable whole-body motion" and "precise end-effector alignment"—pursuing end-effector precision during fast motion leads to falls, while overemphasizing stability at slow speeds makes end-effector errors unacceptable. Through dynamic scaling of σ_χ with reference velocity, base-velocity gating to deactivate end-effector rewards, and variable-speed augmentation enabling the policy to switch between different speed scales during learning, this mechanism allows a single controller to master both slow precise operations (squatting to pick up bottles) and fast dynamic actions (throwing). Ablation shows that removing variable-speed augmentation drops the sword-pulling success rate from 85% to 50%, and removing the adaptive reward drops squat-pickup from 85% to 50%.

  3. Relative pose tracking for non-visually-anchored keypoints: The pelvis and feet have no visual anchoring, and absolute pose tracking during inference accumulates >5 cm drift due to low-level controller errors, causing the walking-cleaning task success rate to collapse from 75% to 0/10. Switching to relative transformations within action chunks confines errors to within chunks rather than accumulating across chunks—a critical engineering decision that makes "whole-body trajectories" truly usable as a policy interface.

  • Training: 7 environments × 7 bottles = 350 demonstrations; testing: 4 unseen environments + 6 unseen objects (including vases with significant shape and texture differences).

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly list limitations in the conclusion section (episode truncated). Inferred boundaries from method details include: the system relies on visual trackers and requires sufficient environmental texture and lighting conditions (Vive Trackers may fail in low-texture or strong-light environments); the low-level controller is not general-purpose—training configurations are unified but cross-task generalization is not validated; evaluation is conducted only on the Unitree G1 platform without validation on other humanoid robots; the 5-keypoint configuration (adding feet) is only reported for the walking-cleaning task (70%) and not systematically evaluated across all tasks; specific hyperparameter values for variable-speed augmentation (s_min, s_max, Δ, etc.) are not disclosed; robustness boundaries for dynamic walking-manipulation transitions, long-duration autonomous operation stability, and performance on more complex terrains are not discussed.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing, prioritize checking the three most error-prone components: First, real-time performance of the human-in-the-loop IK preview—the Mink solver must be fast enough (otherwise operators cannot smoothly adjust motions), and IK targets must use original unscaled trajectories (only pelvis height scaled); any attempt to "optimize" demonstration trajectories will break visual-spatial alignment. Second, the curriculum scheduling of the low-level controller—w_EE increasing linearly from 0 to 0.5, σ_p^min annealing from 0.1 m to 0.01 m between training steps 10k–15k, and variable-speed augmentation σ increasing from 10⁻⁴ to 1.0 must be synchronized; adjusting any one independently may disrupt the learning order of "stabilize global motion first, then refine end-effector alignment." Third, relative pose tracking for non-visually-anchored keypoints—this is the most easily overlooked yet most impactful interface design; absolute pose tracking directly causes 0/10 collapse in the walking-cleaning task, so relative transformations within action chunks must be used rather than global absolute transformations. When comparing data collection efficiency, note that TWIST2 is a teleoperation baseline; HuMI's advantage is especially pronounced in squatting/kneeling-type tasks (TWIST2 completely fails at the proposal task). If downstream tasks are primarily upright manipulation, this advantage may diminish. On the hardware side, note that GoPro timestamp synchronization requires cross-correlation between gyroscope and tracker angular velocities, and gripper width extraction relies on ArUco marker detection—these two points are critical bottlenecks for data quality.
