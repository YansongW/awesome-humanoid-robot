Summary

The use of deep reinforcement learning allows for high-dimensional state descriptors, but little is known about how the choice of action representation impacts the learning difficulty and the resulting performance. Institutions per source list: Xue Bin Peng、Michiel van de Panne.

  • arXiv:1611.01055 Learning Locomotion Skills Using DeepRL: Does the Choice of Action Space Matter?

Overview

This paper systematically compares the effects of four action space parameterizations in deep reinforcement learning (DeepRL)—torque, target joint angular velocity, target joint angle, and muscle activation—on motion imitation learning, covering three characters: a biped, a velociraptor, and a dog. The core contribution lies in revealing the significant impact of action representation on learning speed, final performance, robustness, and policy query rate, and in proposing a heuristic method that alternately optimizes policy parameters and actuator parameters, enabling neural networks to effectively control complex musculoskeletal models.

What It Changes

The value of this work lies not in proposing a new reinforcement learning algorithm, but in elevating "action space design"—a problem often treated as a hyperparameter in engineering practice—to the level of a core factor determining learning success or failure. Previous DeepRL research has mostly focused on network architecture, reward shaping, or algorithmic improvements, while the choice of action representation has often been implicitly treated as a "detail." This paper uses systematic controlled experiments to demonstrate the dominant influence of this "detail" on learning difficulty and final policy quality—especially on complex characters (dog, velociraptor) and high-dimensional muscle models, where the choice of action representation can matter more than the algorithm itself.

What it truly changes is our understanding of "where intelligence comes from": by comparing muscle activation (MTU) with simplified action abstractions (PD), the authors empirically demonstrate that biomechanical structure itself (passive elasticity, damping) bears part of the control computation—that is, "embodied intelligence" or "mechanical intelligence" is quantifiable and exploitable within the DeepRL framework. This opens a new direction for subsequent research: rather than only optimizing the policy network, one can also optimize "body" parameters (actuator characteristics), allowing control and mechanics to co-evolve.

Method Breakdown

Task Framework

  • Standard actor-critic structure, with the policy being a Gaussian distribution π_θ(s,a) = N(μ_θ(s), Σ), where Σ is a fixed, manually specified diagonal covariance.
  • Objective: maximize expected cumulative reward J(π_θ) = E[Σ γ^t r_t], with γ=0.9.
  • State features s_t = (Φ(q_t, q̇_t), Φ(q_t, q̇_t)), i.e., current pose features plus reference pose features, where Φ includes root height, relative link positions, and center-of-mass velocity.

Four Action Models

  • PD (target joint angle): The action specifies a target angle q̂, with torque τ = k_p(q̂ − q) + k_d(q̇̂ − q̇), where q̇̂=0 and k_p, k_d are manually set.
  • Vel (target joint velocity): The action specifies a target velocity q̇̂, with torque τ = k_d(q̇̂ − q̇), where k_d is the same as in PD.
  • Tor (torque): The action directly specifies joint torque, subject to manually set torque limits, requiring no additional actuator parameters.
  • MTU (muscle activation): The action specifies tendon unit activation a_MTU ∈ [0,1], with force computed as F_MTU = F_SE = F_CE + F_PE, where F_CE = a_MTU · F_0 · f_l(l_CE) · f_v(v_CE). Muscles appear in antagonistic pairs, and the action dimension is at least twice that of the other models.

Key Training Techniques

  • Positive temporal difference (PTD) update: Advantage function A(s,a) = I[δ > 0], where δ = r + γV(s') − V(s). The actor is updated only when the TD error is positive, avoiding negative TD updates that push the policy mean toward unknown, poor actions.
  • Bounded action space handling: A modified inverting gradients method is used—when the policy mean μ^i(s) exceeds the bounds [l^i, u^i] and the gradient direction points outside the bounds, the gradient is replaced with l^i − μ^i(s) or u^i − μ^i(s); within the bounds, the original gradient is retained.
  • Exploration annealing: During training, λ ~ Ber(ε), with ε annealed linearly from 1 to 0.2 (over 500k iterations); only tuples with λ=1 are used for actor updates, while the critic can use all tuples.
  • Actuator parameter optimization (Algorithm 2): Alternates between policy learning (Algorithm 1, 250k iterations) and CMA optimization of actuator parameters ψ (250 generations, 16 samples per generation, each sample evaluated via 16 10-second rollouts), for a total of 6 rounds, approximately 50 hours.

Reward Function

r = 0.5·r_pose + 0.05·r_vel + 0.15·r_end + 0.1·r_root + 0.2·r_com, where each component is an exponential reward penalizing deviations in pose, velocity, end-effector, root, and center of mass.

Key Innovations

  1. First systematic quantification of the impact of action space choice on DeepRL motion learning: Under a unified framework (same network structure, reward, and algorithm), four action representations are compared across three characters and seven motions, providing reproducible benchmark data. Previous studies were mostly limited to a single action space and could not answer the fundamental engineering question of "which representation is better and why."

  2. Proposal of a policy-actuator alternating optimization method: Actuator parameters (muscle maximum isometric force, tendon rest length, moment arms, etc.) are incorporated into the optimization loop, analogous to the co-adaptation of "body" and "control" in biological evolution. This is an engineering implementation of the "embodied intelligence" concept—treating the body not as a fixed constraint but as optimizable free parameters, enabling neural networks to effectively control high-dimensional muscle models.

  3. Combination of PTD updates and bounded gradient correction: PTD avoids the problem of Gaussian policy mean drift caused by negative TD updates, while bounded gradient correction is more conservative than the inverting gradients of Hausknecht & Stone (2015)—it only alters the gradient when bounds are violated, retaining the original gradient within bounds, thereby reducing information loss. Together, they enable stable convergence across multiple action spaces.

Key Findings

  • PD is the most stable: It has the fastest learning speed (highest AUC) in 5 of 7 motions, and its final performance is comparably best across all motions.
  • Tor performs worst: It is acceptable on simple bipeds but degrades significantly on dogs and velociraptors (e.g., Raptor Run NCR is only 0.6141).
  • MTU learns slowest but has high potential: Its AUC is generally the lowest (e.g., Biped Run at only 0.5360), but after optimizing actuator parameters, it achieves the best NCR on Biped Walk (0.9584) and produces more natural motion.
  • Robustness: PD is the most robust to external force perturbations across all motions; Tor is notably fragile in bipedal walking; MTU is the least robust in velociraptor running. In irregular terrain tests, the dog's Tor policy was unexpectedly robust due to "unnatural jitter" (the authors suspect this behavior circumvented fall detection).
  • Query rate: PD and Vel perform better at low query rates (15Hz), while Tor degrades fastest; MTU performs better at low query rates and worse at high query rates.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • 2D planar simulation only: All conclusions are based on planar articulated characters; 3D scenarios are not validated, and differences between torque control and muscle models may be even larger in 3D.
  • MTU parameter optimization may not be optimal: The authors acknowledge that the actuator parameters obtained via CMA optimization "may still not be task-optimal," so comparisons between MTU and other models may underestimate its potential.
  • Optimization for a single motion: Actuator parameters are optimized for specific motions and do not consider multi-task adaptability, whereas nature expects a broader range of motor abilities.
  • Hyperparameter sensitivity: Hyperparameters such as exploration rate, covariance Σ, and reward weights significantly affect results. The authors believe the overall trends may generalize but cannot provide definitive conclusions.
  • What was not done: Other RL algorithms (e.g., PPO, SAC) or motor control tasks were not tested; no co-design algorithm for actuator mechanics and control capability was developed.

Engineering Implications

  • Prefer PD or Vel action spaces: If your goal is to quickly obtain a stable policy, PD (target joint angle) is the safest choice—fast learning, good robustness, and insensitive to query rate. Vel is a lightweight alternative to PD with comparable performance on simple tasks.
  • Avoid direct torque control: Unless the task is extremely simple (e.g., 2D biped), Tor policies degrade severely on complex characters and are highly fragile to perturbations. If it must be used, pair it with more conservative exploration strategies and longer training times.
  • MTU requires actuator optimization: Directly using muscle models is almost always the slowest to learn, but optimizing actuator parameters can yield more natural motion. When reproducing, note that MTU optimization takes approximately 50 hours (6 rounds of CMA), and each round requires retraining the policy, incurring high computational cost.
  • Verify key hyperparameters: PTD updates (updating the actor only on positive TD errors) and exploration annealing (ε from 1 to 0.2) are critical for stable training; do not modify them arbitrarily. Bounded gradient correction must be consistent with the definition of action bounds; otherwise, the policy mean may get stuck at the boundary.
  • Query rate selection: If the control frequency is limited (e.g., embedded systems), prefer PD or Vel; if using MTU, lower query rates may actually be better, but the reason is not yet clear and requires your own validation.
  • Most common pitfalls: The action dimension of MTU is more than twice that of other models (e.g., Dog MTU has 44 action dimensions vs. 20 for PD), and the state dimension is also higher (214 vs. 170); the network output layer and covariance Σ must be adjusted accordingly. Actuator parameters (Table 1) number 114–282, making manual setting nearly impossible; CMA optimization is mandatory.
