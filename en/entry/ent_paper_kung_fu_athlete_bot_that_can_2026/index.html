Summary

Current humanoid motion tracking systems can execute routine and moderately dynamic behaviors, yet significant gaps remain near hardware performance limits and algorithmic robustness boundaries. Institutions per source list: 北京理工大学（BIT）、启元实验室（QIYUAN Lab）.

Overview

This paper introduces the KungFuAthlete dataset and a unified training paradigm that enables the Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform highly dynamic martial arts movements (jumps, flips, rapid turns) in Isaac Gym and autonomously recover from arbitrary fall postures. The core contribution lies in integrating high-dynamic motion tracking and fall recovery into a single policy, significantly surpassing existing baselines with an 89.6% success rate and 96.4% fall recovery rate through mechanisms such as Gravity-based Random Fall State Initialization (GRSI) and Low Kinetic Energy Sampling (LKES).

What It Changes

The real change in this work is breaking the "either-or" dilemma between motion tracking and fall recovery. Previously, pure tracking methods like BeyondMimic failed all 0/6 trials in undisturbed single-leg standing tasks, while attempts to integrate recovery tasks such as BFM-Zero and SafeFall either sacrificed tracking accuracy (directional error soaring from 12.55 to 40.39) or relied on dual-policy switching that increased system complexity. The authors demonstrate a key fact: the directional error degradation caused by recovery tasks is not an irreversible cost but can be compensated through carefully designed reward shaping (foot slip penalty, CoM alignment) and data initialization strategies (GRSI), ultimately achieving both a higher success rate than pure tracking methods (89.6% vs 71.5%) and a near-perfect recovery rate (96.4%) within a single policy. This shifts the field's perception of the feasibility of "unified policies"—the problem lies not in the architecture but in data distribution and reward shaping.

Method Breakdown

Training Framework

  • Algorithm: FastSAC (maximum entropy off-policy RL), with actor/critic both being 3-layer MLPs (512 hidden units, ELU activation)
  • Parallel environments: 4096; replay buffer 5×10⁶; learning rate 3×10⁻⁴; γ=0.99; initial entropy temperature 0.1 with adaptive adjustment
  • Training duration: approximately 2 days on a single RTX 4090, 100,000 iterations

Unified Policy Design

  • Task mixing: execute motion tracking with probability p=0.8, and fall recovery with probability 1-p=0.2
  • Recovery trigger threshold τ=0.3m (robot fall detection)
  • Recovery rewards (Table 3):
  • Shoulder relative height penalty (weight -2.0): penalizes z-axis deviation, guiding upright posture
  • XY root movement penalty (weight -1.0): limits horizontal displacement when shoulder height deviates significantly, encouraging in-place recovery
  • Action rate penalty (weight -2.0): penalizes joint abrupt changes when shoulder height deviates, promoting smooth recovery

Gravity-based Random Fall State Initialization (GRSI)

  • The robot is initialized in zero-torque mode and released under gravity with random contact friction for 0.5–2.0 seconds, naturally generating diverse fall configurations (p, r, θ)
  • The dataset is augmented by randomly recombining rotation components (r, θ), preserving local contact structure while introducing different global orientations to enhance generalization

Data Post-processing

  • Root height drift correction algorithm: corrects cumulative root height errors in reference motions
  • Savitzky–Golay smoothing: reduces high-frequency noise in motion sequences, improving trackability

Key Ablation Designs

  • Foot slip penalty (weight 3 or 5): detects slipping when z-axis contact force > 8, penalizing lateral foot velocity
  • Key joint (knee, ankle) action rate penalty + CoM alignment reward (inspired by HuB): eliminates conservative behaviors such as low foot lifting and foot dragging

Key Innovations

  1. KungFuAthlete Dataset: The first dataset of highly dynamic martial arts motions extracted from daily training videos of professional athletes (national-level masters), containing ground and jump subsets, covering balance-challenging patterns such as single-leg standing, punching, and cross-leg stepping. Its value lies in the combination of "long-duration + high-dynamic + balance-challenging" motions, far exceeding the complexity of existing datasets (e.g., Humanoid dataset).

  2. GRSI Data Initialization: Generates fall postures through physical simulation (gravity release) rather than manual design or random sampling, ensuring configurations conform to real dynamic constraints. Rotation component recombination further enhances global orientation diversity, which is key to recovery policy generalization—experiments show that removing GRSI reduces FRR from 96.4% to 90.2%.

  3. Reward Shaping in Unified Policy: Demonstrates that tracking degradation caused by recovery tasks (E_mpboe rising from 12.55 to 40.39) can be reduced to 19.93 with foot slip penalty (weight 5) while maintaining a 6/6 success rate. This finding provides a reproducible recipe for subsequent work that "recovery tasks need not sacrifice accuracy."

Experiments and Results

The experiments validate the method's effectiveness through a combination of comparisons and ablations. For comparison settings, BeyondMimic serves as the baseline, evaluated on the most challenging motion sequences from the self-built KungFu Athletes dataset (totaling 1307 frames, including multiple single-leg standing and punching motions); each method runs 6 independent trials with average performance reported, with directional error and smoothness computed only up to the failure point to ensure fairness. The ablation path is: baseline → adding recovery task → adding foot slip penalty on top of the recovery task (weights 3 and 5). Simulation training is conducted in Isaac Sim 5.0, with evaluation transferred to MuJoCo to reduce simulator bias; real-world deployment uses the Unitree G1 humanoid robot (29 degrees of freedom, approximately 1.3 meters tall). Training uses the FastSAC algorithm, with the Actor being a three-layer fully connected network and the Critic being two four-layer networks. Among recovery-related rewards, the shoulder height z-axis tracking threshold is 1.0, and the foot slip penalty's contact force z-axis threshold is 8 (exceeding this value penalizes lateral foot velocity). Failure criteria are: any joint position tracking error exceeding 0.5 m, directional error exceeding 0.8 rad, or the robot falling.

Implication One: The recovery task significantly enhances balance robustness but at the cost of tracking accuracy. The BeyondMimic baseline cannot maintain single-leg standing even without external disturbances, falling in all 6 trials (Succ. 0/6); after adding the recovery task, the success rate improves to 6/6, with all trials completed without falling, indicating that the recovery task fundamentally solves the balance maintenance problem in highly dynamic motions. However, directional error increases from 12.55 to 40.39 (calculated from table values 12.55→40.39, an increase of approximately 2.2 times), and smoothness deteriorates from 14.2 to 18.6 (calculated from table values 14.2→18.6), showing that the recovery policy sacrifices motion tracking precision and smoothness while ensuring no falls.

Implication Two: The foot slip penalty provides an effective trade-off between accuracy and smoothness. After introducing the foot slip penalty on top of the recovery task, directional error continuously decreases with increasing weight: it drops to 25.27 at weight 3 and further to 19.93 at weight 5 (calculated from table values 40.39→25.27→19.93, a cumulative reduction of approximately 51%). Regarding smoothness, it slightly worsens at weight 3 (18.6→19.2) but improves to 18.0 at weight 5, even better than the model with only the recovery task. This indicates that an appropriately strong foot slip penalty guides the policy to learn cleaner leg-lifting motions while suppressing conservative behaviors like foot dragging, thereby improving both tracking accuracy and motion smoothness.

Implication Three: The behavioral quality of the recovery policy can be explicitly shaped through regularization. Qualitative observations show that without additional regularization, the recovery policy tends toward conservative behaviors (e.g., low foot lifting, ground scraping) to maintain stability; after introducing key joint (knee, ankle) action rate penalties combined with the foot slip penalty, the robot learns clean single-step leg-lifting motions without foot dragging. Additionally, the absence of the CoM alignment reward (inspired by HuB work) causes the CoM to deviate from the support foot during single-leg standing, indicating that this reward is crucial for maintaining static stability in balanced postures. Overall, the unified policy integrates motion tracking and fall recovery into a single policy, enabling the robot to recover from arbitrary initial states to reference motions, reducing reliance on human intervention.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Dataset domain limitation: Covers only martial arts motions; generalization to other highly dynamic domains such as sports and dance is not validated
  • Fall height limitation: Recovery only addresses standing or low-height falls, not high-altitude falls or complex fall scenarios
  • Reference data dependency: Method performance is constrained by reference motion data accuracy; data errors propagate to the robot
  • Training cost: Approximately 2 days of training (single RTX 4090), potentially limiting large-scale expansion
  • Sim-to-real gap: Only evaluated in Isaac Gym; real-world deployment faces unvalidated challenges such as sensor noise, actuator latency, and hardware limitations
  • Missing safe falls: Does not address impact minimization during falls, which is critical for real-world deployment
  • Not specified in the paper: Quantitative real-robot results, specific values for τ_pos/τ_ori/τ_body/τ_bad/ε, inference frequency, and domain randomization parameter ranges

Engineering Insights

  • Priority verification for reproduction: Training platform (Isaac Gym), algorithm (FastSAC), network architecture (3-layer MLP with 512 units), and hyperparameters (batch 4096, lr 3e-4, γ 0.99) must be strictly aligned; the recovery reward's shoulder height threshold of 1.0 and foot slip contact force threshold of 8 are key sensitivity points in ablation experiments
  • Most common pitfalls: The mixing probability between recovery and tracking tasks (p=0.8/0.2) requires careful tuning—too high p leads to insufficient recovery capability, while too low p degrades tracking accuracy; increasing the foot slip penalty weight from 3 to 5 reduces E_mpboe from 25.27 to 19.93, but further increases may introduce overly conservative behaviors
  • Guidance for downstream teams: To reproduce high-dynamic tracking, LKES and SG post-processing are essential (removing them reduces SR by 7.3% and 2.5%, respectively); for recovery generalization, GRSI's rotation recombination augmentation cannot be omitted (FRR drops by 6.2%); evaluation should use MuJoCo as a second simulator to reduce simulator bias and report pre-failure metrics for fair comparison
  • Hardware requirements: Training can be completed on a single RTX 4090, but 4096 parallel environments require confirming sufficient GPU memory; safe fall strategies must be additionally validated before real-world deployment, as the current method does not cover impact minimization
