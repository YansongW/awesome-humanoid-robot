Summary

A control strategy that simultaneously regulates force along constrained directions and position along free directions in contact tasks.

Overview

Force/Position Hybrid Control is an operational strategy that orthogonally decomposes the task space by direction, applying position control and force control separately. It uses a selection matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) to partition the task space into a position-controlled subspace and a force-controlled subspace, controlling contact force in constrained directions (e.g., the normal direction perpendicular to a table surface) and position in free directions (e.g., the tangential direction along the table surface). What it truly changes is not the naive desire to "control force and position simultaneously," but rather explicitly encoding the task semantics of "which direction should control what" into the control law, enabling robots to balance geometric precision and interaction safety in contact-rich manipulation.

Content

What It Is: One Direction, One Control Objective

Force/Position Hybrid Control addresses a fundamental asymmetry in manipulation tasks: different directions have inherently different control objectives. When pushing an object along a table, the horizontal direction requires precise tracking of the desired position (otherwise the object deviates from the target trajectory), while the vertical direction requires maintaining the desired contact force (otherwise either the table gets crushed or contact is lost). If position control is applied to all directions, contact forces become uncontrollable due to uncertainty in environmental stiffness; if force control is applied to all directions, positional accuracy in free directions cannot be guaranteed.

The mathematical core of this method is the selection matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), a diagonal 0/1 matrix:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_m), \quad \sigma_i \in \{0,1\} \]
  • If \(\sigma_i = 1\), the \(i\)-th direction is position-controlled.
  • If \(\sigma_i = 0\), the \(i\)-th direction is force-controlled.

Thus, the task space is orthogonally decomposed into two complementary subspaces: the position-controlled subspace (the image space of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)) and the force-controlled subspace (the image space of \(\mathbf{I} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)). The control law is:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{cmd}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \, \mathbf{K}_p (\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}) + (\mathbf{I} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) \, \mathbf{K}_f (\mathbf{F}_d - \mathbf{F}) \]

where \(\mathbf{x}_d - \mathbf{x}\) is the position error, \(\mathbf{F}_d - \mathbf{F}\) is the force error, and \(\mathbf{K}_p\) and \(\mathbf{K}_f\) are the proportional gains for position and force, respectively. Finally, the task-space force is mapped to joint torques via \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{F}_{\text{cmd}}\).

Why It Exists: Inherent Deficiencies of Single Control Modes

Before Force/Position Hybrid Control, robot manipulation faced a dilemma. Pure position control (e.g., the most common PTP/CP modes in industrial robots) is extremely fragile in contact tasks: even a few millimeters of environmental position error can cause contact forces to surge to destructive levels due to high environmental stiffness. Pure force control, conversely, is nearly unusable in free space—force error signals are meaningless without contact, and position drift becomes inevitable.

The historical role of Force/Position Hybrid Control is precisely to provide the first systematic control framework for tasks with both geometric and force constraints. It does not attempt to approximate both needs with a single unified objective; instead, it acknowledges the anisotropy of the task space and explicitly declares the control mode for each direction via the selection matrix. This idea later profoundly influenced the design of impedance control and admittance control—although the latter two do not explicitly decompose directions, they are essentially dynamic compromises between force and position.

Principle Breakdown

① Selection Matrix: Mathematical Encoding of Task Semantics

The physical meaning of the selection matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is "constraint direction detection." In contact tasks, environmental geometry imposes natural constraints on motion: the direction perpendicular to a table is "locked" by the table, allowing the robot to exert force but not move freely; the direction parallel to the table is unconstrained, allowing free motion but no stable force exertion. The core insight of Force/Position Hybrid Control is: constrained directions should control force, free directions should control position. \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is the mathematical expression of this semantics.

② Orthogonal Decomposition: Avoiding Cross-Channel Coupling

Position control and force control are assigned to orthogonal subspaces, meaning position errors do not produce control actions in the force channel, and force errors do not produce control actions in the position channel. This decoupling is what distinguishes Force/Position Hybrid Control from "weighted summation" style compliant control—the latter is essentially still a single control objective that blends position and force errors with weights, and cannot truly achieve "strict position control in one direction and strict force control in another."

③ Joint Torque Mapping: From Task Space to Joint Space

The \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{cmd}}\) output by the control law is a task-space generalized force, mapped to joint torques via the Jacobian transpose:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{J}^T \mathbf{F}_{\text{cmd}} \]

This mapping assumes a non-redundant, non-singular robot configuration. On humanoid robots, due to the floating base and whole-body coordination requirements, Force/Position Hybrid Control is typically embedded within a Whole-Body Control (WBC) framework, solved jointly with kinematic constraints and dynamic optimization.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Parameter Meaning Typical Value/Description
\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) Selection matrix, diagonal 0/1 1 indicates position channel, 0 indicates force channel; must be preset according to task directions
\(\mathbf{K}_p\) Position proportional gain Determines stiffness of the position channel; must be lower than environmental stiffness to avoid impacts (engineering judgment)
\(\mathbf{K}_f\) Force proportional gain Determines convergence speed of the force channel; excessive values cause force oscillations (engineering judgment)
\(\mathbf{J}^T\) Jacobian transpose Maps task-space forces to joint torques; requires a non-singular robot configuration

Horizontal Comparison: Positioning of Three Major Force Control Frameworks

Framework Core Idea Applicable Scenarios Key Limitations
Impedance Control Represents the robot as a mass-spring-damper system, indirectly controlling force by adjusting impedance parameters Leg landing impact absorption, uncertain environment contact Force accuracy depends on environmental position estimation; large force errors when environmental stiffness is unknown
Admittance Control Outer loop generates position corrections from force errors, inner loop tracks position Stable contact force tracking with wrist force sensors Requires high-precision position inner loop; limited force loop bandwidth
Force/Position Hybrid Control Orthogonal decomposition by direction, independent position and force channels Peg-in-hole insertion, curved surface polishing, table pushing Abrupt selection matrix changes during contact transitions, causing force impacts

The engineering selection rule is: use impedance when joint torque controllability is good, use admittance when position controllability is good. Humanoid robot legs typically use impedance (to absorb landing impacts), while arms often use admittance when wrist force sensors are available (for stable contact force tracking). Force/Position Hybrid Control is suitable for scenarios with clear task direction semantics and the need to simultaneously guarantee geometric precision and force safety.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Industrial assembly is the most classic application scenario for Force/Position Hybrid Control. In peg-in-hole tasks, the axial direction requires controlling insertion force (to prevent jamming or workpiece damage), while the radial direction requires position control (to ensure alignment with the hole center). Traditional position control schemes are highly prone to jamming due to tiny position errors when the peg-hole clearance is small; Force/Position Hybrid Control significantly improves assembly success rates through axial force control and radial position control decomposition.

Humanoid robot manipulation often combines Force/Position Hybrid Control with whole-body control. For example, when a humanoid robot pushes an object on a table, the arm tracks the desired trajectory in the horizontal direction while maintaining constant contact force in the vertical direction; similarly, when wiping a table with a cloth, normal force control ensures consistent cleaning pressure, while tangential position control ensures complete path coverage.

Contact transition scenarios are the most challenging applications of Force/Position Hybrid Control. During the transition from free-space motion to contact establishment, the roles of each direction change abruptly—previously free directions suddenly become constrained, and previously position-controlled directions suddenly require force control. If the selection matrix transition is not smooth, force impacts and instability can occur. In practice, Force/Position Hybrid Control is often combined with impedance frameworks, i.e., "variable impedance + direction selection," reducing stiffness in the approach direction at the moment of contact before gradually establishing the target contact force.

Limitations and Boundaries

The most classic difficulty of Force/Position Hybrid Control is selection matrix switching during contact transitions. During the transition from free-space motion to contact establishment, the roles of each direction change abruptly, and unsmooth switching can cause force impacts. The root cause of this problem is that the selection matrix is a discrete 0/1 variable, while the contact state is a continuously evolving physical process. The gap between discrete switching and continuous physics is an inherent theoretical limitation of Force/Position Hybrid Control.

Additionally, Force/Position Hybrid Control has a strong dependence on environmental geometric models. Setting the selection matrix requires prior knowledge of which directions are constrained and which are free, demanding accurate modeling of the environment geometry. In unknown or dynamically changing environments, online estimation of the selection matrix is itself a challenging problem.

Force/Position Hybrid Control also faces the challenge of cross-channel dynamic coupling. Although the position and force channels are orthogonal in task space, they still influence each other through robot dynamics (especially inertial coupling and Coriolis forces). Under high-speed motion or large inertial loads, this coupling can degrade control performance.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Force/Position Hybrid Control means controlling force and position simultaneously"—inaccurate. It does not control both force and position in every direction; rather, it orthogonally decomposes by direction: each direction either controls force or position, and the two are mutually exclusive. True "simultaneity" only occurs across different directions.

  2. "The selection matrix can be set arbitrarily"—wrong. The selection matrix must match the task's environmental constraints: constrained directions must be set to force channels, and free directions must be set to position channels. Setting them incorrectly leads to control instability or task failure.

  3. "Force/Position Hybrid Control is more advanced than impedance control"—not true. The three force control frameworks each have their applicable scenarios. Force/Position Hybrid Control has clear advantages when direction semantics are well-defined, but impedance control offers better robustness in scenarios with frequent contact transitions and environmental uncertainty.

  4. "Force/Position Hybrid Control does not require force sensors"—wrong. The force channel requires real-time force feedback signals, typically from wrist force/torque sensors or joint torque sensors. Without force feedback, the control law in the force channel cannot be closed.
