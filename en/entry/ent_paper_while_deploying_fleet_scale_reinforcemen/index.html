Overview

This paper proposes Learning while Deploying (LWD), a fleet-level offline-to-online reinforcement learning framework for general-purpose VLA robot policies, implemented by the Agibot team on G1 dual-arm robots. The core contribution lies in transforming deployment from an evaluation endpoint into a training loop, leveraging two components—DIVL (Distributional Implicit Value Learning) and QAM (Q-learning with Adjoint Matching)—to address the two major challenges of heterogeneous fleet replay and generative action heads, consistently outperforming prior post-training baselines across 8 real-world manipulation tasks.

What It Changes

Over the past few years, VLA pretrained models have endowed robots with strong priors, but the community's default paradigm has been: freeze after pretraining or fine-tune with a small amount of expert demonstrations, with deployment serving merely as a flywheel for collecting more demonstrations. What LWD truly changes is this implicit assumption—it no longer treats deployment as an accessory to data collection, but instead treats the entire fleet as a continuously operating reinforcement learning environment. This means that failure trajectories, partial progress, and human interventions—signals that were previously discarded or used only for contrastive learning—now become supervision sources for value learning.

The deeper change lies in the object of post-training. Previous post-training systems (such as RT-X style work) primarily targeted specialized policies, with one controller per task, whereas LWD insists on training a single generalist policy covering all tasks. This is not merely an engineering simplification but a redefinition of "generality": generality is not a static property from pretraining, but a dynamic property continuously refined through heterogeneous experience during deployment. The authors explicitly abandon imitation learning as a post-training mechanism, pivoting to offline-to-online RL, which requires the value function to remain stable under sparse delayed rewards and highly heterogeneous data distributions—a difficulty previous systems deliberately avoided.

Method Breakdown

LWD's architecture is a closed loop: pretrained policy deployed to the fleet → autonomous rollouts and human interventions aggregated into a shared replay buffer → centralized learner updates the policy → periodic pushback to the fleet.

Two-Phase Training Pipeline

  • Offline phase: Uses previously collected robot data (expert demonstrations, historical rollouts, exploration data near failure modes) for offline RL initialization, providing a stable starting point for the policy and critic. The key here is avoiding the cold-start problem of online exploration from scratch.
  • Online phase: The current policy is deployed to the fleet, each robot executes real tasks and uploads trajectories to a shared online buffer; the learner trains on a mixture of the static offline buffer and the growing online buffer, with periodic policy updates pushed back.

Component One: DIVL (Distributional Implicit Value Learning)

  • Instead of regressing a single scalar value, it learns the distribution of action values in the dataset, extracting quantile statistics as TD bootstrap targets.
  • Preserves IQL's in-distribution value learning principle, avoiding overestimation from out-of-distribution maximization—especially critical for heterogeneous replay, where reward sparsity and success rates vary drastically across tasks.
  • Uses multi-step TD targets for long-horizon tasks to accelerate propagation of sparse terminal rewards.

Component Two: QAM (Q-learning with Adjoint Matching)

  • Designed for flow-matching action heads. Modern VLA policies generate actions through multi-step denoising without tractable likelihoods, making direct policy gradient unstable.
  • Uses critic gradients to guide policy extraction, reformulating the guided optimization as local regression along the flow trajectory, avoiding backpropagation through the full generative process.

Overall Decoupling

DIVL handles policy evaluation (learning values from heterogeneous replay), while QAM handles policy extraction (converting values into flow policy updates). This decoupling allows value learning to be independent of action head architecture, and policy updates to be independent of noise in value estimates.

Not specified in the paper: DIVL's quantile parameter choices, QAM's specific regression target form, the mixing ratio of offline to online data, and the learner's update frequency and pushback period.

Key Innovations

  1. Turning deployment itself into an RL training signal source: Previous data flywheels only utilized successful trajectories; LWD explicitly incorporates failures, partial progress, and human interventions into value learning. This is not simple data augmentation but a redefinition of "what constitutes a learning signal"—failure trajectories provide negative value supervision in TD learning, and human interventions provide guidance near successful states.

  2. DIVL's distributional value learning adapted to heterogeneous replay: IQL was previously used primarily with homogeneous data; LWD extends it to distributional form, using quantile statistics as bootstrap targets. This design directly addresses the core contradiction of fleet data—success rates, reward densities, and intervention frequencies vary enormously across tasks, and a single scalar value cannot capture this variability, whereas the distributional form is naturally robust.

  3. QAM solving policy extraction for generative action heads: Flow-matching action heads are the current mainstream choice for VLA, but they lack tractable likelihoods, making both maximum-likelihood-based policy extraction and direct policy gradient infeasible. QAM reformulates critic guidance as local regression along the flow trajectory, bypassing backpropagation through the full generative process—a practical solution for RL training of generative policies that is decoupled from action head architecture.

Experiments and Results

Experiments were conducted on a fleet of Agibot G1 dual-arm robots, covering 8 real-world manipulation tasks, including multiple long-horizon tasks lasting 3-5 minutes (brewing Kung Fu tea, mixing cocktails, making juice, packing shoes, grocery restocking). A single generalist policy was trained to cover all tasks.

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly include a limitations discussion section. Inferred boundaries from factual points include:

  • No comparison with specialized policies: No detailed comparison between LWD's generalist policy and per-task specialized policies is provided, making it impossible to assess the performance cost of generality.
  • No large-scale deployment validation: Only proposed as future direction, not validated in more home, store, or factory scenarios.
  • Improvement on non-long-horizon tasks not mentioned: If improvements on short tasks are limited, the framework's value may be concentrated in long-horizon scenarios.
  • Fleet size not mentioned: Impossible to determine at what fleet scale the framework remains effective, or how communication and synchronization overhead scale.

Not specified in the paper: LWD's performance on short-horizon tasks, sensitivity to human intervention frequency, and the impact of offline data quality on final performance.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing LWD, the first thing to verify is value learning stability—DIVL's quantile parameters and IQL's expectation quantile choices directly determine the noise level of TD targets, which may be the most sensitive part of the entire framework. It is recommended to first validate whether DIVL converges stably on small-scale offline data before introducing online data.

The most likely pitfall is overfitting to heterogeneous replay: the online buffer grows quickly, and if the mixing ratio is inappropriate, the value function will rapidly bias toward recent data, causing catastrophic forgetting. The authors mention "avoiding overfitting to transient online data" but do not provide specific ratios; reproduction requires self-experimentation to determine the offline/online data mix.

Practical guidance for downstream teams:

  • If you already have a pretrained VLA model and fleet deployment, LWD's value lies in converting existing deployment data (including failures) into policy improvements without redesigning the data collection pipeline.
  • QAM's local regression design means you can swap action head architectures without rewriting the value learning component—architectural decoupling is a practical engineering dividend.
  • Long-horizon tasks are LWD's advantage zone; if your business scenarios are primarily short tasks, the gains may be limited—run small-scale validation before committing full resources.
  • Contact Agibot (research@agibot.com) for more implementation details, but note that training configurations are not publicly disclosed, so reproduction costs may be high.
