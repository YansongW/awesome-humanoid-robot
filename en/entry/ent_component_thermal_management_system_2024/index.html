Overview

A Thermal Management System is the collective term for heat sinks, heat pipes, fans, and liquid cooling devices in humanoid robots that keep computing chips, power electronics, and actuators within safe operating temperature ranges. What it truly changes is not the act of "cooling" itself, but rather enabling high-power-density computing and high-dynamic actuation to coexist within a limited volume—without thermal management, compute power and torque are merely specifications on paper.

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition of Thermal Management Systems

Thermal management systems fall under the component category, within the domains of components and design engineering, spanning the midstream and upstream of the value chain. The English name is Thermal Management System.

From a physical composition standpoint, a thermal management system includes the following core components: heat sink—metal fin structures that increase contact area with air; heat pipe—a two-phase heat transfer device that efficiently conducts heat through working fluid phase change; vapor chamber—a two-dimensional extension of heat pipes that rapidly and uniformly distributes heat; liquid cooling—a cooling method that removes heat through liquid circulation; fan—a forced convection device that enhances the convective heat transfer coefficient. It also includes thermal interface materials (TIM), used to fill gaps between chips and heat sinks, with common types including thermal grease, thermal pads, phase change materials, liquid metal, and graphite sheets.

In humanoid robots, the thermal management system serves clear targets: computing chips such as CPU, GPU, and NPU, power electronics such as motor drivers, as well as actuators and battery packs. Its goal is to keep the temperatures of these heat sources within a safe window, avoiding performance throttling or thermal runaway.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The complete control loop of a humanoid robot can be summarized as: physical world → sensors → analog-to-digital conversion → preprocessing → perception and state estimation → decision and planning → control law computation → power amplification → actuators → physical world. Every stage involves computation, latency, and energy consumption. This means the computing system must complete sensor data throughput, state estimation, motion planning, and deep learning inference on millisecond or even microsecond timescales; the power system must provide kilowatt-level instantaneous power within limited volume and weight budgets while ensuring safety.

The fundamental reason thermal management systems exist is that high power density inevitably accompanies high heat density. If heat generated by CPUs, GPUs, NPUs, motor drives, and batteries cannot be dissipated in time, two severe consequences arise—performance throttling (computing capability constrained by temperature) or thermal runaway (self-accelerating exothermic reactions inside batteries causing rapid temperature rise). Lithium-ion battery thermal runaway is typically triggered by overcharging, over-discharging, short circuits, mechanical damage, or high temperatures, with the process including SEI film decomposition (approximately 80–120 °C), separator shrinkage (approximately 130 °C), cathode oxygen release and electrolyte oxidation (approximately 150–250 °C), ultimately leading to internal short circuits and violent heat release.

From a historical perspective, early robots were mostly laboratory prototypes with low power density, where natural convection sufficed for heat dissipation. As humanoid robots move toward commercial mass production, high-compute chips and high-torque-density actuators are packed into volumes comparable to the human body, transforming thermal management from an "optional accessory" into a "system-level constraint."

Principle Breakdown

① Three Fundamental Mechanisms of Heat Transfer

Heat transfer has three fundamental mechanisms: conduction, convection, and radiation. Conduction transfers heat through interactions between microscopic particles within a substance, requiring no macroscopic motion; convection is heat transfer caused by macroscopic motion of a fluid (gas or liquid); radiation transfers heat through electromagnetic waves, requiring no medium.

② Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction and Thermal Resistance Networks

Fourier's law of heat conduction states that conductive heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient. The thermal resistance chain from chip to environment includes: chip junction-to-case thermal resistance, thermal interface material (TIM) thermal resistance, heat sink thermal resistance, and convective thermal resistance. The total temperature rise can be expressed as the product of heat flow and total thermal resistance:

\[ \Delta T = P \cdot \left( R_{\text{jc}} + R_{\text{TIM}} + R_{\text{hs}} + R_{\text{conv}} \right) \]

where \(R_{\text{jc}}\) is the chip junction-to-case thermal resistance, \(R_{\text{TIM}}\) is the interface material thermal resistance, \(R_{\text{hs}}\) is the heat sink thermal resistance, and \(R_{\text{conv}}\) is the surface convective thermal resistance. The TIM thermal resistance can be further expressed as \(R_{\text{TIM}} = \text{BLT} / (k \cdot A)\), where BLT is the bond line thickness, \(k\) is the TIM thermal conductivity, and \(A\) is the contact area.

③ Phase-Change Heat Transfer in Heat Pipes and Vapor Chambers

Heat pipes have far higher heat transfer capability than solid metal, with equivalent thermal conductivity reaching 1000–10000 W/(m·K), making them suitable for transporting heat from confined spaces to remote heat sinks. Vapor chambers further expand heat sources into planar heat sources, reducing local heat flux density.

④ Dimensionless Numbers in Convective Heat Transfer

Convective heat transfer intensity is characterized by three dimensionless numbers: the Reynolds number (Re) represents the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, determining whether flow is laminar or turbulent; the Prandtl number (Pr) represents the ratio of momentum diffusion to thermal diffusion; the Nusselt number (Nu) represents the relative intensity of convective heat transfer versus pure conduction. For forced convection of air over a flat plate, the local Nusselt number in laminar flow can be approximated as:

\[ Nu_x = 0.332 \, Re_x^{1/2} \, Pr^{1/3} \]

Key Parameters and Specifications

Key parameters for thermal management systems include thermal resistance, junction temperature, case temperature, ambient temperature, temperature rise, heat flux density, thermal conductivity, and convective heat transfer coefficient. The following key values are provided in the source material:

Parameter Value Range Description
Thermal grease thermal conductivity Approximately 1–10 W/(m·K) Common TIM, requires low BLT
Thermal pad thermal conductivity Approximately 3–15 W/(m·K) Pre-formed, easy to assemble but higher thermal resistance
Liquid metal thermal conductivity Approximately 20–80 W/(m·K) Extremely high thermal conductivity but electrically conductive with corrosion risk
Heat pipe equivalent thermal conductivity On the order of 1000–10000 W/(m·K) Two-phase heat transfer, suitable for heat transport in confined spaces
SEI film decomposition temperature Approximately 80–120 °C First stage of thermal runaway
Separator shrinkage temperature Approximately 130 °C Second stage of thermal runaway
Cathode oxygen release temperature Approximately 150–250 °C Third stage of thermal runaway

When selecting components, attention must be paid to heat sink thermal resistance, fin surface area, base plate area, and surface convective thermal resistance. Increasing the number or height of fins increases surface area, but simultaneously reduces flow velocity, lowers the convective heat transfer coefficient \(h\), and reduces fin efficiency, so an optimal geometric design exists.

Horizontal Comparison

Selecting a thermal management solution is essentially a trade-off among heat dissipation capability, volume and weight, cost, and reliability:

Solution Heat Dissipation Capability Volume and Weight Cost Applicable Scenarios
Natural convection heat sink Low Medium Low Low-power chips, prototype validation
Forced air cooling (fan + fins) Medium Medium Medium Medium-power computing platforms
Heat pipe / vapor chamber High (equivalent thermal conductivity 1000–10000 W/(m·K)) Small Medium Heat transport in confined spaces
Liquid cooling Highest Large (includes pump and tubing) High High-power-density computing and actuators

Engineering judgment: humanoid robots typically employ hybrid solutions—air cooling or liquid cooling for computing chips, thermally conductive structural components plus natural convection for actuators, and heat pipes plus phase change materials for batteries. Liquid cooling offers the strongest heat dissipation capability, but the volume, weight, and reliability issues of pumps and tubing require careful evaluation for full-system deployment.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Thermal management systems have been widely applied in prototype validation, academic research, and early commercial products of humanoid robots. Taking Tesla Optimus as an example, its Gen 3 builds on the Gen 2's 173 cm torso, increasing single-hand degrees of freedom from 11 DOF to 22 DOF, with 25 actuators per hand and 50 actuators across both hands. Such high-density actuator integration inevitably accompanies high heat density, imposing stringent requirements on thermal management.

On the computing side, high-compute platforms represented by Jetson AGX Orin are the primary targets of thermal management systems. Its Roofline ridge point is approximately 1–3 FLOPs/Byte, with high compute density when running convolutional neural networks, and chip power consumption can reach tens of watts, necessitating active cooling solutions.

Limitations and Boundaries

Thermal management systems have clear physical boundaries. First, heat dissipation capability is constrained by ambient temperature—the higher the ambient temperature, the smaller the temperature rise margin at the same thermal resistance. Second, passive cooling (natural convection + radiation) has limited capability, and high-power-density scenarios must rely on active cooling (fans or liquid cooling), but active cooling introduces noise, vibration, power consumption, and reliability issues. Third, heat pipes and vapor chambers have capillary limits and boiling limits, beyond which heat transfer performance deteriorates sharply. Fourth, liquid metal TIM, despite its extremely high thermal conductivity (approximately 20–80 W/(m·K)), is electrically conductive and poses corrosion risks, requiring strict sealing in consumer-grade products.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A thermal management system is just adding a fan"—Wrong. A fan is merely a forced convection device; what truly determines heat dissipation capability is the complete thermal resistance chain from chip to environment: junction-to-case resistance, TIM resistance, heat sink resistance, and convective resistance, any of which can be the bottleneck.
  2. "Higher TIM thermal conductivity is always better"—Not entirely true. The actual thermal resistance of a TIM also depends on bond line thickness (BLT) and contact area. A material with high thermal conductivity but large BLT may have higher actual thermal resistance than a material with lower thermal conductivity but small BLT.
  3. "Heat pipes have high equivalent thermal conductivity, so they can replace heat sinks"—Wrong. Heat pipes merely transport heat from one location to another; ultimately, a heat sink plus convection is still required to dissipate heat into the environment. Heat pipes solve "how heat gets out," while heat sinks solve "how heat is dissipated."
  4. "Battery thermal management only needs to prevent overheating"—Incomplete. Battery thermal management must prevent both overheating (thermal runaway) and overcooling (increased internal resistance and capacity degradation at low temperatures), and must also use balancing to equalize voltage/capacity across series-connected cells, preventing individual cells from overcharging or over-discharging.
