Summary

Performing agile navigation with four-legged robots is a challenging task due to the highly dynamic motions, contacts with various parts of the robot, and the limited field of view of the perception sensors. Institutions per source list: ETH Zurich（宇树团队）.

Overview

This paper presents a fully learned agile navigation pipeline for quadruped robots, consisting of three modules: perception, locomotion, and navigation. Trained in simulation and deployed directly on a real ANYmal D robot, it achieves parkour maneuvers such as crossing gaps, climbing high platforms, and crouching through low passages. The core contribution lies in decoupling high-level scene understanding from low-level motor skills via hierarchical reinforcement learning, and addressing the perception-decision-control coupling challenge in end-to-end training through multi-resolution voxel perception and a hybrid policy distribution.

What It Changes

Before this work, agile locomotion for quadrupeds either relied on strong model assumptions like MPC (which is fragile against slipping and non-foot contacts), sampling-based planning (requiring prior maps, computationally expensive, and producing only statically stable motions), or expert demonstrations and motion capture systems. What this paper truly changes is: for the first time, the entire "perception-navigation-locomotion" pipeline is handed over to learning, without any expert data, offline computation, or environmental priors, enabling the robot to autonomously decide and execute parkour maneuvers in dynamic, partially observable, high-impact scenarios.

The second key change is in how "contact" is handled. Previous methods either explicitly planned foot contact sequences or hardcoded jump controllers. This work completely abandons explicit contact modeling, allowing low-level skills to naturally emerge non-foot contact behaviors such as knee support and belly friction through reinforcement learning—something nearly impossible under the MPC framework. Meanwhile, it transforms high-level navigation from "geometric path planning" into "skill selection + command generation," letting the planner directly understand the capability boundaries of each motor skill rather than relying on simplified kinematic models.

Method Breakdown

Overall Architecture

The pipeline consists of three modules running asynchronously/synchronously at different frequencies on the onboard computer: - Perception module (asynchronous, NVIDIA Jetson Orin): receives point clouds from 6 Realsense depth cameras + Velodyne LiDAR, outputs a compact latent tensor \( l \) and a 2.5D elevation map. - Locomotion module (50 Hz): contains 5 skill policies (walk, climb up, climb down, jump, crouch), taking proprioception + local elevation map + intermediate commands as input, and outputting joint position commands \( q^* \). - Navigation module (5 Hz): receives latent tensor \( l \), goal position, and proprioceptive state, outputting skill index \( s \) and local commands \( (r^*, t^*, \psi^*) \).

Perception Module

  • Multi-resolution encoder-decoder: A coarse network (voxel 12.5 cm, map 4 m × 4 m) handles large-scale scene understanding with autoregressive feedback (transforming the previous frame's output into the current frame and concatenating) to accumulate evidence under occlusion; a fine network (voxel 6.25 cm, map 2 m × 2 m) handles high-precision reconstruction near the robot, taking the coarse network's last-layer features + raw point cloud as input.
  • Unsupervised training: 2000 trajectories × 100 time steps, trained with binary cross-entropy for occupancy probability and Euclidean distance for centroid positions. Data augmentation includes point perturbation, random blobs, point-block removal, and pose noise.
  • Key design: Uses dense 3D convolutions rather than sparse implementations (sparse scales poorly in 4096 parallel RL), requiring approximately 45 GB GPU memory; no skip connections, forcing the latent tensor to carry more abstract scene information.

Locomotion Module

  • Each skill is trained separately with PPO, sharing an observation space (base velocity \( v_b \), angular velocity \( \omega_b \), gravity \( g_b \), joint states \( q, \dot{q} \), goal position \( r^* \), time \( t^* \), heading \( \psi^* \), 2.5D elevation map \( h \)), with joint position commands as the action space.
  • Symmetry augmentation: Leverages ANYmal D's front-back/left-right symmetry by replicating each transition with all symmetric variants; to address poor convergence for mirrored states, the original action probability is set to the probability of all symmetric variants (guided initialization).
  • Skill-specific designs:
  • Climb up/down: uses height curriculum (max 1 m); climb-down adds high-impact termination conditions (preventing learned jumping-down behavior that could damage the robot); climb-up reduces knee/base contact penalties, naturally emerging "knee-first-then-foot" behavior.
  • Jump: max gap 1 m, using gap-size curriculum.
  • Crouch: minimum passage height 0.4 m, using height curriculum.
  • Walk: mixed terrain (60% stairs, 20% slopes, 20% random obstacles), capable of 40° slopes, 0.25 m steps, and 2 m/s on flat ground.
  • Elevation map perturbation during training (single-point noise + map translation up to 7.5 cm) enhances robustness.

Navigation Module

  • Hierarchical reinforcement learning: Outer 5 Hz navigation policy, inner 50 Hz locomotion policy (frozen). Navigation observations are \( (v_b, g_b, r^*_G, t^*_G, l) \), with a hybrid action space—a Gaussian distribution outputs continuous commands \( (r^*, t^*, \psi^*) \), and a categorical distribution selects skill \( s \).
  • Sparse reward: Only at the final time step of an episode, penalizing goal distance (\( \|r - r^*_G\| < 0.4 \) counts as success), allowing the policy to freely explore paths.
  • Curriculum learning: Goals move progressively from near to far; otherwise, the robot gets stuck in front of larger obstacles.
  • Key decision: Rolling out the actual low-level policy during training (rather than a simplified kinematic model), enabling the navigation policy to understand each skill's patterns and capability boundaries.

Key Innovations

  1. Fully learned agile navigation pipeline: No expert demonstrations, no pre-built maps, no motion capture—the entire pipeline from raw sensor data to joint commands is differentiable (or RL-trainable), a first in quadruped parkour.
  2. Multi-resolution voxel perception + autoregressive feedback: The coarse network provides large-scale scene priors, the fine network provides near-field precision, and the autoregressive mechanism lets the robot "remember" scene structure after occlusions disappear—a key innovation for handling partial observability.
  3. Hybrid distribution policy output: Unifies continuous commands (Gaussian) and discrete skill selection (categorical) in a single actor, letting the high-level policy directly control "when to switch skills" rather than indirectly inducing it through reward functions—more direct and stable than traditional hierarchical RL.

Real Robot

  • Maximum speed 2 m/s; HFE motors deflected over 160° in trajectory A; in trajectory B, motors saturate during climbing to push the robot onto a 0.9 m platform.
  • In scenario A, the robot reaches the leftmost box (0.8 m wide, smaller than a standing footprint) after stairs at 1.5 m/s; jump skill was trained with boxes twice the size.
  • The perception module hallucinates stairs in blind spots and inflates table shapes, but the navigation/locomotion modules are unaffected, and the network quickly corrects once better views are obtained.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Scalability unverified: Only tested in structured scenes with pallet-sized boxes; extending to collapsed buildings or real parkour courses requires more obstacle types and skills.
  • Strong module coupling: The navigation module can only receive latent tensors from the specific perception module and must use the same locomotion policy; modifying any module requires retraining the others. The entire pipeline has 8 neural networks, making tuning costly.
  • Perception blind-spot issue: A blind spot exists directly beneath the robot (due to camera placement), causing table shape inflation during crouching and hallucinated stairs during climbing—though not affecting current tasks, it could become a bottleneck in more complex scenarios.
  • No long-term stability testing: Real-world deployment only demonstrates short-duration tasks; drift accumulation or hardware wear over extended operation is not addressed.
  • Training details undisclosed: Learning rates, batch sizes, training steps, and full reward function designs are not reported, making reproduction difficult.

Engineering Insights

  • Check latency budgets first: Point cloud publishing latency on the real robot reaches 250 ms; this paper solves it by directly subscribing to depth images (reducing to 25 ms)—any reproduction project should prioritize optimizing perception pipeline latency over algorithmic improvements.
  • Pitfall of symmetry augmentation: Directly mirroring states leads to poor convergence; the original action probability must be set to the probability of all symmetric variants (guided initialization), otherwise the policy falls into asymmetric behaviors (e.g., only climbing forward).
  • Elevation map perturbation is critical: Adding noise + translation up to 7.5 cm to the 2.5D map during training is a major source of real-world robustness for locomotion policies and cannot be omitted.
  • Curriculum design determines success: Without a curriculum, the navigation module gets stuck in front of larger obstacles; climb-up/down skills require height curricula, and climb-down additionally needs high-impact termination conditions (otherwise the robot learns to jump down—feasible in simulation but damaging to the real robot).
  • Hardware requirements: The perception module's dense 3D convolutions require approximately 45 GB GPU memory, necessitating multi-GPU or large-memory setups for training; at inference, the perception module runs asynchronously on Jetson Orin, separated from the locomotion/navigation modules, requiring attention to message synchronization mechanisms.
