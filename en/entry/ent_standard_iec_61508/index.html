Overview

IEC 61508 is the fundamental functional safety standard for electrical/electronic/programmable electronic safety-related systems published by the International Electrotechnical Commission. It defines Safety Integrity Levels (SIL) from SIL1 to SIL4, quantifying the ability of a safety function to correctly execute under fault conditions using the probability of dangerous failure. What it truly changes is not "how to make systems more reliable," but rather a fundamental shift in safety philosophy: "when the system fails, how do we ensure it still enters a safe state?"

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

IEC 61508 (Functional safety of electrical/electronic/programmable electronic safety-related systems) is the foundational generic standard in functional safety, published by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), with the second edition released in 2010. It addresses safety functions performed by electrical, electronic, and programmable electronic (E/E/PE) systems, providing a complete engineering framework from hazard analysis, safety requirements allocation, design implementation, to verification and validation.

In the context of humanoid robots, the core question IEC 61508 answers is: When the main control chip crashes, neural network inference produces errors, or sensor signals become abnormal, can the safety-related control system bring the robot into a safe state? It does not care whether the robot "can walk"; it only cares whether the robot "must be able to stop when it should."

Compared to ISO 13849, IEC 61508 has a broader scope—the latter primarily targets machinery safety-related control systems, while IEC 61508 covers all E/E/PE safety-related systems. The PL levels of ISO 13849 (PL a to PL e) and the SIL levels of IEC 61508 (SIL1 to SIL4) are often cross-referenced in engineering practice, but their quantification methods and certification paths differ.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before IEC 61508, the design of safety-related systems lacked a unified quantitative language. Different industries operated independently: process industries used relay interlocks, aviation used redundant channels, and nuclear industries used diversified protection systems—but the question "how safe is safe enough" had no comparable, auditable answer.

The historical contribution of IEC 61508 lies in introducing the Safety Integrity Level (SIL) as a quantitative yardstick, transforming "safety" from a qualitative slogan into a computable engineering parameter. It quantifies safety integrity into four levels, SIL1–SIL4, using dangerous failure probability, and distinguishes two metrics based on operating mode: low demand mode uses the average probability of failure on demand PFD\(_{avg}\), while high demand / continuous mode uses the probability of dangerous failure per hour PFH.

For humanoid robots, the positioning of IEC 61508 is particularly unique. Humanoid robots currently lack a dedicated Type C product safety standard—bipedal platforms do not fully fall under ISO 10218's "industrial robots" nor ISO 13482's "personal care robots." Manufacturers typically need to combine multiple standards and demonstrate their applicability to certification bodies, and IEC 61508, as a Type B generic safety standard, is one of the core bases in this combined reference that determines safety control architecture and component selection.

Principle Breakdown

① Safety Lifecycle: The Organizational Framework of Functional Safety

IEC 61508 organizes all safety work into a safety lifecycle: hazard and risk analysis → safety requirements allocation → safety integrity design → verification and validation → operation and maintenance → decommissioning. Each phase has documented deliverables, and the core of certification audits is the "safety case" formed by these deliverables.

The engineering implication of this framework is that functional safety is not a "test patch" applied after design completion, but an architectural constraint embedded from the requirements phase. For humanoid robots, this means the safety layer must be independent of the functional layer running the VLA stack—safety monitoring cannot rely on the main controller's compute power but must be executed by an independent hardware channel.

② Quantifying SIL: PFH and PFD

IEC 61508 defines SIL levels using dangerous failure probability. For continuously operating humanoid robot safety functions (such as safe stop, speed monitoring), the PFH metric for high demand / continuous mode is used:

SIL Level PFH (probability of dangerous failure per hour) Risk Reduction Factor
SIL1 \(10^{-6} \sim <10^{-5}\) 10–100
SIL2 \(10^{-7} \sim <10^{-6}\) 100–1000
SIL3 \(10^{-8} \sim <10^{-7}\) 1000–10000
SIL4 \(10^{-9} \sim <10^{-8}\) 10000–100000

The risk reduction factor is defined as the ratio of baseline risk to the residual risk after implementing the safety function. The higher the SIL level, the more stringent the safety function requirements, and the higher the demands on redundant architecture, diagnostic coverage (DC), and common cause failure (CCF) control.

③ Correspondence with ISO 13849

ISO 13849 primarily applies to safety-related control systems in machinery, and its PL levels correspond to the probability of dangerous failure per hour \(PFH_d\):

PL \(PFH_d\) (per hour) Typical Application
PL a \(5 \times 10^{-5} \sim <10^{-4}\) Low risk
PL b \(3 \times 10^{-5} \sim <5 \times 10^{-5}\) General risk
PL c \(2 \times 10^{-6} \sim <3 \times 10^{-5}\) Medium risk
PL d \(1 \times 10^{-6} \sim <2 \times 10^{-6}\) Higher risk
PL e \(\sim 1 \times 10^{-6}\) High risk

IEC 62061 is the application standard of IEC 61508 in machinery safety, using SIL levels combined with the PFH\(_d\) calculation method from ISO 13849 to assess system safety integrity. In actual projects, humanoid robot safety functions (such as safe stop, speed monitoring, collision detection) must simultaneously satisfy applicable machinery safety standards and functional safety standards.

Key Parameters and Specifications

SIL Levels and Target Failure Measures: IEC 61508 defines four levels, SIL1–SIL4, corresponding to different PFH/PFD ranges. For complex electromechanical systems like humanoid robots, SIL2/PL d is typically a higher requirement, while SIL3/PL e is used for critical functions involving frequent close interaction with humans.

Four Elements of Safety Integrity (ISO 13849 framework): Category (architectural category), MTTF\(_d\) (mean time to dangerous failure), DC\(_{avg}\) (average diagnostic coverage), CCF (common cause failure). Together, these four determine the achievable PL level of a safety function.

Safety Layer Independence: The typical target for critical safety functions in humanoid robots is PL d / SIL 2 to PL e / SIL 3, and the safety layer must be independent of the functional layer running the VLA stack. This means safety monitoring requires an independent clock source, independent power rail, and independent firmware—this is precisely the rationale for safety MCUs.

When Type C standards conflict with Type B standards, Type C takes precedence. The current awkward position of humanoid robots is that no dedicated Type C standard exists; manufacturers typically need to combine multiple standards and demonstrate their applicability to certification bodies, which is also a fundamental reason for the long certification cycles of humanoid robots.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Industrial Robot Domain: ISO 10218-1/-2 requires safety-related control systems of industrial robots to meet the functional safety requirements of ISO 13849 or IEC 61508. Safety functions such as safe stop, speed monitoring, and axis limiting typically target PL d / SIL 2.

Collaborative Robot Domain: The four collaboration modes defined by ISO/TS 15066 (hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, power and force limiting, safety-rated monitored stop) all rely on functional safety control systems for implementation. Contact force monitoring in power and force limiting mode requires the safety layer to complete risk assessment and trigger a stop within millisecond-level response times.

Personal Care Robot Domain: ISO 13482 covers three types of robots: mobility assistance, physical assistance, and mobile companion robots. For critical functions involving frequent close interaction with humans, SIL3/PL e is the target level.

Humanoid Robot Domain: Since no dedicated Type C standard exists, manufacturers typically combine ISO 13482, ISO 10218, and ISO/TS 15066, using IEC 61508 / ISO 13849 as the underlying functional safety framework. The safety MCU acts as a safety island independent of the main control SoC, executing safety functions such as emergency stop signal monitoring, joint current monitoring, and communication heartbeat detection.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Does Not Cover "Insufficient Performance" Hazards: IEC 61508 and ISO 13849 address "system faults"—hazards caused by functional failure. But humanoid robots also have another class of hazards: the system has no fault at all, yet the functionality itself is insufficient for the environment. For example, a vision model misidentifies a person as background in backlight conditions, or a VLA policy produces dangerous actions for out-of-distribution instructions. This type of "insufficient performance" hazard is handled by the Safety Of The Intended Functionality (SOTIF, originating from ISO 21448 in the automotive domain) framework.

② Safety ≠ Reliability: A highly reliable system rarely fails, but when it does fail, it may enter a dangerous state. Functional safety is concerned with the consequences and detectability of failures, not merely their frequency. Dangerous failures (failures that lead to loss of a safety function) and safe failures (failures that affect availability but do not cause danger) must be treated distinctly.

③ Long Certification Cycles: A complete certification cycle typically takes 6–18 months and should be initiated in parallel with overall architecture design. Compliance requirements directly translate into engineering design parameters—maximum motion speed, end-effector contact force limits, emergency stop button position and color, enclosure flame retardancy rating, battery thermal runaway protection, etc. The earlier standard requirements are incorporated into design freeze, the lower the cost of later rectification.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Higher SIL levels are always better"—Wrong. SIL4 requires PFH below \(10^{-8}\), and the corresponding redundant architecture, diagnostic coverage, and verification costs increase exponentially. For humanoid robots, SIL2/PL d is typically a higher requirement, while SIL3/PL e is used for critical functions involving frequent close interaction with humans. Over-engineering not only drives up costs but may also introduce new failure modes due to increased complexity.

  2. "Compliance with standards = exemption from liability"—Wrong. Compliance does not exempt liability, but it significantly reduces legal risk. When an accident occurs, "whether applicable current standards were followed" is the primary basis for courts and regulators to determine whether a manufacturer fulfilled its duty of care.

  3. "Functional safety = redundant design"—Wrong. Redundancy is only one means of improving safety integrity; diagnostic coverage (DC) and common cause failure (CCF) control are equally critical. Two identical channels may fail simultaneously due to the same design flaw—this is precisely the problem CCF analysis addresses.

  4. "SIL and PL can be directly converted"—Wrong. Although both are quantified based on PFH, their architectural requirements, diagnostic capabilities, and verification methods differ. In engineering practice, cross-referencing is common, but formal certification must follow the requirements of each respective standard.
