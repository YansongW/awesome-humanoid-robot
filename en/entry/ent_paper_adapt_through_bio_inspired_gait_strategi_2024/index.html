Summary

Legged robots must adapt their gait to navigate unpredictable environments, a challenge that animals master with ease. However, most deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approaches to quadruped locomotion rely on a fixed gait, limiting adaptability to changes in terrain and dynamic state.

Overview

This paper proposes a bio-inspired DRL control framework for quadruped robots, integrating a gait selection policy π_G, a bio-inspired gait scheduler (BGS), and a locomotion policy π_L, enabling the Unitree A1 to adapt to non-nominal terrains under zero-shot conditions. The core contribution lies in jointly training gait selection using multiple biomechanical metrics (CoT, τ%, W_ext, c_avg^err), reproducing animal gait transition phase behavior for the first time on a real robot, and validating the critical role of the gait reference β_L in robustness to rough terrain.

What It Changes

The bottleneck of existing DRL quadruped control frameworks is not single-gait tracking accuracy, but adaptability "beyond the policy": terrains outside the training distribution, velocity commands, and contact perturbations can quickly render fixed-gait policies ineffective. What the authors truly change is elevating "gait" from a preset parameter to an online-decision latent variable—not letting the policy implicitly "blend" gaits in a continuous action space, but explicitly and discretely selecting one of eight gaits, with the locomotion policy generating coordinated foot trajectories for that gait. This breaks the implicit assumption of "one policy, one gait" in DRL frameworks, shifting adaptability from "parameter generalization" to "structural generalization."

Another key shift lies in the training signal for gait selection: instead of velocity-interval heuristics or hand-crafted rules, the authors directly use the animal's biomechanical optimization objectives (energy consumption, force saturation, mechanical work, stability) as the reward for π_G. This changes the nature of the "gait switching" problem—from an engineering tuning problem to a learnable multi-objective optimization problem—and its behavioral patterns (transition phases, velocity dependence) quantitatively match animal data, providing a new tool for validating biomechanical hypotheses on robotic platforms.

Method Breakdown

The framework is a three-layer cascade with different frequencies and clearly separated responsibilities:

1. Gait Selection Policy π_G (100 Hz)

  • Input: o_G = [s, β_G, v_B^cmd, v̇_B^cmd, Γ*_{t−1}] ∈ ℝ⁶⁶
  • Output: Γ* ∈ [0,7], mapped to eight gaits (stand, trot, run, bound, pronk, limp, amble, hop)
  • Reward: r_G = 0.4·r_u + ψ(CoT) + ψ(τ%) + ψ(c_avg^err) + ψ(W_ext), where ψ(x)=1−tanh(x²) normalizes each metric
  • Key design: r_smooth penalizes ψ(CoT+τ%+c_avg^err+W_ext) during gait transitions, suppressing frequent meaningless switches

2. Bio-inspired Gait Scheduler BGS (pseudo-gait procedural memory)

  • Outputs β_L = [c_ref, p_x_ref, p_y_ref, p_z_ref] ∈ ℝ¹⁶, defining each foot's contact state and reference position in the world frame
  • Gait cycle count changed to C = e^(−2Ω), enabling near-instant transitions when Froude number Ω≥2 (corresponding to the typical threshold for quadrupeds transitioning from trot to run)
  • Transition resolution n = 1 + Ω_stab/Ω_stab_next: small n (smooth) when transitioning from high Ω_stab to low Ω_stab, large n (fast) in the reverse direction, reproducing the animal behavior of "slow run-to-trot descent, fast trot-to-run ascent"

3. Locomotion Policy π_L (500 Hz)

  • Input: o_L = [β_L, s, v_B^cmd] ∈ ℝ⁶⁹, with state s generated by SE from sensor vector σ
  • Output: target joint positions q*, converted to torques via PD controller (K_p=25, K_d=1, 1000 Hz)
  • Reward: r_L = −1.5·r_η + 15·r_v^cmd − 10·r_f − 5·r_stab, where r_η penalizes jerk/torque/action changes, r_f tracks the gait reference, and r_stab includes support foot sliding, base orientation, and hip position terms

4. Training Strategy

  • π_L trained only on flat terrain (deliberately excluding rough terrain to isolate β_L's contribution); π_G trained on h_terr⁰~h_terr³ (max height 0~0.2m)
  • All π_G variants (π_G^CoT, π_G^τ%, π_G^W_ext, π_G^c_err, π_G^uni) are paired with π_L^bio for training
  • PPO: 240 environments, lr=5e-4, GAE λ=0.95, clip=0.2, MLP [512,256,128] + LeakyReLU

Key Innovations

  1. Multi-metric joint gait selection: For the first time, four biomechanical metrics (CoT, τ%, W_ext, c_avg^err) are unified into the π_G reward, rather than a single energy metric. The authors demonstrate that a single CoT metric cannot produce animal-like transition phases (π_G^CoT transitions too early and unstably), and only joint optimization reproduces the animal behavior of "transition phase centered on λ^CoT"—a direct refutation of the assumption that "energy-optimal equals gait-optimal."

  2. BGS as a learnable "cerebellum": Treating the gait reference β_L as a strong prior for the locomotion policy rather than a negligible auxiliary signal. Ablation experiments show that removing β_L causes π_L to repeatedly fail on rough terrain (even when trained on that terrain), while π_L^bio succeeds zero-shot—β_L provides geometric constraints on foot trajectories, preventing the policy from learning coordination patterns from scratch, highly analogous to the cerebellum's feedforward correction role in animal locomotion.

  3. Robots as biomechanical hypothesis validation platforms: The gait transition trigger sequence exhibited by π_G^uni in real deployment (W_ext, τ%, c_avg^err spikes preceding gait changes, with CoT lagging) matches animal data, and the finding that τ% strongly correlates with stability has never been reported in reference [33]. This provides a low-cost, repeatable experimental pathway for validating animal locomotion theories with robots.

Real-World Deployment (Unitree A1)

  • Uneven grassy terrain: manually cycled through seven gaits, v_x^cmd=0.5 m/s, all successful, red shaded regions indicate transition periods
  • Loose wood: rear foot slipping caused collisions, π_G^uni recovered stability using pronk and bound
  • Low-friction boards/muddy grass: critical stability loss events occurred, but all recovered
  • Key observation: in all real experiments, spikes in W_ext, τ%, and c_avg^err coincided with or preceded gait changes, while CoT peaks lagged—consistent with animal data

Training Cost

  • π_L: 20k iterations, 6 hours; π_G: 20k iterations, 9 hours; single RTX3090, RaiSim

Boundaries and Limitations

The paper does not explicitly list a limitations section, but the following can be inferred from method details: - Fidelity of metric surrogates: CoT is approximated by joint torque × velocity + 0.3τ², W_ext only considers kinetic/potential energy changes, and c_avg^err substitutes for gait cycle CV—these surrogates may distort at high speeds or extreme terrains, and the authors did not perform sensitivity analysis - Training-deployment terrain gap: π_L is trained only on flat terrain; although zero-shot success is achieved, more extreme terrains beyond h_terr³ (e.g., rubble piles, snow) were not tested - Fixed gait library: The eight gait parameters (period, duty cycle, phase) are manually set, not learned; the BGS's Raibert heuristics may produce suboptimal references under non-periodic perturbations - No perceptual lookahead: The framework relies solely on proprioception, with no visual/tactile anticipation, relying on reactive recovery for sudden large disturbances (e.g., steps); the authors plan to integrate perception sensors in the future - Single-robot validation: Only validated on the Unitree A1, without testing transferability to other configurations (e.g., larger/smaller robots)

Engineering Insights

When reproducing or borrowing from this framework, the following aspects are most prone to pitfalls:

  1. First verify BGS's Ω calculation: Accurate estimation of the Froude number Ω directly affects transition timing. The authors changed the transition cycle count to C=e^(−2Ω); if your robot's mass/leg length differs, the Ω threshold needs recalibration—suggest first sweeping Ω∈[1,3] in simulation and observing whether gait transition speed matches animal data.

  2. π_G reward weights are sensitive: In r_G = 0.4·r_u + ψ(CoT) + ψ(τ%) + ψ(c_avg^err) + ψ(W_ext), the tanh normalization in ψ makes metrics dimensionally consistent, but if the utility weight w_u=0.4 is increased, the policy will favor velocity tracking at the expense of biomechanical realism; if decreased, degenerate behaviors like "stop-and-go to save energy" may emerge. Start from 0.4 and fine-tune gradually.

  3. π_L training must use only flat terrain: The authors deliberately exclude rough terrain to isolate β_L's effect. If you add rough terrain during training, π_L may learn to "power through" rather than rely on the gait reference, reducing deployment-time sensitivity to β_L—this would break the framework's modular design.

  4. State estimator noise model: The noise amplitudes applied to σ via domain randomization (e.g., ω_B at 0.015𝒩(0,1)) are tuned. If your robot's SE noise characteristics differ, rematching is needed—too little noise increases sim-to-real gap, too much makes the policy overly conservative.

  5. Gait switching frequency: Experiment (a) switches gaits every 1s, experiment (b) every 2s—this is to sufficiently excite π_G exploration. If your downstream task requires slower switching (e.g., long-duration stable walking), π_G needs retraining, otherwise the policy may switch too frequently due to training distribution mismatch.

  6. Most easily overlooked pitfall: π_G^uni in real deployment relies on "spikes" in W_ext, τ%, and c_avg^err to trigger gait changes, but these metrics fluctuate minimally on flat terrain. If your deployment scenario is flat ground, π_G may not switch gaits for extended periods—this matches animal behavior (trot at low speeds), but if the task requires rapid speed changes, ensure velocity command changes are large enough to excite metric variations.
