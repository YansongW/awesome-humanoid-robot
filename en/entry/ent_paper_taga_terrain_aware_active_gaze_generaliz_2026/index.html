Summary

Agile humanoid locomotion across diverse challenging terrain demands both wide perceptual coverage and precise local geometry understanding. Motivated by the way humans selectively look at relevant terrain during locomotion, we introduce TAGA, a Terrain-aware Active Gaze learning framework for Attention-based humanoid control. Institutions per source list: 新加坡国立大学Marmot实验室、浙江大学、华南理工大学.

Overview

TAGA is an active perception framework for agile locomotion in humanoid robots, implemented by the research team in Isaac Lab simulation and on the Unitree G1 real robot. Its core contribution lies in combining an active gaze mechanism for "where to look" with multimodal perception fusion. Through differentiable gaze point prediction and cross-attention mechanisms, it achieves comparable or even superior generalization performance to full-image scanning while significantly reducing training costs.

What It Changes

Existing perception-based locomotion methods face a dilemma: height-map-based approaches incur sharply rising computational costs as the perception range expands, while forward-looking depth-map methods can see far but miss critical terrain details near the feet. More fundamentally, these two perception paradigms—visual foresight and local precision from height maps—are inherently complementary, yet existing methods either rely on a single source or only loosely couple them, never truly addressing the active perception problem of "when and where to look." When humans and animals walk over complex terrain, their gaze actively shifts to task-relevant regions rather than uniformly scanning the entire field of view. TAGA changes this paradigm: instead of treating perception as passive information collection, it models perception as a learnable decision process, allowing the policy to autonomously determine which local terrain information is most critical for the next action.

The practical significance of this change is that it breaks the linear coupling between perception range and computational cost. By focusing attention on a compact region of the height map (a K=11 cropped patch), TAGA reduces the complexity of local geometry processing by nearly an order of magnitude while retaining forward-looking visual cues. More importantly, this active gaze mechanism is not a hand-designed heuristic rule but emerges automatically through end-to-end reinforcement learning—the policy learns where to look on different terrains, which is more adaptive than any fixed strategy.

Method Breakdown

The overall framework of TAGA is a POMDP policy. The observation space consists of three parts: a forward-looking depth map d_t ∈ ℝ^{1×36×64}, an ego-centric height map h_t^{xyz} ∈ ℝ^{3×21×21}, and a 5-frame proprioceptive history p_t^H. The action space is 29-dimensional target joint positions, output at 50 Hz and tracked by a low-level PD controller at 200 Hz.

Task-Relevant Active Gaze Module

  • A lightweight prediction head f_roi receives the concatenation of visual embedding e_t^d and proprioceptive embedding e_t^p, and outputs a normalized 2D gaze position r_t ∈ [0,1]²
  • Maps r_t to height map grid coordinates (u_t, v_t) = (⌊r_t^x M⌋, ⌊r_t^y M⌋), where M=21
  • Crops a compact terrain patch h̃_t^{xyz} of size K=11 via differentiable bilinear grid sampling
  • A boundary penalty L_roi prevents the gaze point from degrading to the height map boundary, with margin m=0.05

Visual-Motor Fusion Encoder

  • The cropped terrain patch is encoded via a lightweight CNN and a spatial coordinate MLP, producing a pointwise terrain embedding E_t^m ∈ ℝ^{K×K×128}, used as key and value
  • A projection head f_proj generates the query from visual and proprioceptive embeddings
  • Multi-head cross-attention fusion: e_t^{pg} = MHA(f_proj([e_t^p, e_t^d]), E_t^m, E_t^m), outputting ℝ¹²⁸

Action Decoding and Training

  • Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module with N_e=5 experts; a gating network g(·) generates soft routing weights α_t^i = Softmax(g(e_t^π))_i, and the final action is a_t = Σ α_t^i E_i(e_t^π)
  • Asymmetric actor-critic PPO training; the critic receives privileged information (true linear velocity, full height map)
  • Two-stage training: Stage 1 runs 30k iterations of noise-free training to learn core skills; Stage 2 runs 10k iterations with reduced entropy coefficient and deployment-oriented domain randomization
  • A contrastive loss L_con aligns MoE gating embeddings with full height map terrain embeddings in a shared prototype space via Sinkhorn balanced assignment, encouraging expert terrain specialization
  • Symmetry augmentation exploits the left-right symmetry of humanoid robots by mirroring observations and actions and incorporating them into PPO updates

Key Innovations

Active gaze as a learnable decision: "Where to look" is modeled as an end-to-end differentiable prediction head rather than hand-crafted rules or fixed strategies. This is the first time an active perception mechanism is jointly optimized with a reinforcement learning policy, making the gaze behavior itself part of the policy. Its importance lies in the policy learning to adaptively allocate perception resources across different terrains—gazing at footholds during gap crossing and at the next support surface on sparse stepping stones.

Cropped attention replacing full-image processing: A compact 11×11 patch is cropped from the 21×21 height map via differentiable bilinear sampling, reducing the input scale of local geometry processing by approximately 73%. This not only lowers computational cost but, more importantly, forces the policy to focus on task-relevant regions through an information bottleneck, avoiding information redundancy and overfitting in full-image processing. Experiments show that this cropped attention performs comparably to full-image scanning on OOD terrains while reducing training cost by 65.2%.

Synergy between MoE and terrain contrastive learning: A contrastive loss aligns MoE gating embeddings with terrain embeddings from the full height map in a shared prototype space, causing expert networks to spontaneously specialize to different terrain types. This resolves the common expert degeneration problem of MoE in reinforcement learning while providing the policy with terrain-aware modular decision-making capabilities.

Experiments and Results

Simulation evaluation was conducted in Isaac Lab with 8,000 parallel environments on 4 RTX 5090 GPUs, with a total training time of approximately 17 GPU-days. Each terrain was evaluated over 1,000 trials.

Boundaries and Limitations

The authors explicitly acknowledge the actuator thermal load issue: the dynamic actions implemented by TAGA (explosive jumps, precise footholds, dynamic balance recovery) impose high thermal loads on actuators during prolonged operation, which may lead to gradually degrading control precision, manifesting as shortened jump distances or missed footholds. Additionally, the method relies heavily on height map quality—on complex terrain, poor height map reconstruction directly leads to improper gaits or failures. The paper does not explicitly mention the data volume (the specific scale of AMP reference data) or transferability to different robot platforms. Notably, the baseline methods in the real-world evaluation (HPL, PIM, CMoE, etc.) were not reproduced under identical experimental conditions but rather cited from their respective papers' best results; therefore, cross-method comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing TAGA, the first things to verify are the initialization of the gaze prediction head and the weight of the boundary penalty—from the ablation experiments, TAGA-InactiveGaze (fixed gaze point) drops sharply to 57.10% on Gaps, indicating that the gaze mechanism is crucial for gap-type terrains, but an overly strong boundary penalty may cause the gaze point to converge prematurely to the central region, weakening adaptability. Second, the two-stage training strategy cannot be skipped: the noise-free training in Stage 1 establishes core skills, and the domain randomization fine-tuning in Stage 2 (especially depth camera degradation simulation and height map noise) is key to real-world generalization. The most likely pitfall lies in the Sinkhorn balanced assignment implementation of the contrastive loss L_con—if the update frequency of the prototype dictionary C or the temperature coefficient T_con is set improperly, MoE experts may degenerate rather than specialize. For downstream teams, it is recommended to first validate gaze behavior on a small-scale terrain set (e.g., only gaps and stepping stones) before scaling to full-terrain training; the training cost is approximately 17 GPU-days (4×RTX 5090), and if resources are limited, consider reducing the number of parallel environments to 4,000 and observing performance degradation. During deployment, pay attention to actuator thermal management—TAGA's explosive actions may trigger overheating protection during prolonged operation; it is recommended to include actuator temperature monitoring and degradation strategies in real-robot testing.
