Overview

Simulation of manipulation and grasping is a key process node in the P7 stage (simulation platform construction and validation) of the humanoid robot development pipeline, and belongs to the method type. It validates upper-limb manipulation tasks in a virtual environment through technical means such as arm/dexterous hand models, grasp pose generation, and contact force feedback, thereby reducing physical debugging risks. What it truly changes is not the superficial form of "replacing hardware testing with software," but rather the shift of grasp capability validation from a "hardware-first, post-hoc remediation" serial model to a "simulation-first, virtual-physical联动" parallel validation paradigm.

Content

What it is: A precise definition of manipulation and grasping simulation

Manipulation and grasping simulation (P7.2.3) is the third sub-task under the P7.2 simulation validation implementation work package in the P7 stage. Its core objective is to reproduce the grasping and manipulation process of typical objects by the humanoid robot's upper limb (arm and dexterous hand/gripper) in a virtual physical environment. This process uses the URDF/SDF models output from the P6 stage as the base input, and by configuring contact models, friction parameters, and hand kinematic constraints, it performs closed-loop validation of grasp pose generation algorithms, force-controlled contact stability, and manipulation task feasibility.

From an engineering process perspective, P7.2.3 internally expands into five third-level sub-tasks: P7.2.3.1 input review and target quantification, P7.2.3.2 solution/method design, P7.2.3.3 implementation/prototype/sample fabrication, P7.2.3.4 validation and issue closure, and P7.2.3.5 documentation output and downstream delivery. Each sub-task follows a unified four-level key action structure internally, ensuring full-chain traceability from input to delivery.

Why it exists: Pain points and historical positioning

In early practices of humanoid robot development, validation of upper-limb manipulation algorithms relied heavily on physical prototype debugging. Every failed grasp meant mechanical wear, motor overload, or even hardware damage, and debugging cycles were often measured in weeks. This "hardware-first, post-hoc remediation" serial model severely slowed the iteration speed of grasp algorithms—a simple adjustment to pose parameters might require waiting for a multi-day physical testing window.

The emergence of manipulation and grasping simulation is precisely to break this bottleneck. It allows developers to repeatedly test grasp poses, force-control strategies, and object interaction behaviors in a virtual environment at near-zero cost, exposing algorithm defects and system risks before physical prototype debugging. What it truly changes is not the superficial form of "replacing hardware testing with software," but rather the transformation of humanoid robot development from a "hardware-first, post-hoc remediation" serial model to a "simulation-first, virtual-physical联动" parallel validation paradigm. This shift reduces the iteration cycle of grasp algorithms from weeks to days or even hours, significantly lowering development costs.

Principle breakdown

① Contact model: The physical foundation of grasping simulation

The core of grasping simulation lies in the precise modeling of contact forces. When a dexterous hand's finger contacts an object's surface, the contact force F consists of the normal force F_n and the tangential friction force F_t, whose relationship follows the Coulomb friction model:

\[ \|F_t\| \leq \mu \|F_n\| \]

where μ is the friction coefficient. When the tangential force exceeds the friction limit, sliding occurs between the finger and the object, leading to grasp failure. The simulation platform updates the contact force and object pose at each time step by solving the contact dynamics equations, thereby determining whether the grasp is stable. The accuracy of the contact model directly determines the consistency between simulation results and physical behavior—an overly simplified model may miss sliding events, while an overly detailed one incurs prohibitive computational costs.

② Grasp pose generation: From "touching" to "grasping"

Grasp pose generation is the core algorithmic component of manipulation and grasping simulation. Given the object's geometric model and hand kinematic constraints, the algorithm needs to search for a set of finger joint angles that form a force-closure or form-closure contact configuration with the object's surface. Force closure requires that contact forces can resist external force disturbances in any direction, while form closure requires that the geometric distribution of contact points fully constrains the object's motion. The simulation platform generates candidate grasp poses through sampling, optimization, or learning-based methods and validates their stability in the physics engine.

③ Force-controlled contact stability: The key difference between simulation and reality

Force-controlled contact stability is what distinguishes manipulation and grasping simulation from pure kinematic simulation. In simulation, force-control strategies achieve desired contact forces by controlling joint torques, with contact force feedback coming from virtual force sensors. The simulation platform must simultaneously model the noise characteristics and latency of force sensors to evaluate the robustness of force-control algorithms on physical hardware. The fidelity of this aspect directly determines the success rate of sim-to-real transfer.

Horizontal comparison

Manipulation and grasping simulation differs significantly from other simulation tasks in the P7 stage (such as motion control simulation and sim-to-real transfer evaluation). Motion control simulation focuses on whole-body dynamics and gait stability, with its core challenges being foot-ground contact and balance control; manipulation and grasping simulation focuses on the interaction between the upper-limb end effector and objects, with core challenges in hand kinematic constraints and contact force control. The two differ in physical engine configuration, sensor models, and validation metrics.

Compared with physical grasping tests, the advantages of manipulation and grasping simulation lie in low cost, repeatability, and observability—simulation allows pausing, replaying, and visualizing contact force distributions at any time, which is difficult to achieve in physical testing. Its disadvantage lies in insufficient model fidelity—simplifications in contact models, uncertainty in friction parameters, and deviations in sensor noise simulation can all lead to gaps between simulation results and physical behavior. Therefore, the positioning of manipulation and grasping simulation is "pre-research and screening," not "replacement for physical validation."

Who uses it: Application cases

Manipulation and grasping simulation has clear application scenarios in the humanoid robot development pipeline. In the P7.2.3.1 input review and target quantification stage, developers need to organize the upstream input list and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task owners, timelines, and risk registers. This stage ensures that simulation task objectives align with upstream requirements, avoiding the awkward situation of "simulation completed but nobody uses it."

In the P7.2.3.2 solution/method design stage, developers need to form no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, establish an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, and ultimately organize a review and freeze the solution. Candidate solutions may include different grasp pose generation algorithms (e.g., sampling-based vs. optimization-based), different contact model accuracy configurations, or different hand model complexity choices.

In the P7.2.3.3 implementation/prototype/sample fabrication stage, developers build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulations or prototype validation, and log anomalies and deviations. This stage is the main body of simulation work, requiring iterative refinement of grasp poses and force-control parameters.

In the P7.2.3.4 validation and issue closure stage, developers formulate test/review plans and pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output issue lists and improvement measures. This stage ensures that simulation results meet completion criteria and tracks unresolved issues to closure.

In the P7.2.3.5 documentation output and downstream delivery stage, developers write documents per templates with references to raw data, complete internal review and version control, and publish and notify downstream dependents. This stage ensures that simulation outcomes are traceable, reusable, and formally delivered to downstream stages (e.g., P10 physical testing).

Limitations and boundaries

The primary limitation of manipulation and grasping simulation lies in the trade-off between contact model accuracy and computational cost. High-fidelity contact models (such as elastic contact models) can more accurately simulate deformation and stress distribution between fingers and objects, but the computational cost increases significantly, making real-time simulation difficult. Engineering judgment: under current computational conditions, simulation platforms typically adopt a simplified scheme of rigid contact models plus Coulomb friction, which is sufficiently accurate for most rigid-object grasping scenarios but falls short for soft objects or fine manipulation tasks.

The second limitation is the contradiction between hand model complexity and simulation efficiency. Fully actuated dexterous hands can have up to 16–24 DOF (per neighbor card corpus), each requiring kinematic and dynamic computation, with simulation speed decreasing significantly as DOF increases. Underactuated dexterous hands have 8–16 DOF (per neighbor card corpus); although simulation is faster, control precision is lower, making it difficult to validate fine manipulation algorithms.

The third limitation is the inherent gap in sim-to-real transfer. Friction coefficients, sensor noise, and actuator latency in simulation are all artificially set and deviate from physical reality. Even if the grasp success rate in simulation reaches 100%, the physical success rate may be far lower. Therefore, the output of manipulation and grasping simulation should be regarded as "pre-screening of candidate solutions," not "final validation conclusions."

Common misconceptions

  1. "Simulation success equals physical success"—Wrong. Contact models, friction parameters, and sensor noise in simulation are idealized, and sim-to-real transfer has an inherent gap. Simulation success only indicates that the algorithm works under ideal conditions; physical validation remains an essential step.

  2. "The finer the contact model, the better"—Wrong. High-fidelity contact models are closer to physical reality but incur huge computational costs, potentially making simulation speed insufficient for iteration needs. Engineering judgment: choose an appropriate contact model accuracy based on task requirements, balancing fidelity and efficiency.

  3. "The higher the hand model DOF, the better"—Wrong. Fully actuated dexterous hands (16–24 DOF) offer high dexterity but are complex to control and costly (per neighbor card corpus); underactuated dexterous hands (8–16 DOF) have strong adaptive grasping capabilities but lower control precision (per neighbor card corpus). Hand model selection should be based on task requirements rather than pursuing high DOF.

  4. "Grasping simulation only needs to focus on finger-object contact"—Wrong. Manipulation and grasping simulation also needs to consider arm kinematics, the object's trajectory in space, and post-grasp manipulation tasks (such as搬运, placement). Ignoring these aspects leads to severe discrepancies between simulation results and physical behavior.
