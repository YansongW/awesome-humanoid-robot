Overview

A linear actuator is an actuating component that converts rotary motion into linear motion. In humanoid robots, it handles critical linear motion functions such as parallel ankle joint actuation and parallel torso spine actuation. What it truly changes is not the motor's rotational output itself, but the topological restructuring of robot joints from "rotational degrees of freedom" to "linear degrees of freedom"—this makes high-stiffness, high-torque-density linear joints possible.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

A linear actuator is an integrated actuating component that converts the rotary motion of a motor (stepper or servo) into linear motion of the output shaft through a screw transmission mechanism. Its core structure is an integrated "motor + screw" design, with screw types primarily including trapezoidal lead screws and ball screws.

In the context of humanoid robots, linear actuators specifically refer to mechatronic products that are compact, self-locking, and suitable for precision push rods and linear joint applications. They differ from rotary actuators (such as harmonic reducers combined with frameless torque motors) in that they output linear displacement rather than angular displacement.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robot joint design has long faced a fundamental contradiction: rotary motors naturally output torque, but many motions of human joints (especially ankles, spine, and finger bases) are essentially linear-driven. Forcing rotary actuators plus linkage mechanisms to approximate linear motion introduces additional inertia, backlash, and complexity.

The historical positioning of linear actuators is precisely to resolve this contradiction. They internalize the "rotary-to-linear" conversion within the actuator itself, allowing system designers to directly obtain linear thrust output. Taking Dingzhi Technology's ball/trapezoidal lead screw linear actuators as an example, their integrated stepper/servo motor + screw design is compact and self-locking, making it suitable for robotic linear joints and precision push rod applications.

A deeper driving force comes from the parallel actuation topology requirements of humanoid robots. As described in Chapter 8, many local mechanisms adopt parallel design principles: the ankle joint uses two linear actuators in parallel to drive roll/pitch, improving torque density and stiffness; the torso uses multiple elastic actuators in parallel to drive the spine, mimicking the flexibility and load-bearing capacity of the human spine; the hand base employs miniature parallel mechanisms internally to improve stiffness. These scenarios inherently require "linear output" actuating components rather than indirect solutions of rotary actuators plus transmission mechanisms.

Principle Breakdown

① Rotary-to-Linear Kinematic Conversion

The core kinematic relationship of a linear actuator is determined by the screw lead \(l\)—the distance the nut advances per full screw revolution. Let the screw rotation angle be \(\theta\) (rad); then the nut's linear displacement is:

\[ x = \frac{l}{2\pi} \, \theta \]

Differentiating with respect to time yields the relationship between linear velocity and angular velocity:

\[ v = \frac{l}{2\pi} \, \omega \]

where \(\omega\) is the screw angular velocity (rad/s). This relationship reveals the essence of a linear actuator: it is a "rotary-to-linear" transmission whose ratio is uniquely determined by the lead \(l\).

② Force and Torque Conversion

Neglecting losses, the axial thrust produced by motor output torque \(\tau\) is:

\[ F = \frac{2\pi}{l} \, \tau \]

Including transmission efficiency \(\eta\):

\[ F = \frac{2\pi \, \eta}{l} \, \tau \]

Or solving inversely for the required motor torque:

\[ \tau = \frac{F \, l}{2\pi \, \eta} \]

This formula is the core basis for linear actuator selection. It shows that: the smaller the lead, the lower the motor torque required for the same thrust, but the lower the output speed as well—this is the fundamental "force vs. speed" trade-off in linear actuator design.

③ Self-Locking Principle

A key characteristic of trapezoidal lead screws is self-locking: when the helix angle is smaller than the equivalent friction angle, the screw will not rotate backward under axial load. This means the linear actuator can hold its position when power is removed, without requiring an additional brake. This property is critical for robot safety—for example, in a parallel torso spine, even if power is interrupted, the spine will not collapse under gravity.

Horizontal Comparison

Linear actuators, rotary actuators, and planetary roller screw actuators constitute the three main forms of humanoid robot actuators:

Dimension Ball/Trapezoidal Lead Screw Linear Actuator Rotary Actuator (Harmonic + Frameless Motor) Planetary Roller Screw Actuator
Output form Linear displacement Angular displacement Linear displacement
Typical load ≤500 N Torque-type Up to 8000 N-class
Self-locking Trapezoidal screw: yes; ball screw: no No (requires brake) Depends on design
Structural complexity Low Medium High
Typical applications Precision push rods, small-load linear joints Rotary joints (shoulder, elbow, hip) High-thrust linear joints (Optimus legs)

The key distinction is: ball/trapezoidal lead screw linear actuators are optimal in the "medium load, high precision, compact size" range, while planetary roller screws are irreplaceable in "ultra-high thrust" scenarios. Rotary actuators cover all rotational degree-of-freedom scenarios.

Beyond robotics, linear actuators are also widely used in automation equipment for precision push rods, positioning stages, and similar scenarios. Their ±0.02 mm positioning accuracy and ±0.01 mm repeat positioning accuracy are sufficient for most industrial positioning requirements.

Limitations and Boundaries

The core limitation of linear actuators lies in the thrust-stroke-speed trilemma:

  1. Thrust ceiling: The 500 N maximum load means they cannot handle high-thrust linear joints. If Tesla Optimus leg linear joints need to output 8000 N-class thrust, a planetary roller screw solution is required (based on corpus data, at 8000 N thrust, 5 mm lead, and 0.90 efficiency, the motor would need approximately 7.07 N·m continuous torque).

  2. Speed limitation: Lead and rotational speed jointly determine linear speed. A small lead (e.g., 1 mm) amplifies thrust but produces extremely low output speed at the same rotational speed, making it unsuitable for high-speed linear motion scenarios.

  3. The double-edged sword of self-locking: The self-locking property of trapezoidal lead screws is an advantage for holding position when power is removed, but it becomes resistance when reverse driving is needed (e.g., external force pushing), potentially reducing the system's backdrivability transparency.

  4. Stroke limitation: The 10–300 mm stroke range limits its application in long-stroke linear motion (such as large leg strides).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "A linear actuator is a linear motor"—Wrong. A linear motor (Linear Motor) directly produces linear motion without any conversion mechanism; whereas a linear actuator is essentially an integrated "rotary motor + screw" whose kinematic relationship is determined by the lead \(l\). The two differ completely in structure, control, and performance characteristics.

  2. "Ball screws are always better than trapezoidal lead screws"—Wrong. Ball screws have high efficiency and low wear, but they lack self-locking and are costly; trapezoidal lead screws, despite lower efficiency, offer good self-locking and low cost, making them superior in applications requiring position holding when power is removed. The choice depends on the specific scenario.

  3. "The smaller the lead, the better"—Wrong. A small lead does amplify thrust (\(F = 2\pi\eta\tau/l\)), but it simultaneously reduces output speed (\(v = l\omega/2\pi\)). Designers must trade off between thrust and speed rather than blindly pursuing a small lead.

  4. "Linear actuators are only for small loads"—Partially correct. The typical 500 N ceiling does limit their use in primary load-bearing joints, but parallel actuation (such as dual actuators in the ankle) can combine output forces to achieve higher equivalent thrust.
