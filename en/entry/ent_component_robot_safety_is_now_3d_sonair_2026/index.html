Content

What It Is: A Perception Leap from 2D Planes to 3D Space

ADAR One is an acoustic detection and ranging (ADAR) sensor developed by Sonair, positioned as safety-rated 3D perception hardware. It is not a traditional ultrasonic ranging module but a complete, independently certified safety sensor system designed specifically for human-robot collaboration scenarios.

The sensor is certified for SIL2 (Safety Integrity Level 2, as defined by IEC 61508) and PL d (Performance Level d, as defined by ISO 13849) applications. This means it meets all requirements of the European Machinery Directive for the safe detection of people and objects and can be used directly as a core perception element in safety circuits.

What it truly changes is not "how far the robot can see," but "whether the robot can reliably know that a human is nearby under any conditions"—this is the fundamental question of safe perception.

Why It Exists: Structural Blind Spots in 2D Safety Systems

Most current mobile robot systems rely on 2D laser scanners to define the safety perimeter. These sensors emit laser beams within a single horizontal plane and measure reflection times to build a map of obstacles within that plane. However, this approach has a fundamental flaw: it cannot detect people and obstacles above or below a single plane.

In industrial environments, this means:

  • A worker squatting to repair equipment may be entirely below the scanning plane, becoming "invisible";
  • Goods stacked on aisles that are higher or lower than the scanning plane also go undetected;
  • Humanoid robots, with their multi-degree-of-freedom torsos and arms, have a range of motion far exceeding a single plane, making 2D scanning completely inadequate for covering their workspace.

Sonair CEO Knut Sandven put it succinctly: "The bottleneck for safe human-robot coexistence is not intelligence or speed, but safe perception—reliably knowing that a human is nearby under any conditions." This observation reveals a long-standing structural gap in the industry: AI advancements have made robots smarter, but safety infrastructure has not kept pace.

Principle Breakdown

① Core Mechanism of Acoustic Detection and Ranging (ADAR)

ADAR One uses ultrasonic waves as its detection medium. Unlike lasers or cameras that rely on light reflection, ultrasonic sensors emit sound wave pulses and receive echoes, calculating target distance by measuring the time difference between transmission and return. The basic ranging principle is:

\[ d = \frac{v_{\text{sound}} \cdot t_{\text{flight}}}{2} \]

where \(d\) is the target distance, \(v_{\text{sound}}\) is the speed of sound (approximately 343 m/s at room temperature, a general physical constant), and \(t_{\text{flight}}\) is the round-trip time of the sound wave. The division by 2 accounts for the round trip of the sound wave.

② Implementation of 3D Spatial Perception

ADAR One's key breakthrough lies in extending ultrasound from "single-point ranging" to "3D spatial perception." Through a phased array or multi-transducer array, it simultaneously transmits and receives multiple acoustic signals within a 180°×180° field of view, reconstructing the 3D distribution of obstacles using time-of-flight and phase differences of echoes from different directions.

The advantages of this approach include:

  • Unaffected by lighting conditions: Reliable operation in darkness, strong light, glare, and other scenarios where cameras fail;
  • Unaffected by color and texture: Laser scanners may fail on dark or highly reflective surfaces, while sound waves are immune to such issues;
  • Full-height coverage: Can detect squatting people on the ground, obstacles above the scanning plane, and other targets that 2D systems cannot perceive.

③ Technical Basis for Safety Certification

SIL2 and PL d certification require the sensor to have fail-safe characteristics: when the sensor itself malfunctions, the system must detect the fault and enter a safe state rather than output erroneous safety signals. This requires ADAR One to have:

  • Self-diagnostic capability: Continuous monitoring of its own operating status to detect transducer failures, signal anomalies, and other issues;
  • Redundant design: Redundant configuration of critical paths to ensure a single fault does not compromise safety functions;
  • Deterministic response: Providing deterministic safety outputs within specified response times.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

ADAR One's target market is autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) and industrial automation. Its tiny footprint design enables flush embedding into almost any robot form factor, including humanoid robots.

Typical application scenarios include:

  • Human-robot collaboration workstations: Providing certified 3D safety monitoring within industrial robot work areas, replacing or supplementing traditional 2D safety scanners;
  • Autonomous mobile robot navigation: Detecting people and obstacles at all heights in dynamic environments such as warehouses and factories to avoid collisions;
  • Humanoid robot safety layer: Operating as foundational safety perception infrastructure, independent of camera and AI systems, providing deterministic judgments on "whether the space is safe" for higher-level decision-making.

According to available information, ADAR One has entered series production and is already shipping on deployed industrial robots. Sonair's CEO positions it as "a new safety layer, a certified 3D perception foundation that sits beneath any camera, AI stack, or motion system, independently verifying whether the space around the robot is safe."

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Sound speed is affected by ambient temperature: The speed of sound varies with temperature, which may affect ranging accuracy. While temperature compensation can mitigate this, additional calibration is needed in environments with extreme temperature variations (engineering judgment).
  2. Limited detection range: Ultrasonic sensors typically have shorter effective detection distances than LiDAR, making them suitable for close-range safety monitoring rather than long-range navigation (specific values require separate verification).
  3. Multipath effects: Sound waves may undergo multiple reflections in complex environments, producing false echoes that require signal processing algorithms to suppress.
  4. Does not replace cameras and AI: ADAR One is positioned as a safety layer rather than a primary perception sensor; it verifies "whether the space is safe" but does not provide semantic understanding (e.g., "what object is this").
  5. Certification scope: SIL2 / PL d applies to specific safety functions; applications requiring higher safety levels (e.g., SIL3 / PL e) require separate evaluation.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "ADAR One is just an ordinary ultrasonic sensor"—Incorrect. It has passed SIL2 / PL d safety certification, featuring fail-safe characteristics and deterministic responses that ordinary ultrasonic modules do not possess.
  2. "A 3D sensor can replace 2D safety systems"—Not entirely. ADAR One is a complement rather than a simple replacement; it fills the height-dimension blind spots of 2D systems, but 2D laser scanning still has advantages in long-range detection and perimeter definition.
  3. "Safety certification is just a marketing gimmick"—Incorrect. SIL2 / PL d certification means the sensor has undergone independent verification and meets the functional safety requirements of IEC 61508 and ISO 13849, which are hard requirements at both legal and insurance levels.
  4. "Ultrasound is unreliable in industrial environments"—Partially a misconception. Noise and vibration in industrial environments can indeed interfere with ultrasound, but ADAR One's design goal is precisely "reliable under any conditions"; its signal processing algorithms' anti-interference capability requires separate verification.
