Summary

Whole-body Humanoid-Object Interaction (HOI) is bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-fidelity 3D data. While video generative priors offer a promising alternative, existing methods suffer from \textit{Representation Misalignment} due to their reliance on geometric priors (e.g., explicit CAD models), and \textit{Retargeting Complexity} arising from intensive morphing and morphological mismatch. Institutions per source list: Zhejiang University、Shanghai AI Laboratory、CUHK 等.

Overview

Imagine2Real proposes a zero-shot whole-body human-object interaction (HOI) framework that leverages video generation models as motion priors, bypassing geometric priors and complex retargeting through a unified 4D point trajectory representation and a behavior foundation model (BFM)-based keypoint tracker. The method achieves real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, with the core contribution being the combination of sparse keypoint tracking with BFM latent space search, enabling general interaction policies without CAD models and task-specific rewards.

What It Changes

Existing HOI methods face a triple dilemma: VLA end-to-end models are limited by scarce humanoid-specific data, AMP-style methods are prone to mode collapse and require manual reward engineering, and motion tracking methods are confined to rigid playback. More critically, using video generation models as upstream motion sources presents two fundamental obstacles—representation misalignment (extracting 3D motion from 2D videos requires strong geometric priors such as CAD models) and retargeting complexity (dense low-level signals require explicit mapping to specific humanoid embodiments, with object geometry further amplifying errors). What Imagine2Real truly changes is reframing the problem from "dense motion retargeting" to "sparse keypoint tracking," using a unified 4D point trajectory representation for both robot and object motion, completely circumventing the need for geometric priors. This shift makes zero-shot HOI possible because sparse keypoints (base + hands) can be robustly extracted from videos without task-specific reward design.

Method Breakdown

Three-Stage Progressive Training

  1. BFM Backbone Pretraining (~68.5h data): The motion encoder ℰ maps δ-step action sequences to 32-dimensional latent codes z_t, the autoregressive predictor 𝒫 estimates the prior ẑ_t based on proprioceptive history o_{0:t}^prop and the previous latent state, and the decoder 𝒟 converts latent commands into low-level action targets. History is compressed via GRU, with instantaneous observations including joint positions, velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity, local end-effector positions, and the previous action.
  2. Keypoint Tracker Training (~8.86h data): The BFM backbone is frozen, and the latent residual planner ℋ receives sparse keypoint tracking targets m_{t:t+δ}^kp, outputting residual commands z_t^res. The new latent command z'_t = ẑ_t + z_t^res is then decoded into whole-body actions. Only three keypoints are tracked: the robot base and both hands.
  3. Interaction Adapter Fine-tuning (~0.43h data): Inspired by ResMimic, joint-level residual actions Δa_t are learned to refine the keypoint tracker output, with the final command being a_t^int = a'_t + Δa_t.

Video-to-Motion Pipeline

Monocular RGB + text instruction → video generation (Seedance 2.0 Fast) → SAM3 segmentation of key regions → SpaTrackerV2 tracking of 4D point trajectories → outlier filtering based on visibility and confidence → geometric mean to generate stable reference trajectories. For Mocap deployment, initial-frame precise global position scaling calibrates trajectories to real-world dimensions.

Key Design Decisions

  • BFM latent space as search domain: Significantly reduces action rate and action smoothness errors compared to raw joint space while maintaining natural gait
  • Progressive training: Each stage benefits from the largest-scale relevant supervision, and the interaction adapter can be efficiently trained with simple tracking rewards
  • Exponential kernel reward: r = exp(-||x - x̂||²/σ), avoiding manually designed complex heuristics

Key Innovations

  1. Unified 4D point trajectory representation: For the first time, robot and object motion are uniformly represented as 4D point trajectories, aligned in a common coordinate system, completely eliminating geometric prior requirements. This is a fundamental improvement over existing independent estimation methods (GVHMR for the robot, FoundationPose for objects), which introduce spatial and depth discrepancies.

  2. Sparse keypoint tracking in BFM latent space: Only three keypoints (base + hands) suffice to produce stable gaits, overcoming the limitations of VisualMimic (5 keypoints lacking joint-level information) and HEAD (3 keypoints relying on AMP, limiting scalability). Leveraging the BFM latent space as the search domain is key—sparse signals still preserve motion naturalness in latent space.

  3. Three-stage progressive learning: From large-scale proprioceptive data (68.5h) to loco-manipulation data (8.86h) and then to task-specific HOI data (0.43h), each stage utilizes the largest-scale relevant supervision. The interaction adapter leverages foundational priors, bypassing the complex mechanisms required for learning from scratch (object force curricula, contact rewards, physics state initialization).

Real-World Evaluation

Successfully lifted various types of boxes on the Unitree G1 and executed semantically rich interaction tasks (e.g., striking an "Iron Man" pillar). All quantitative metrics are evaluated in MuJoCo to prevent overfitting to Isaac Gym.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Mocap dependency: Real-world deployment relies on motion capture systems, with marker occlusion being prominent in close-contact tasks (e.g., box pushing). The authors suggest future integration of on-device SLAM and multi-camera fusion (e.g., HTC VIVE Trackers).
  • Open-loop pipeline: The current system is open-loop; iteratively prompting the video model provides a pseudo-closed-loop approximation but is extremely inefficient. True closed-loop operation requires joint fine-tuning of the video generator and motion tracker with physical feedback, but the strongest video models are closed-source APIs, and open-source alternatives (e.g., Wan2.2) lack the physical consistency needed for complex interactions.
  • Task scope: Dexterous hand manipulation is not addressed (the interaction adapter only selects box carrying and pushing tasks); marker orientation extraction is not solved (leading to lower Push Box success rates).
  • Not explicitly stated in the paper: Specific inference frequency latency, full network architecture details, and reward formulations are deferred to Appendix A.

Engineering Insights

  • First verify data retargeting quality: The BFM backbone relies on pink inverse kinematics retargeting, and the keypoint tracker relies on holosoma HOI retargeting; retargeting errors propagate directly to downstream policies. When reproducing, prioritize verifying whether retargeted motions remain physically plausible.
  • The three-stage training order is non-negotiable: Each stage depends on the frozen backbone of the previous stage; skipping pretraining and directly training the keypoint tracker will lose the natural gait prior of the BFM latent space. Note that the latter two stages require injecting 5cm Gaussian noise to simulate 3D tracker noise.
  • Most common pitfall: missing marker orientation: The direct cause of the lower Push Box success rate is the lack of orientation input. When reproducing, if push performance needs improvement, additional solutions for robustly extracting object orientation from videos are required.
  • Reward function design: The exponential kernel reward r = exp(-||x - x̂||²/σ) avoids manual heuristics, but the choice of σ is critical—from the keypoint tracker to the interaction adapter, the marker tracking bandwidth tightens from 0.2 to 0.1, with object pose reward weights as high as 12.0. When reproducing, strictly follow the reward weights and σ values in Table 4.
  • Domain randomization scope: Table 5 provides the complete domain randomization parameters (mass ±20%, friction 0.3-1.5, push forces up to 20N, etc.), which are key to Sim2Real success. Full reproduction is recommended rather than simplification.
