Overview

The Model of collision and self-interference is a critical process node in the URDF modeling and kinematics verification stage of humanoid robots. Its core task is to construct a geometric and logical constraint system that ensures no collisions occur between limbs and the environment, or between limbs themselves, during robot motion. What it truly changes is not the act of "checking whether the robot will hit something," but rather transforming "safe motion" from an empirical judgment into a quantifiable, traceable, and automatically verifiable engineering standard.

Content

What It Is: A Geometric and Logical System Defining "Safety Boundaries" for Robot Motion

The collision and self-interference model is essentially a determination system that couples robot body geometry, environmental geometry, and kinematic constraints. It consists of three parts: collision body geometric representation (convex hull/simplified mesh), collision detection engine (FCL/PyBullet), and collision matrix (defining which link pairs allow or prohibit collisions).

In the humanoid robot development workflow, this model serves a bridging role: upstream, it receives kinematic parameters and link geometry from the URDF modeling stage; downstream, it provides collision constraint inputs for motion planning, trajectory generation, and safety protection logic. It is not an independent simulation tool but a "safety gatekeeper" embedded in the development toolchain—any motion command must pass its verification before being sent to the physical robot.

Why It Exists: The Engineering Paradox of Collision Bodies Being "Too Tight Causing False Positives, Too Loose Causing Missed Detections"

Humanoid robots are typical systems with "high degrees of freedom, strong coupling, and multiple constraints." Directly importing CAD models into a collision detection environment presents three fatal issues: first, CAD models contain extensive decorative geometry (fillets, threads, chamfers), and using them directly for collision detection leads to computational explosion; second, coordinate system definitions in CAD assemblies are often based on design convenience rather than kinematic conventions; third, CAD models are static, while collision detection requires dynamic determination during motion.

The existence of the collision and self-interference model is precisely to address these issues. It reduces computational overhead through geometric simplification (convex hull/simplified mesh), explicitly declares allowed/prohibited collision pairs through the collision matrix, and ensures the model has no false-positive self-collisions under typical actions and no environmental penetration in critical poses through simulation verification. What it truly changes is not the act of "detecting collisions," but turning "safety" from a vague qualitative requirement into a quantifiable KPI.

Principle Breakdown

① Geometric Simplification: Dimensionality Reduction from CAD to Collision Bodies

CAD models contain extensive decorative geometry (fillets, threads, chamfers), and using them directly for collision detection leads to computational explosion. The collision and self-interference model employs two simplification strategies:

  • Convex Hull: Wraps complex concave geometry into a minimal convex hull, and collision detection algorithms have efficient determination methods for convex bodies (e.g., the GJK algorithm). The advantage of convex hulls is fast computation and low false-positive rates, but they introduce "virtual volume"—two links that do not actually touch may be judged as colliding due to hull expansion.
  • Simplified Mesh: Retains key surface features while removing decorative details. Simplified meshes are closer to real geometry but have higher computational overhead than convex hulls.

In engineering practice, a hybrid strategy of "convex hull as primary, simplified mesh as supplementary" is typically adopted: convex hulls for large-scale rapid screening, and simplified meshes for precise determination in critical regions.

② Collision Matrix: Explicitly Declaring "Who Can Touch Whom"

The collision matrix is an N×N boolean matrix (N being the number of links) that defines whether collisions are allowed between any two links. A matrix element of true indicates allowed collision (e.g., adjacent links connected by joints), while false indicates prohibited collision (e.g., between left and right arms). The value of the collision matrix lies in:

  • Reducing the "self-interference" problem from "all-to-all detection" to "critical-pair detection," lowering computational complexity from O(N²) to O(M), where M is the number of prohibited collision pairs (engineering judgment: M is typically much smaller than N²).
  • Explicitly declaring design intent—which parts may approach each other during motion and which must maintain distance.

③ Collision Detection Engine: Division of Labor Between FCL and PyBullet

FCL (Flexible Collision Library) is an efficient collision detection library supporting GJK/EPA algorithms for convex bodies, suitable for large-scale rapid screening. PyBullet provides a complete physics simulation environment supporting rigid-body dynamics, contact force calculation, and visual debugging. In engineering practice, FCL is typically used for offline batch verification, while PyBullet is used for online visual debugging and anomaly reproduction.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The collision and self-interference model is a standard procedure in the humanoid robot development workflow, with primary users including:

  • Motion Planning Engineers: Use the collision model to verify trajectory safety before generating trajectories; perform final confirmation with the collision model after trajectory generation.
  • Simulation Verification Engineers: Load the collision model in the PyBullet environment, execute batch simulations of typical actions (e.g., walking, grasping, squatting), record collision events, and analyze causes.
  • Safety Protection Logic Developers: Integrate the collision model's output into real-time safety monitoring, triggering emergency stops or avoidance when imminent collisions are detected.

Typical application scenarios: Before a robot executes a "dual-arm coordinated carrying" task, engineers use the collision matrix to confirm that the left and right arms will not interfere with each other along the carrying trajectory; before the robot enters a narrow environment, the simplified mesh model is used to verify whether the minimum distance between the body and the environment meets safety thresholds.

Limitations and Boundaries

The collision and self-interference model is not omnipotent; its limitations include:

  1. Precision loss from geometric simplification: Convex hulls introduce "virtual volume," causing false positives; simplified meshes may lose tiny features, causing missed detections. Engineering practice requires a trade-off between computational overhead and precision (corpus: key constraint).
  2. Dynamic limitations of static models: The collision model is based on URDF static geometry and cannot reflect dynamic factors such as elastic deformation and joint clearance during motion.
  3. Uncertainty in environmental models: Environmental geometry typically comes from perception systems and contains measurement errors and dynamic changes; the collision model cannot fully eliminate this uncertainty.
  4. Maintenance cost: After design changes, collision bodies and the collision matrix must be updated synchronously; otherwise, new false positives or missed detections will be introduced (corpus: maintainability constraint).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The finer the collision body, the better"—Wrong. Fine meshes have high computational overhead and are sensitive to minor errors, making them prone to false positives. Engineering practice should prioritize convex hulls for rapid screening and use fine meshes only in critical regions.
  2. "The collision matrix can be omitted"—Wrong. Without a collision matrix, the system performs collision detection on all link pairs, resulting in massive computational overhead and frequent false positives. The collision matrix is a key design for reducing computational load and improving accuracy.
  3. "Passing simulation means real-machine safety"—Wrong. Simulation environments cannot fully replicate real-world factors such as friction, deformation, and sensor noise; passing collision model verification is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.
  4. "The collision model is a one-time effort"—Wrong. Collision bodies and the collision matrix must be updated synchronously after design changes; otherwise, the model will gradually become ineffective (corpus: maintainability constraint).
