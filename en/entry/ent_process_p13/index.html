Overview

Electronics & Power is the 13th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. This stage covers core work packages such as computing platform architecture, PCB design, communication buses, and power management, serving as the physical foundation connecting the mechanical body with intelligent algorithms. What it truly changes is not merely "drawing circuits," but achieving a verifiable balance among the robot's computational power, real-time performance, and energy efficiency under a unified design constraint.

Content

What It Is: Positioning and Responsibilities of P13 in WBS V3

P13 Electronics & Power is situated in the mid-to-late stages of the humanoid robot development process, inheriting upstream mechanical overall design (P3) and system architecture definition, while providing the physical carrier for downstream software integration and full-machine debugging. In WBS V3, P13 expands into several level-3 subtasks, each following a unified five-step engineering action template: input review and target quantification, candidate solution development and evaluation, implementation/prototype/sample fabrication, verification and issue closure, and documentation output and downstream delivery.

Core deliverables of this stage include: computing architecture diagrams, per-node compute/power budgets, safety partitions, schematics, PCBs, BOMs, and DFM reports. These outputs are not mere "drawing stacks" but the single source of truth for downstream software, algorithm, and manufacturing teams—if P13's power budget is inaccurate, the full-machine thermal design fails; if safety partitions are unclear, functional safety certification cannot be obtained.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early humanoid robot development, the electronics and electrical system was often treated as the "final wiring step," leading to three systemic pain points:

First, imbalanced compute distribution. Early approaches tended to pile all computational tasks onto a single high-performance CPU, causing real-time control loops to be preempted by AI inference tasks, with joint response delays reaching tens of milliseconds—making the robot walk as if "drunk." One of P13's core missions is to solve this problem at its source through architectural design.

Second, the "hidden explosion" of power and heat. With 20–40 joint motors across the body, multiple cameras, LiDAR, and compute units operating simultaneously, peak power consumption can reach hundreds of watts. Without a power budget at the design stage, full-machine thermal design is doomed to fail—resulting in either frequency throttling or board burnout.

Third, the conflict between safety and functionality. Functional safety requires physical isolation between monitoring and actuation loops, but early designs often ran safety monitoring functions "conveniently" on the main control chip—if the main controller crashed, safety monitoring failed with it. P13 resolves this contradiction through the architectural principle of "independent safety monitoring."

From a historical perspective, P13 is the checkpoint where "mechatronics" transitions from slogan to engineering reality: what it truly changes is not "circuit design" itself, but elevating the electronics and electrical system from "auxiliary support" to "a design domain as important as mechanical structure."

Principle Breakdown

① Computing Platform Architecture: Division of Labor for Heterogeneous Compute

P13.1.1 Computing platform architecture and selection follows a clear design logic: AI tasks on GPU, real-time control on MCU/FPGA, independent safety monitoring. The essence of this logic is "allocating compute by temporal determinism":

  • GPU domain (e.g., Jetson series) handles non-real-time or weakly real-time tasks such as visual perception, SLAM, and path planning—these tasks are compute-intensive but latency-tolerant (accepting 10–100 ms jitter).
  • MCU/FPGA domain handles strongly real-time control such as joint current loops, velocity loops, and torque loops, with cycle requirements typically at 0.1–1 ms and jitter controlled to microsecond levels.
  • Safety monitoring domain is independent of the above two domains, typically implemented via a separate MCU or hardware logic, monitoring bus voltage, joint temperature, and emergency stop signals—directly cutting power upon anomaly.

The physical basis for this division is that different computational tasks have vastly different "temporal determinism" requirements. GPU scheduling latency is unpredictable, while MCU interrupt response times can be deterministic. In formulaic terms, the end-to-end latency of a real-time control loop is:

\[ T_{\text{loop}} = T_{\text{sensor}} + T_{\text{comm}} + T_{\text{compute}} + T_{\text{actuator}} \]

where \(T_{\text{compute}}\) is the only term that architects can actively control. Reducing \(T_{\text{compute}}\) from 5–20 ms on a GPU to 0.1–0.5 ms on an MCU is the key to whether the entire control loop can remain stable (inferred from corpus data).

② PCB Design: Freezing Architecture onto Copper Foil

P13.1.2 Methods/tools for self-developed or outsourced PCB design include: carrier boards, joint driver boards, sensor interface boards, and safety monitoring boards. The design logic is "solidifying computing, communication, power, and safety functions into reliable hardware," with key constraints being EMC, SI/PI, thermal, space, and manufacturability.

The core contradiction here is the coupling between signal integrity and power integrity. Power MOSFET switching on joint driver boards generates enormous di/dt, which couples through the ground plane into analog signal loops on sensor interface boards, causing encoder reading jumps. Common engineering solutions include:

  • Layered routing: isolating power and signal layers with ground planes
  • Local shielding: routing sensitive analog signals as differential pairs with shielding covers
  • Decoupling capacitor arrays: placing multi-stage decoupling capacitors near power devices

The completion criterion for PCB design is the full set of "schematic, PCB, BOM, and DFM report," where the DFM report directly interfaces with manufacturing to ensure low-cost, repeatable mass production.

③ Five-Step Engineering Actions for Level-3 Subtasks

Each level-3 subtask of P13 (e.g., P13.1.1.1 through P13.1.1.5) follows a unified five-step template, which is essentially the concretization of the "V-model" development process in the electronics domain:

  1. Input review and target quantification: List all upstream inputs and confirm versions, convert acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establish task owners, timelines, and risk registers.
  2. Candidate solution development and evaluation: Develop no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, build an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organize reviews, and freeze the solution.
  3. Implementation/prototype/sample fabrication: Build models/prototypes and record key parameters, execute simulation or prototype validation, and document anomalies and deviations.
  4. Verification and issue closure: Develop test/review plans with pass criteria, execute tests and record raw data, and output issue lists with improvement measures.
  5. Documentation output and downstream delivery: Write documents per templates with raw data references, complete internal reviews and version control, and publish with notifications to downstream dependencies.

The value of this template lies in transforming "design" from a one-off act of inspiration into an auditable, traceable engineering process. Every decision has candidate solution comparisons, every validation has raw data support, and every deliverable has version control.

Horizontal Comparison

The relationship between P13 and adjacent stages can be understood through comparison:

  • With P3 (System Architecture and Mechatronic Overall Design): P3 defines "what joints the robot should have and the torque/speed requirements for each joint," while P13 defines "what chips and circuit boards to use to implement control of these joints." P3 outputs requirements; P13 outputs implementation.
  • With P13.1.2 (Self-developed/Outsourced PCB Design): P13.1.1 determines "which compute nodes to use," while P13.1.2 determines "how these nodes are physically carried." The former is an architectural decision; the latter is physical implementation.
  • With P9 (Software Middleware): P13 provides the hardware platform, while P9 provides the software runtime environment. P13's communication bus selection (e.g., EtherCAT) directly determines P9's real-time ceiling.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The P13 methodology is widely applied in both industry and academia. Taking a typical bipedal humanoid robot as an example:

  • Computing platform: A Jetson series unit is mounted in the head for visual perception, a self-developed carrier board integrating MCU and FPGA is placed in the waist or back, and joint driver boards are located within joint modules.
  • Communication architecture: Joint driver boards communicate with the main controller via EtherCAT bus, sensor interface boards collect IMU, encoder, and torque sensor data, and the safety monitoring board independently monitors bus voltage and emergency stop signals.
  • Power system: Battery output is distributed via a power management board to the compute, drive, and safety domains, with each domain independently powered and filtered to prevent power disturbances from propagating across domains.

The engineering value of this process lies in the five-step template providing clear acceptance criteria and deliverables for each subtask, enabling parallel development across teams—mechanical, electronics, and software teams can align progress within the same WBS framework.

Limitations and Boundaries

The P13 methodology has the following boundary conditions:

First, it does not cover specific chip selection details. The Jetson / Intel NUC / FPGA / MCU mentioned in methods/tools are platform-level options; specific model selection requires consideration of supply chain, cost, and ecosystem maturity, which falls outside the scope of the P13 template's guidance.

Second, it does not solve algorithm-level real-time issues. P13 guarantees that "hardware is capable of completing computation within 0.1–1 ms," but software task scheduling and interrupt priority configuration remain the responsibility of software teams. Hardware capability is a necessary but not sufficient condition.

Third, it may lead to over-design for small robots. For platforms with fewer degrees of freedom, such as wheeled or quadruped robots, the full "GPU + MCU/FPGA + independent safety monitoring" architecture may be cost-prohibitive; in such cases, the safety and compute domain partitioning can be simplified (engineering judgment).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "More compute power is always better"—Wrong. Humanoid robots face extremely tight constraints on power, heat, and weight; over-provisioning compute leads to thermal runaway and reduced battery life. The correct approach is to allocate compute based on task requirements, with GPU handling only non-real-time tasks.
  2. "Safety monitoring can run on the main controller"—Wrong. Functional safety requires physical isolation between monitoring and actuation loops; safety monitoring must operate independently when the main controller crashes. This is the bottom-line architectural principle of P13.
  3. "PCB design is purely hardware work"—Wrong. PCB design requires close collaboration with software teams, especially for interface definitions, interrupt pin assignments, and communication timing, which must be aligned with software teams during the design phase.
  4. "The five-step template is process redundancy"—Wrong. The "candidate solution evaluation" and "verification closure" steps in the template are critical for ensuring design quality; skipping them often results in rework costs far exceeding upfront design costs.
