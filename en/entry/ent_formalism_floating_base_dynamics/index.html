Overview

Floating-Base Dynamics is a mathematical framework for describing the equations of motion of robotic systems whose base (torso) can translate and rotate freely in space. Unlike fixed-base industrial manipulators, the base of a humanoid robot has no direct actuators; its 6 degrees of freedom can only be controlled indirectly through contact forces with the ground, making it inherently an underactuated system. The floating-base dynamics equations directly relate base acceleration to contact forces, serving as the theoretical cornerstone for gait planning, whole-body QP control, and dynamic balance control.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Floating-base dynamics is a formalization method for studying the motion of robotic systems whose base is not mechanically constrained and can move freely in space. It describes the system configuration using a set of generalized coordinates:

\[ \mathbf{q} = (\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}}, \mathbf{R}_{\text{base}}, \mathbf{q}_{\text{joints}}) \]

where \(\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the base position, \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{base}} \in SO(3)\) is the base orientation, and \(\mathbf{q}_{\text{joints}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j}\) are the joint angles. The essential difference from fixed-base robots is that the base's 6 degrees of freedom (3 translational + 3 rotational) have no corresponding actuators; they are passive degrees of freedom.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Before floating-base dynamics emerged, robot dynamics research primarily targeted industrial manipulators—bases bolted to the ground or a fixture, with all degrees of freedom directly driven by motors, making the system fully actuated. Humanoid robots broke this premise: their torso floats in the air with no physical support anchoring it to the inertial frame. Continuing to use fixed-base dynamics modeling would yield incorrect conclusions—for example, it cannot explain why a standing robot's feet must generate specific ground reaction forces, nor can it answer core questions like "how to recover balance through ankle torques when the torso tilts."

The historical significance of floating-base dynamics lies in elevating "contact forces" from constraint conditions to state variables. In fixed-base models, contact forces are byproducts of end-effector interaction with the environment; in floating-base models, contact forces are the only means of maintaining base posture. This shift in perspective transformed humanoid control from "trajectory tracking" to "contact force management," directly giving rise to modern balance control methods such as the ZMP stability criterion, capture point theory, and whole-body QP control.

Principle Breakdown

① Mass Matrix Partitioning: Splitting the System into "Upper" and "Lower" Blocks

The standard form of the floating-base dynamics equation is:

\[ \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{v}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{S}^T \boldsymbol{\tau} + \sum_{c} \mathbf{J}_c^T(\mathbf{q}) \mathbf{F}_c \]

where \(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\) is the \(n \times n\) mass matrix including the floating base and joints, \(\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{v})\) is the Coriolis and centrifugal term, \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})\) is the gravity term, \(\mathbf{S}\) is the selection matrix (mapping joint torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) to generalized coordinate space, acting only on joints, not on the floating base), \(\mathbf{J}_c\) is the Jacobian for the \(c\)-th contact point, and \(\mathbf{F}_c\) is the contact force.

Partitioning the mass matrix into floating base (subscript \(b\)) and joints (subscript \(j\)) blocks, the equation splits into two groups:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{M}_{bb} & \mathbf{M}_{bj} \\ \mathbf{M}_{jb} & \mathbf{M}_{jj} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\mathbf{v}}_b \\ \dot{\mathbf{v}}_j \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C}_b \\ \mathbf{C}_j \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{g}_b \\ \mathbf{g}_j \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \boldsymbol{\tau} \end{bmatrix} + \sum_{c} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{J}_{c,b}^T \\ \mathbf{J}_{c,j}^T \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{F}_c \]

② Mathematical Manifestation of Underactuation: No Torque Term in the Upper Block

Note that the coefficient of \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) in the upper block (floating base) equation is zero—this is the mathematical manifestation of underactuation. The base acceleration \(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_b\) is entirely determined by contact forces \(\mathbf{F}_c\). This means a humanoid robot cannot "directly command" torso motion like an industrial manipulator; it can only indirectly alter torso posture through contact forces between the feet and the ground. This constraint permeates all balance control algorithms: whether ZMP criteria or capture point methods, they all fundamentally answer "how to generate appropriate contact forces to drive the base."

③ Contact Forces as "Inputs" Rather Than "Constraints"

In the floating-base framework, contact forces \(\mathbf{F}_c\) are not passive constraint reactions but variables that must be actively solved. They must satisfy friction cone constraints (to prevent slipping) and unilateral constraints (feet can only push the ground, not pull). These constraints, solved jointly with the floating-base dynamics, constitute the core equality constraints in whole-body QP control.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Whole-Body QP Control: Modern humanoid controllers (e.g., Atlas, Digit platforms) solve a quadratic programming problem at each control cycle, with the floating-base equation serving as the dynamic equality constraint. Task tracking objectives + dynamic equality + friction cone inequalities + torque limits form the core structure identical to modern humanoid controllers. Real-time QP solvers (e.g., OSQP, qpOASES, ProxQP) complete the solution within the control cycle.

Gait Planning: Floating-base dynamics reveals the essence that "base acceleration is entirely determined by contact forces," so the core task of gait planning becomes designing the time sequence of foot contact forces to drive the base along the desired trajectory.

Dynamic Balance Control: Capture point methods transform dynamic balance into a foot placement geometry problem, with floating-base dynamics as the underlying model—by controlling center-of-mass angular momentum and contact forces, the robot recovers from arbitrary disturbance states to stable standing.

Limitations and Boundaries

Floating-base dynamics is an idealized model with the following boundaries:

  1. Rigid body assumption: The equations assume all links are rigid, neglecting structural flexibility. Actual humanoid robot links undergo elastic deformation under high-speed motion, leading to model mismatch (engineering judgment).
  2. Simplified contact models: Point or planar contact assumptions ignore the complex friction distribution and deformation between the foot and the ground.
  3. Computational cost: Mass matrix inversion and Coriolis term computation become significantly expensive at high degrees of freedom; real-time control requires recursive Newton-Euler algorithms or precomputation techniques.
  4. Not applicable to flight phases: When both feet leave the ground (e.g., jumping, flying), the contact force terms vanish, the system becomes fully underactuated, and a switch to the centroidal momentum model is required.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Floating base means free-floating"—Wrong. Floating base means the base has no mechanical constraints, but the robot still interacts with the environment through contact forces. Free-floating (e.g., space robots) is a special case with zero contact forces.
  2. "Underactuated means uncontrollable"—Wrong. Underactuated means the number of actuators is fewer than the number of DOFs, but through indirect control via contact forces, the system can still achieve stable equilibrium—this is the essence of human walking.
  3. "Floating-base and fixed-base equations are interchangeable"—Wrong. In fixed-base equations, \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{I}\) (identity matrix), while in floating-base equations, \(\mathbf{S}\) maps torques only to joint components; the two have fundamentally different structures.
  4. "Contact forces are constraint reactions that don't need active solving"—Wrong. In the floating-base framework, contact forces are variables that must be solved jointly with the equations of motion and must satisfy inequality constraints such as the friction cone.
