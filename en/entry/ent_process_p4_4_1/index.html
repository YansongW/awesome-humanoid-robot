Overview

Life and failure analysis of joints is the first third-level subtask in the joint module and drive system design phase (P4.4 Joint Reliability). It uses tools such as accelerated life testing, vibration testing, wear analysis, and FMEA to estimate the wear and failure modes of joints within the target lifespan, and outputs a life test plan, failure mode analysis, and maintenance interval recommendations. What it truly changes is not the passive maintenance of "repair after failure," but the proactive reliability engineering of "predicting when and why failures will occur during the design phase."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Joint life and failure analysis is an engineering process that, under expected operating conditions, systematically evaluates the life boundaries of robot joint modules, identifies potential failure modes, quantifies failure risks, and develops verification and maintenance strategies. It is not a single test but a complete workflow consisting of input organization, method design, implementation and verification, problem closure, and documentation delivery.

In the WBS V3 hierarchy, this task falls under P4.4 Joint Reliability, numbered P4.4.1, and further expands into five fourth-level subtasks: P4.4.1.1 Input Organization and Target Quantification, P4.4.1.2 Solution/Method Design, P4.4.1.3 Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication, P4.4.1.4 Verification and Problem Closure, and P4.4.1.5 Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery. Each subtask contains several key actions, forming a complete loop from "clarifying what needs to be done" to "delivering to whom it will be used."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Joints in humanoid robots are the subsystem that experiences the most frequent alternating loads and has the most severe failure consequences in the entire machine. A hip joint withstands multiple impact loads per second during walking and may endure instantaneous torques several times body weight during a fall. If a joint fails suddenly during operation, it can cause abnormal motion at best, or lead to loss of control and falls of the entire machine at worst, resulting in property damage or even personal injury.

In traditional industrial robotics, joint life analysis is relatively simple—loads are fixed, motion patterns are predictable, and maintenance intervals are fixed. However, humanoid robots face three unique challenges: highly variable load conditions (walking, running, jumping, being pushed), extremely tight space and weight budgets (making it impossible to simply increase safety factors), and high maintenance costs (the entire machine must be disassembled to replace a joint). These factors dictate that life and failure analysis for humanoid robot joints must be brought forward to the design phase, rather than analyzing after prototype failures occur.

This task holds a starting position within P4.4 Joint Reliability—the life test plan it outputs serves as the basis for subsequent reliability verification, the failure mode analysis serves as input for design improvements, and the maintenance interval recommendations directly guide user operation and after-sales strategies.

Principle Breakdown

① Systematic Identification of Failure Modes: The Structured Thinking of FMEA

FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) is one of the core methods of this task. Starting from the smallest constituent components of the joint, it systematically asks three questions: In what way might this component fail? What consequences does the failure have on the higher-level system? How likely is this failure to occur, and how difficult is it to detect? Through structured tables and scoring (severity, occurrence, detection), risks are quantified and prioritized, allowing high-risk failure modes to be addressed first.

② Physical Basis of Life Estimation: Cumulative Damage from Wear and Fatigue

The core physical mechanism of joint life is cumulative damage. For mechanical wear, the Archard wear law provides the relationship between wear volume, load, and sliding distance:

\[ V = k \frac{F \cdot s}{H} \]

where \(V\) is the wear volume, \(k\) is the wear coefficient, \(F\) is the normal load, \(s\) is the sliding distance, and \(H\) is the material hardness. For fatigue failure, Miner's linear cumulative damage rule linearly superimposes damage under different stress amplitudes, and failure is determined when cumulative damage reaches 1. Together, these two models form the theoretical basis for life estimation.

③ The Mathematical Logic of Accelerated Life Testing

Natural life testing takes too long, so engineering practice employs Accelerated Life Testing (ALT) to shorten test duration under more severe stress conditions. The core assumption is that the acceleration factor \(A_F\) can be described by the Arrhenius model (temperature acceleration) or the inverse power law model (mechanical stress acceleration):

\[ A_F = \left( \frac{S_{\text{acc}}}{S_{\text{use}}} \right)^n \]

where \(S_{\text{acc}}\) is the accelerated stress, \(S_{\text{use}}\) is the use stress, and \(n\) is an exponent related to the material and failure mechanism (engineering judgment). Through the acceleration factor, short-duration test results can be extrapolated to life under normal operating conditions.

Horizontal Comparison

Joint life and failure analysis has a clear division of labor with adjacent tasks. Compared with P4.4 Joint Reliability as a whole, P4.4.1 focuses on life and failure prediction analysis, while P4.4 also includes subsequent stages such as reliability allocation and reliability verification. Compared with the FMEA method itself, FMEA is a general-purpose tool, whereas P4.4.1 is its specific application in the joint context, additionally incorporating physical testing methods such as accelerated life testing, vibration testing, and wear analysis.

At the methodological level, the comparison between accelerated life testing and natural life testing is particularly critical: the former is short in duration and low in cost but carries extrapolation errors; the latter yields accurate results but requires long cycles and high costs. Engineering practice typically relies primarily on accelerated life testing, supplemented by a small number of natural life tests for calibration (engineering judgment).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The primary users of this task are reliability engineers, mechanical design engineers, and test engineers at robotics companies. In the development process, it receives the joint load spectrum output from upstream dynamics analysis and delivers the life test plan and maintenance strategy downstream.

Typical application scenarios include: comparing the life of different reducer, bearing, and seal solutions before joint solution freeze to select a solution that meets the MTBF target; verifying design life through vibration testing and wear analysis during the prototype stage; and optimizing maintenance intervals based on failure mode analysis results during mass production to reduce total lifecycle costs.

Limitations and Boundaries

This task has clear boundaries. It focuses on the life and failure of the joint itself and does not involve system-level reliability analysis of the entire machine (such as redundancy design or system-level FMEA). It depends on the accuracy of upstream inputs—if the load spectrum is inaccurate, life estimation becomes meaningless. Extrapolation results from accelerated life testing carry inherent uncertainty and must be calibrated through engineering judgment and verification testing. Additionally, test cost is a hard constraint, and solution design must balance cost against accuracy.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Once FMEA is done, everything is settled"—Wrong. FMEA is only the front end of risk identification; subsequent verification through testing, design improvements, and maintenance strategies are required to close the loop. The five subtasks of P4.4.1 exist precisely to ensure this loop is not broken.

  2. "Accelerated life test results can be directly extrapolated"—Wrong. The acceleration factor model relies on the assumption that the failure mechanism remains unchanged. If the accelerated stress changes the failure mode (e.g., from wear to thermal aging), the extrapolated results will be severely distorted. Failure mechanism consistency must be confirmed through failure physics analysis.

  3. "Life analysis is a matter for the testing phase"—Wrong. P4.4.1.1 explicitly requires that acceptance criteria be converted into quantifiable KPIs during the design phase. Life analysis must be brought forward to the solution design phase; otherwise, it cannot guide selection and design improvements.
