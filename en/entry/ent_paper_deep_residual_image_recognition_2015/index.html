Summary

Deeper neural networks are more difficult to train. We present a residual learning framework to ease the training of networks that are substantially deeper than those used previously. Institutions per source list: Microsoft Research.

Overview

Kaiming He et al. proposed the residual learning framework (ResNet), which models deep networks as residual functions F(x) + x via identity shortcut connections, solving the optimization degradation problem of extremely deep networks (e.g., 152 layers). This work set new records across ImageNet classification, detection, localization, and COCO detection/segmentation tasks, becoming a milestone foundational architecture in deep learning for vision.

What It Changed

Before ResNet, the vision community generally believed that network depth was key to accuracy, but architectures like VGG and GoogLeNet exhibited a "degradation" phenomenon beyond a certain depth where training error increased rather than decreased—this was not overfitting but optimization difficulty. The authors experimentally proved that simply increasing the number of iterations (3×) could not resolve the degradation, indicating the problem lay in the optimizer's ability to fit deep non-identity mappings. The real change of ResNet was this: it explicitly reformulated the implicit assumption of "letting the network fit the target mapping H(x)" into "letting the network fit the residual H(x)-x," and introduced parameter-free identity shortcut connections. This change seemed minor, yet it enabled networks with hundreds of layers to be stably optimized by SGD for the first time, and the identity connections added no parameters or computational cost—this was the essential difference from contemporaneous Highway networks (which had gating and parameters). It changed the field's understanding of "depth": depth was no longer a training obstacle but a systematically exploitable source of accuracy.

Method Breakdown

Core Building Block

  • Residual block definition: y = F(x, {W_i}) + x, where F is a stack of convolutional layers (e.g., two 3×3 convolutions), x is the input, and the identity shortcut connection directly adds them
  • When dimensions do not match: y = F(x, {W_i}) + W_s x, where W_s is a linear projection (1×1 convolution), used only when increasing dimensions

Architecture Design Rules

  • Layers with the same output feature map size maintain the same number of filters; when the feature map size is halved, the number of filters is doubled
  • Downsampling is performed directly by convolutions with stride=2, and the network ends with global average pooling + 1000-way fully connected layer + softmax
  • Bottleneck design (ResNet-50/101/152): each residual function uses a 1×1→3×3→1×1 three-layer stack, where the 1×1 layers handle dimension reduction and restoration, and the 3×3 layer acts as the bottleneck (with small input/output dimensions), significantly reducing computational cost

Key Training Configurations

  • ImageNet: SGD, mini-batch=256, lr=0.1 (divided by 10 on plateau), trained for 60×10^4 iterations, weight decay=0.0001, momentum=0.9, no dropout
  • BN applied after each convolution and before activation; weight initialization follows the method in [13]
  • CIFAR-10: mini-batch=128, lr=0.1 (divided by 10 at 32k and 48k iterations), terminated at 64k iterations; the 110-layer network uses warm-up lr=0.01 for the first 400 iterations

Detection/Segmentation Adaptation

  • Replace VGG-16 in Faster R-CNN/FCN with ResNet-101, leaving all other detection/segmentation techniques unchanged
  • After pretraining, freeze BN layers (statistics computed on ImageNet); during fine-tuning, BN becomes a linear activation with constant offset, reducing memory consumption
  • Fine-tuning: lr=0.001, 240k iterations, mini-batch=1 (detection) or 8 (segmentation), weight decay=0.0001, momentum=0.9

Key Innovations

  1. Identity Shortcut Connections: Unlike the gated shortcuts in Highway networks, identity connections are parameter-free and never closed, allowing information to always pass through completely. This enables the residual function F to learn "corrections" rather than complete mappings, mechanistically alleviating gradient vanishing and optimization difficulties in deep networks. Appendix experiments show that identity connections are crucial for bottleneck architectures—if replaced with projections, time complexity and model size double.

  2. Response Strength Analysis of Residual Functions: The authors measured the standard deviation of outputs at each layer (after BN, before nonlinearity) and found that ResNet layer responses are generally smaller than those of plain networks, and the deeper the network, the smaller the per-layer response. This directly validates the hypothesis that "residual functions are closer to zero," providing mechanistic evidence for the effectiveness of residual learning.

  3. Depth as a Universal Accuracy Lever: In detection/segmentation tasks, merely replacing the backbone (VGG-16→ResNet-101) without modifying any detection/segmentation techniques improved COCO detection mAP@[.5,.95] from 21.9% to 27.6% (a relative improvement of approximately 26%), and segmentation mIoU from 24.4% to 28.6%. This proves that the benefits of deep representations transfer losslessly to downstream tasks without task-specific design.

Competition Results

  • ILSVRC 2015 detection: ensemble model test mAP=62.1%, surpassing the runner-up by 8.5 points
  • ILSVRC 2015 localization: top-5 localization error of 9.0%, a 64% reduction relative to ILSVRC 14 results
  • COCO 2015 detection: ensemble of 3 models test-dev mAP@[.5,.95]=37.4%

Boundaries and Limitations

  • The 1202-layer ResNet on CIFAR-10 had a test error (7.93%) higher than the 110-layer network (6.43%), attributed by the authors to overfitting (19.4M parameters vs 50k training images), but attempts with strong regularization such as maxout/dropout showed no significant gains; optimizing over-parameterized networks remains an open problem
  • The root cause of the degradation problem was not fully explained; the authors only speculated it was related to optimization difficulty without providing theoretical analysis
  • Multi-scale training was not performed (only multi-scale testing), and multi-scale testing was applied only to the Fast R-CNN step, not to the RPN
  • The paper does not report precise training time and hardware configuration; code was not released
  • The "28% relative improvement" in the abstract is inconsistent with the main text data (approximately 26%); the authors acknowledged in the appendix that this number is not directly reproducible

Engineering Insights

  • Reproduction priority: First verify the BN freezing strategy—during detection fine-tuning, BN statistics must be frozen from the pretrained model; otherwise, small-batch training (detection mini-batch=1) causes severe fluctuations in BN statistics, which is the most common pitfall
  • Identity connections are not replaceable: In bottleneck architectures, replacing identity shortcuts with projections doubles time complexity and model size while providing only marginal accuracy gains (differences among options A/B/C in Table 10 are <1%); parameter-free identity connections should be the engineering priority
  • Match depth with data volume: The lesson from the 1202-layer network overfitting on 50k images shows that extremely deep networks require correspondingly large data or strong regularization; with limited data, around 110 layers is a reasonable choice for CIFAR-10-scale problems
  • Extremely low downstream transfer cost: Detection/segmentation tasks achieve significant gains by merely replacing the backbone without modifying task heads; pretraining + frozen BN + fine-tuning is the standard pipeline and can be reused for other vision tasks
  • Computational budget reference: The 152-layer ResNet has 11.3B FLOPs, lower than VGG-16 (15.3B) and VGG-19 (19.6B); increased depth does not necessarily increase computational burden, and the bottleneck design is key
