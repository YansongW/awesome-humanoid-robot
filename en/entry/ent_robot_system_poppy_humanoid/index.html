Summary

An open-source 3D-printed humanoid robot created by Inria's Flowers team, about 84 cm and 3.5 kg with 25 degrees of freedom including a 5-DoF bio-inspired spine, driven by 25 ROBOTIS Dynamixel servos and programmed in Python or Scratch via the pypot library, designed for education and HRI research.

Overview

Poppy Humanoid is an open-source 3D-printed humanoid robot initiated in 2012 by the Flowers team at Inria Bordeaux Sud-Ouest (in partnership with Ensta ParisTech), stemming from Matthieu Lapeyre's doctoral thesis (supervised by Pierre-Yves Oudeyer) and funded by the ERC Explorer grant. The community is now maintained by the non-profit organization Poppy Station. The full robot stands approximately 84 cm tall and weighs 3.5 kg, with 25 degrees of freedom (including a 5-DoF fully actuated bio-inspired spine and a bent-knee leg configuration) (source: research archive poppy-humanoid.md, same below). What truly sets it apart is not its locomotion capabilities as a humanoid, but rather its introduction of "backdrivable, teachable, 3D-printable" transparency into education and human-robot interaction research—transforming a humanoid robot from a closed industrial exhibit into an open-source platform that any lab can disassemble, rewrite, and even redesign.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Poppy Humanoid is a complete humanoid robot platform combining open-source hardware + open-source software. On the hardware side, all structural components are 3D-printed and driven by 25 ROBOTIS Dynamixel smart actuators; on the software side, it is based on a Raspberry Pi and the proprietary pypot Python library, offering a full interface from low-level joint control to high-level behavior orchestration. In terms of licensing, the hardware is CC BY-SA 4.0, the software is GPLv3, and the documentation is CC-BY-SA 4.0.

It falls under the robot system (robot_system) type, positioned at the midstream value chain level—it does not manufacture motors or sensors but integrates off-the-shelf smart actuators, open-source controllers, and 3D-printed structural parts into a reproducible, modifiable complete robot solution. Unlike industrial humanoids (e.g., Unitree H1) that pursue dynamic performance, Poppy Humanoid's core design goal is education and HRI (human-robot interaction) research: enabling users to understand every joint, every line of code, and even redesign the robot's body.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before Poppy Humanoid emerged (around 2012), humanoid research platforms faced a dilemma: industrial-grade platforms (e.g., Honda ASIMO, Aldebaran NAO) were powerful but expensive and had closed software—a single NAO cost over €10,000 and did not allow hardware modifications; DIY platforms (e.g., robots built with hobby servos) were cheap but lacked a unified software stack and community support, leading every lab to reinvent the wheel.

Poppy Humanoid precisely filled this gap: 3D printing reduced structural costs, Dynamixel smart actuators eliminated the need for motor driver circuit design, the pypot library unified the software interface, and open-source licenses permitted all modifications. Its historical significance lies in proving that "a complete humanoid robot can be forked, modified, and redistributed like open-source software"—a philosophy that later influenced next-generation open-source humanoid platforms such as ToddlerBot (Stanford, 2025).

Principle Breakdown

① Smart Actuator Bus: The "Nervous System" of 25 Joints

All 25 degrees of freedom of Poppy Humanoid are driven by ROBOTIS Dynamixel smart actuators. Each actuator integrates a motor, gearbox, absolute encoder, and driver circuit internally, daisy-chained via a TTL serial bus. This means the external controller (Raspberry Pi) needs only a single signal wire to control all 25 joints simultaneously, eliminating the need for individual wiring per joint. The actuators handle closed-loop position, velocity, and torque control internally, while the external controller only sends command packets.

② Compliant Mode: The Core of Backdrivable Teaching

Dynamixel actuators support a "compliant mode"—in this state, the motor outputs no torque, and joints can be freely moved by external forces. The core selling point of Poppy Humanoid's teaching design is precisely "hands-on" programming by demonstration: the teacher places the robot in compliant mode, manually poses the target posture, the software records each joint angle, and then replays it as a motion sequence. This capability allows students with zero programming background to teach the robot a new movement within minutes.

③ pypot Library: The Abstraction Layer from Joints to Behaviors

pypot is a Python library developed by the Poppy team that encapsulates Dynamixel bus communication, kinematics calculations, and behavior orchestration. It offers two types of interfaces: low-level interfaces directly read and write joint angles, velocities, and torques; high-level interfaces support defining "behaviors" (e.g., walking, waving) and binding them to sensor events. pypot also integrates support for Scratch graphical programming, enabling even elementary school students to control the robot by dragging and dropping blocks.

Poppy Humanoid's unique advantages include: a 5-DoF bio-inspired spine (most platforms of similar size have only 2–3 waist degrees of freedom) and compliant backdrivable teaching capability; main disadvantages include: the 2014 hardware design is now outdated (no battery, limited FSR sensor precision), and community maintenance has largely stalled (last push to the main repository was 2021-12-06, official documentation last updated 2022-12-20).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

The Poppy community website explicitly categorizes users into three groups: educators (teaching computer science through robots), artists (using robots to "bring ideas to life"), and scientists and makers (open-source, hackable nature is a core advantage for experimentation and knowledge sharing). Specific application scenarios include:

  • Classroom teaching: Poppy Torso (a simplified version of Poppy Humanoid) is designed as a tabletop teaching platform, paired with Scratch graphical programming, suitable for classroom and FabLab settings.
  • HRI research: The Flowers team itself uses Poppy Humanoid to study robotic models of infant motor development—simulating how infants explore their bodies and environment through compliant backdriving and programmable impedance.
  • Artistic performance: Artists leverage its modular and parametric design to modify the body structure (e.g., swapping arms, adding decorations) and choreograph dances or interactive installations.
  • Engineering education: Multiple engineering schools use it in robotics courses, allowing students to practice kinematics calibration, trajectory planning, and force control.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Hardware aging: The 2014 design is now outdated—no battery power (limited mobility), limited FSR force sensor precision, and low-resolution PS Eye cameras. It needs to be verified whether it remains a suitable starting point for new research projects.
  2. Stalled community: The GitHub main repository's last push was 2021-12-06, and official documentation was last updated 2022-12-20, with maintenance largely halted. New users may face compatibility issues between dependency libraries and newer Raspberry Pi versions.
  3. Non-trivial cost: The hardware cost of approx. €7,500–8,000 (with motors approx. €5,000) mainly comes from 25 high-end actuators, which is not friendly to individual hobbyists—"open-source" does not mean "cheap."
  4. Limited locomotion: High gear-ratio actuators plus 3D-printed structural parts preclude dynamic walking or jumping; its value lies in teaching and interaction rather than locomotion performance.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Poppy Humanoid is a toy"—Wrong. It is a complete, programmable humanoid research platform used by institutions like Inria for serious HRI and developmental robotics research; "looking like a toy" is precisely its design goal—lowering psychological barriers so non-expert users are willing to interact with it.
  2. "3D printing = cheap"—Wrong. 3D-printed structural parts are indeed inexpensive, but the 25 Dynamixel actuators (approx. €5,000) are the major cost. What is open-sourced is the design, not the materials.
  3. "Compliant mode = no torque control"—Wrong. Compliant mode is just one operating mode of Dynamixel actuators (zero torque output); Poppy also supports torque control mode, allowing programmable joint torque output for research such as impedance control.
  4. "The Poppy project is dead"—Partially true. The main repository has indeed stopped maintenance, but the Poppy Station non-profit organization still maintains the community and documentation, and its design philosophy (open-source, printable, backdrivable) has profoundly influenced subsequent open-source humanoid platforms.
