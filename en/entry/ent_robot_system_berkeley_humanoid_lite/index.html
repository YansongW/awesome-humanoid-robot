Summary

An open-source 0.8 m, 16 kg humanoid robot from UC Berkeley with 22 active degrees of freedom and a sub-5,000 USD bill of materials, built around self-designed quasi-direct-drive actuators with 3D-printed cycloidal reducers, trained in NVIDIA Isaac Lab with zero-shot sim-to-real reinforcement learning.

Overview

Berkeley Humanoid Lite is an open-source lightweight humanoid robot initiated by UC Berkeley's Hybrid Robotics Group (Koushil Sreenath's team) and the SLICE Lab. It is part of the BAIR Commons HIC repository, with the paper arXiv:2504.17249 (2025-04). The robot stands 0.8 m tall, weighs 16 kg, and has 22 active degrees of freedom (6 per leg, 5 per arm). The total BOM cost is approximately $4,312 when sourced in the US and approximately $3,236 when sourced in China, with the official marketing claim of "under $5,000" (source: research archive berkeley-humanoid-lite.md, same below). What it truly changes is not the performance ceiling of humanoid robots, but the barrier to entry for humanoid robotics research—compressing a reproducible, modifiable, and extensible complete platform into a desktop 3D printer and an e-commerce shopping list.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Berkeley Humanoid Lite is a fully open-source, modular 3D-printed, lightweight humanoid robot research platform with a total cost under $5,000. Its core design philosophy is "reproducibility first": all structural components can be manufactured with a standard desktop FDM printer (PLA), all electronic and mechanical parts can be sourced through e-commerce platforms, and all design files, embedded code, and training/deployment frameworks are released under open-source licenses. The code uses the MIT License, while CAD and other assets use CC BY-SA 4.0.

From a system architecture perspective, it is an extension of the quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuator approach: a self-developed 3D-printed cycloidal reducer combined with high-torque-density brushless motors significantly lowers the cost and manufacturing barrier of traditional metal harmonic reducers while maintaining force transparency. The robot has 22 active degrees of freedom (6 per leg, 5 per arm), trains reinforcement learning policies based on NVIDIA Isaac Lab, and achieves zero-shot policy transfer from simulation to real hardware.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the field of humanoid robotics research, open-source platforms before 2024 faced a structural contradiction: either expensive or weak. Commercial humanoid robots (such as Unitree H1, Tesla Optimus) offer strong performance but are costly, closed-source, and opaque—researchers cannot modify hardware or delve into low-level control logic. Meanwhile, low-cost open-source platforms often use simple servos or high-reduction-ratio rigid joints, whose dynamic performance is insufficient to support research topics like walking and jumping. This contradiction has long confined humanoid robotics research to a few laboratories with expensive hardware resources.

The historical positioning of Berkeley Humanoid Lite is that it is the first open-source platform to bring a "reproducible quasi-direct-drive humanoid" to under $5,000. It takes a path completely different from commercial products: rather than pursuing performance limits, it pursues "research usability"—allowing an ordinary laboratory to assemble a humanoid robot capable of running reinforcement learning policies in about one week, with a BOM cost of approximately $4,312 (US sourcing) or approximately $3,236 (China sourcing). Its emergence pulls "humanoid robotics research" back from a resource-intensive field to an algorithm-intensive one.

Principle Breakdown

① 3D-Printed Cycloidal Reducer: A Torque Density Solution in Plastic

The core of a quasi-direct-drive actuator is low-reduction-ratio (typically 3:1–10:1) transmission. Berkeley Humanoid Lite chooses a cycloidal structure over planetary gears because of the inherent defects of plastic materials: FDM-printed spur gears are prone to tooth breakage under repeated impact loads, whereas cycloidal reducers distribute loads through simultaneous multi-tooth engagement, allowing them to withstand higher torque at the same external dimensions. This is a typical engineering decision of "compensating for material shortcomings with structural design."

② Quasi-Direct-Drive Architecture: Current Equals Torque

The robot uses two specifications of self-developed quasi-direct-drive actuators: 6512 (10 units) and 5010 (12 units). Taking the 6512 as an example, its BOM is approximately $188 (US) / \(157 (China), with core components being the MAD Components M6C12 150KV drone brushless motor (\)129) and the ST B-G431B-ESC driver board. Due to the low reduction ratio, the output torque is approximately proportional to the motor current:

\[ \tau_{\text{out}} \approx G \, k_t \, I_m - J_{\text{eq}} \ddot{\theta}_m \]

where G is the reduction ratio, k_t is the motor torque constant, I_m is the q-axis current, and J_eq is the equivalent moment of inertia. The second term is the inertia compensation term. Because the reduction ratio is small, friction and backlash nonlinearities are also small, making this estimate sufficiently accurate in engineering practice—this is the entire secret behind "force control without torque sensors."

③ Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real: Isaac Lab Training Pipeline

The locomotion controller is trained based on NVIDIA Isaac Lab using reinforcement learning (RL) methods. Training is completed in simulation, and the policy is deployed directly to real hardware without any fine-tuning on real data. The key to achieving zero-shot transfer lies in "domain matching" between simulation and reality: actuator latency, friction torque, and communication latency (CAN 2.0 bus at 1 Mbps, actuators and IMU at 250 Hz) are all modeled in simulation. This reflects the engineering judgment that "the sim-to-real gap is not filled by tuning parameters, but by designing it into the simulation from the start."

Berkeley Humanoid Lite is not positioned to compete with commercial products on performance, but to fill the gap of "open-source + reproducible + quasi-direct-drive." Its cost advantage comes from two dimensions: first, 3D-printed structural components replace metal machining; second, mass-produced components such as drone brushless motors are used instead of custom servo motors. This is the engineering judgment of "using supply chain economies of scale to replace customization."

Who Uses It · Application Cases

  • Academic Research: The UC Berkeley team itself uses it to validate reinforcement learning walking policies, demonstrating the feasibility of zero-shot policy transfer from simulation to real hardware (paper arXiv:2504.17249).
  • Teaching and Courses: With an assembly time of approximately 3 days and low BOM cost, it is suitable as a humanoid robot experimental platform for robotics courses (engineering judgment).
  • Community Collaboration: The GitHub repository HybridRobotics/Berkeley-Humanoid-Lite has approximately 1,417 stars / 215 forks (snapshot as of 2026-07-01, active), with a Discord community and WeChat group supporting user feedback, issue submission, and code contributions.
  • Rapid Algorithm Iteration: Fully open-source design files and training frameworks allow researchers to modify hardware (e.g., changing actuator specifications) or algorithms (e.g., modifying reward functions) and quickly validate results.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited Performance Ceiling: The torque density and durability of the 3D-printed cycloidal reducer are lower than those of metal harmonic reducers, and the robot's 16 kg weight also limits dynamic performance. It is suitable for research scenarios such as walking and jogging, but not for heavy-load manipulation or high-speed, highly dynamic motion (engineering judgment).
  2. Plastic Component Durability: Although the cycloidal structure optimizes load distribution, the fatigue life of PLA material under long-term cyclic loading remains a weakness. The design team conducted extensive durability testing, but long-term reliability needs to be confirmed independently.
  3. Limited Computational Resources: The main controller is an Intel N95 mini PC (approx. $129) with limited computing power, unable to run large-scale vision-language models or complex perception algorithms. This restricts its application in embodied intelligence directions (e.g., VLA models).
  4. Basic Sensor Configuration: It is equipped only with a BNO085 IMU, with no depth camera or LiDAR. Teleoperation relies on SteamVR base stations + controllers, and perception capabilities lag significantly behind commercial platforms.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "3D-printed reducer = toy"—Wrong. The cycloidal structure distributes loads through simultaneous multi-tooth engagement, and at low reduction ratios (quasi-direct-drive), its torque density and durability are sufficient to support walking tasks. The design team conducted extensive durability testing to eliminate concerns about the reliability of plastic components.
  2. "Quasi-direct-drive = direct-drive"—Wrong. It has a reducer (cycloidal); without it, the motor torque would be insufficient. The point is "low enough to just preserve transparency." This follows the QDD approach validated by the MIT Cheetah series.
  3. "Zero-shot sim-to-real = simulation is realistic enough"—Not entirely. The implementation of zero-shot transfer depends both on the accuracy of simulation modeling and on the robustness of the policy itself (e.g., domain randomization). Berkeley Humanoid Lite's contribution lies in open-sourcing the entire pipeline, allowing researchers to reproduce and improve it.
  4. "Low cost = poor performance"—Context matters. In research tasks such as walking and RL algorithm validation, its performance is sufficient to support scientific research; however, in industrial-grade loads or long-duration continuous operation scenarios, it indeed falls short of commercial platforms.
