Overview

Figure 02 is the second-generation general-purpose humanoid robot released by Figure AI in August 2024, designed for industrial manufacturing and logistics scenarios, and has been deployed on a pilot basis at the BMW Spartanburg plant. It is equipped with the in-house developed Helix VLA (Vision-Language-Action) model, capable of controlling the upper body at 200 Hz and achieving zero-shot grasping of thousands of unseen objects. What it truly changes is not the locomotion capability of "how fast a robot can walk," but the practical path of "whether a robot can collaborate with humans on the factory floor."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Figure 02 is a general-purpose humanoid robot, belonging to the robot_system entity class. Its design goal is not a research platform for the laboratory, but a commercial product that directly enters industrial manufacturing and logistics scenarios to work alongside human workers. The entire machine features a matte black appearance and integrated cable routing, with all cables housed inside the torso to avoid snagging or tangling in factory environments.

From a system architecture perspective, Figure 02 comprises three core subsystems: the perception system (6 RGB cameras and multimodal sensors), the computing system (dual NVIDIA RTX GPU modules), and the decision-making system (the in-house developed Helix VLA model). These three work together to achieve an end-to-end closed loop from environmental perception and semantic understanding to action execution.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The humanoid robot industry has long faced a "valley of death": prototype robots that can run and jump in the laboratory become fragile, inefficient, and difficult to maintain once they enter real factories. Figure 02 exists precisely to bridge this gap.

Pain Point 1: Insufficient task generalization capability. Traditional industrial robots can only execute pre-programmed fixed actions; changing a workpiece or shifting a position causes them to "crash." Figure 02 achieves zero-shot grasping of thousands of unseen objects through the Helix VLA model, meaning it does not need to be retrained for each new object.

Pain Point 2: Questionable safety in human-robot collaboration. Factory environments are full of dynamic changes—workers moving around, stacked materials, and varying lighting conditions. Figure 02's 6 RGB cameras and multimodal sensors support all-weather environmental perception, and with a 200 Hz upper-body control frequency, it can respond to unexpected situations within milliseconds.

Pain Point 3: Deployment cost and maintenance complexity. Compared to its predecessor Figure 01, Figure 02's dual NVIDIA RTX GPU computing modules provide approximately 3 times the on-device inference capability (estimated based on corpus data), meaning more computation is completed locally, reducing reliance on cloud computing power and lowering the complexity of factory network deployment.

From a historical positioning perspective, Figure 02 sits at a critical juncture where humanoid robots transition from "showpieces" to "productivity tools." It chose automotive manufacturing as its first deployment scenario, partnering with BMW to validate processes such as chassis assembly and material handling. This strategy contrasts with Tesla Optimus's approach of targeting its own factories—Figure AI has chosen the "external customer validation" path.

Principle Breakdown

① Helix VLA: A Unified Decision Framework for Vision-Language-Action

The core intelligence of Figure 02 comes from the Helix VLA (Vision-Language-Action) model. The essence of VLA is compressing visual perception, language understanding, and action generation into a single neural network, where the input is camera images and natural language instructions, and the output is a sequence of joint actions.

Figure 02's Helix model adopts a "dual-system" architecture: one system handles semantic understanding (comprehending the meaning of "place that bolt onto the tray"), while the other handles action generation (converting semantics into specific joint trajectories). This division of labor allows Figure 02 to both understand abstract instructions and execute fine-grained operations.

② 200 Hz Control Frequency: The Key to Dexterous Upper-Body Manipulation

Control frequency determines the robot's response speed to dynamic environments. Figure 02's upper-body control frequency reaches 200 Hz, meaning control commands are updated every 5 milliseconds. This frequency is critical for fine operations such as grasping and assembly—unexpected events like dropped objects or workpiece shifts can be quickly corrected.

③ Dual-GPU On-Device Inference: An Engineering Choice for Computing Downshift

Figure 02 is equipped with dual NVIDIA RTX GPU modules, providing approximately 3 times the on-device inference capability of Figure 01 (estimated based on corpus data). The advantage of on-device inference lies in low latency and high reliability—it does not depend on network connectivity and will not "stutter" due to factory network fluctuations. This is a hard requirement in industrial scenarios: a single pause on an assembly line can result in losses of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Who Is Using It: Application Cases

The BMW Spartanburg plant is Figure 02's most critical validation scenario. This plant is one of BMW's largest production bases globally, where Figure 02 is responsible for processes such as chassis assembly and material handling that involve human collaboration. Choosing automotive manufacturing as the first deployment scenario is reasonable: automotive production lines have a high degree of task standardization and repetition, and the factory environment is relatively controllable, making it suitable for validating the robot's reliability and safety.

From a supply chain perspective, Figure 02's upstream core components and technology sources include: NVIDIA GPU computing modules (computing platform), the in-house developed Helix VLA model (decision-making system), OpenAI voice interaction (human-machine interface), and 6×RGB cameras with depth perception modules (perception system). Downstream application customers currently focus primarily on BMW manufacturing bases.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Battery life bottleneck. A battery life of approximately 5 hours means Figure 02 cannot support continuous all-day operation and requires shift-based charging or battery replacement. This is a clear efficiency shortfall in factory environments operating two or three shifts.

② Limited walking speed. A walking speed of approximately 1.2 m/s is close to human walking pace, but it may be insufficient in logistics scenarios requiring rapid movement. It is better suited for "fixed-point operations" than "large-area inspections."

③ Price barrier. The industry estimate of approximately 130,000 USD per unit (third-party estimate) means only large enterprises can afford batch procurement. Small and medium-sized factories will find it difficult to bear this cost in the short term.

④ Scenario boundaries. The currently validated scenarios are concentrated in automotive manufacturing. For more complex unstructured environments (such as construction sites or home settings), Figure 02's generalization capability has not yet been fully verified.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Figure 02 is a general-purpose robot that can do anything"—Wrong. Its design goal is explicitly directed at industrial manufacturing and logistics scenarios, with its degrees of freedom configuration, payload capacity, and battery life all optimized around this goal. Having it serve dishes or do housework is neither the design intent nor necessarily more efficient than specialized robots.

  2. "A 200 Hz control frequency means the entire machine responds quickly"—Inaccurate. 200 Hz is the upper-body control frequency; the control frequency for walking and full-body coordination may not be the same. The high-frequency control of the upper body serves fine manipulation, while walking control focuses more on stability and energy efficiency.

  3. "The VLA model gives Figure 02 general intelligence"—This requires caution. Helix VLA achieves zero-shot grasping of thousands of unseen objects, but this is still generalization at the "manipulation level," and there remains a considerable gap from true general intelligence (such as autonomously planning complex tasks or understanding abstract goals).

  4. "Figure 02 is already in mass production"—Inaccurate. Its status is enterprise pilot/limited deployment, and there is still a distance to large-scale mass production. The 130,000 USD price also indicates that it remains in a high-cost phase.
