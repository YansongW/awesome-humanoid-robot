Overview

The Livox Mid-360 LiDAR is a 360° hybrid solid-state LiDAR launched by Livox Technology, employing non-repetitive scanning (solid-state rotation) technology. It is specifically designed for robot navigation, SLAM, and blind-spot filling. With a weight of 265 g, dimensions of 65 mm × 65 mm × 60 mm, and a typical ranging distance of 150 m (@10% reflectivity), it has become a mainstream external perception option balancing performance and cost in humanoid and mobile robotics. What it truly changes is not "how far LiDAR can detect," but "whether a robot can simultaneously obtain surround-view and high-density point clouds on a compact body."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The Livox Mid-360 is a non-repetitive scanning LiDAR in Livox Technology's Mid series. Unlike traditional mechanical rotating LiDARs (such as the Velodyne VLP-16), it employs a wedge prism scanning scheme, where the rotation and refraction of the prism achieve irregular beam coverage within the field of view, accumulating high-density point clouds over short periods. Its horizontal field of view (FOV) is 360°, vertical FOV is 59°, and typical ranging distance is 150 m at 10% reflectivity (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5 Commercial LiDAR Product Comparison Table).

Within Livox's product line, the Mid-360 is positioned as a robotics 360° LiDAR, differentiated from the automotive-grade HAP/Horizon and surveying-grade Avia. It is officially described as "Small But Mighty," emphasizing omnidirectional perception capability within a compact footprint (source: Livox official product page).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before the Mid-360, robots seeking 360° surround-view point clouds were almost entirely dependent on mechanical rotating LiDARs. While products like the Velodyne VLP-16 and Ouster OS1 were mature with complete FOV coverage, they suffered from three structural pain points:

  1. High size and weight costs: Mechanical rotating structures require motors to spin the entire transmitter/receiver module; components such as housings, bearings, and slip rings inflate size and weight, making deployment in humanoid robot heads or chests difficult.
  2. Persistently high costs: The machining and assembly tolerances for precision mechanical rotating parts are extremely demanding, keeping 16-line mechanical LiDAR unit prices at the multi-thousand-dollar level for years.
  3. Reliability affected by vibration: Exposed rotating components face challenges in lifespan and stability under continuous vibration from robot walking and running.

The Mid-360's "solid-state rotation" approach—no exposed rotating motor, with beam deflection achieved via internal prisms—circumvents these issues at the physical level. It compresses 360° surround-view capability into 265 g and 65 mm × 65 mm × 60 mm dimensions while lowering the price to the consumer/entry-industrial range. This makes placing the primary LiDAR in a humanoid robot's head/chest an engineering-feasible option rather than something confined to research prototypes (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5).

Principle Breakdown

① Non-Repetitive Scanning: Trading Time for Density

Mechanical rotating LiDARs produce fixed horizontal beam patterns, resulting in sparse vertical point clouds (e.g., VLP-16 has only 16 lines). The Mid-360's wedge prism scanning differs: prism rotation causes the beam to trace Lissajous-like patterns within the FOV, covering increasingly dense areas over time. This means:

  • Single-frame point cloud density is low, but multi-frame accumulation yields extremely high density—particularly advantageous for static scene mapping;
  • For dynamic scenes, algorithms (such as the open-source LIO-Livox) can fuse IMU data for motion compensation, enabling robust LiDAR-inertial odometry (source: Livox open-source algorithm page).

② Ranging Principle: ToF and Pulse Time-of-Flight

The Mid-360 uses Time-of-Flight (ToF) ranging. The laser emits pulses, the receiver records the echo time difference, and distance is given by:

\[ R = \frac{c \, \Delta t}{2} \]

where \(c\) is the speed of light and \(\Delta t\) is the time interval between transmission and reception. The ToF approach is favorable for long-range ranging and is unaffected by target color (unlike structured light), enabling a typical ranging distance of 150 m even at 10% low reflectivity (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5).

③ FOV and Blind Zone Design

The Mid-360's horizontal FOV is 360° and vertical FOV is 59°. Compared to the common ±15° vertical range of mechanical rotating radars (e.g., VLP-16), the 59° vertical coverage allows robots to simultaneously perceive the ground, forward obstacles, and overhead structures (such as door frames and shelves)—critical for humanoid robot stair climbing and indoor navigation. The combination of vertical FOV and horizontal 360° makes it frequently used as a blind-spot-filling LiDAR—completing the blind zones of primary sensors such as cameras or forward-facing LiDARs (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5 terminology explanation).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

According to Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5 and the Livox official application scenarios page, typical deployments of the Mid-360 include:

  • Quadruped robots: Leveraging 360° surround-view and lightweight (265 g) advantages, deployed at the front/rear or top of the body for indoor/outdoor SLAM and autonomous navigation.
  • Cleaning robots: Serving as the primary LiDAR or blind-spot-filling LiDAR, covering close-range obstacle avoidance and edge-following cleaning for floor scrubbers.
  • AMRs (Autonomous Mobile Robots): In warehouse and factory environments, the Mid-360's 59° vertical FOV simultaneously perceives shelves, ground, and obstacles, supporting navigation in high-density storage scenarios.
  • Humanoid robots: The Wiki explicitly notes that humanoid robots typically place compact solid-state/hybrid solid-state LiDARs on the head, chest, or shoulders for mid-to-short-range 3D mapping and obstacle avoidance; the Mid-360's size and weight make it a candidate for this layout (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Sparse single-frame point clouds: Non-repetitive scanning yields lower single-frame point cloud density than mechanical multi-line radars at similar price points, limiting real-time detection capability for high-speed moving targets. Engineering judgment: if the application requires high-frequency (>10 Hz) dense point clouds for target tracking, assess whether requirements are met.
  2. Limited ranging ceiling: 150 m (@10% reflectivity) is sufficient for robotics scenarios but far below automotive-grade HAP (180 m) or Avia (190 m); unsuitable for high-speed long-range autonomous driving forward perception.
  3. No built-in inertial navigation: The Mid-360 does not output attitude data by itself; fusion with an IMU (e.g., via the LIO-Livox algorithm) is required for robust odometry. The Wiki emphasizes that humanoid robot selection must consider hard synchronization capability with IMU/Camera (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5).
  4. Environmental robustness: The Wiki notes that selection must consider sunlight/interference robustness—under strong sunlight or with multiple LiDARs interfering with each other, point cloud quality may degrade (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The Mid-360 is a purely solid-state LiDAR"—inaccurate. It belongs to the hybrid solid-state category, with an internal rotating prism but no exposed rotating motor. The Wiki glossary classifies it as "solid-state rotation," fundamentally distinct from pure solid-state solutions like Flash LiDAR or OPA (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5 terminology explanation).
  2. "360° FOV means no blind zones"—incorrect. 360° refers to the horizontal direction; the vertical direction is only 59°, leaving blind zones at the top and bottom. Moreover, non-repetitive scanning does not provide uniform coverage in extremely short time frames (e.g., a single frame), resulting in transient sparse regions.
  3. "Non-repetitive scanning is inferior to mechanical radars"—depends on the scenario. For static mapping and slow-moving robots, multi-frame accumulated point cloud density far exceeds 16-line mechanical radars; for high-speed dynamic targets, the regular beam patterns of mechanical radars are easier to track. Engineering judgment: selection should be based on task dynamics rather than "line count" intuition.
  4. "Humanoid robots must use 360° LiDAR"—the Wiki explicitly states that humanoid robots typically do not need 360° rooftop mechanical rotating LiDARs, instead placing compact solid-state/hybrid solid-state LiDARs on the head, chest, or shoulders (source: Project Wiki Chapter 5 §5.3.5). 360° surround-view is an option, not a requirement.
