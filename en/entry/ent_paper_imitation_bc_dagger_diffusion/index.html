Overview

This paper addresses the issue of compound errors in sequence prediction problems such as imitation learning, which arise from violations of the i.i.d. assumption. It proposes an iterative algorithm for training stationary deterministic policies and formalizes it as a no-regret algorithm within the online learning framework. The authors prove that, under an additional reduction assumption, the algorithm can find a policy with strong performance under the induced observation distribution. They validate its superiority over previous methods on two imitation learning tasks and one sequence labeling benchmark.

What It Changes

Imitation learning has long been plagued by "compound errors": during training, the policy learns under the expert state distribution, but during deployment, it must contend with the distribution induced by its own actions. The shift between these distributions causes errors to accumulate over time. Previous methods either trained non-stationary policies (such as DAgger's online aggregation) or relied on stochastic policies, often requiring many iterations to converge. What this paper truly changes is reframing the problem as "no-regret optimization in online learning" and proving that an iterative algorithm training a stationary deterministic policy—rather than a stochastic or non-stationary one—can also achieve strong theoretical guarantees. This breaks the implicit assumption that "one must sacrifice determinism or stationarity to achieve distribution matching," providing a theoretical foundation for more stable and reusable policies in real-world deployment.

Method Breakdown

Algorithm Framework

  • Iteratively trains a stationary deterministic policy \(\pi_\theta\), collecting data at each step under the observation distribution induced by the current policy, rather than solely under the expert distribution.
  • Treats each iteration as a step in online learning: the policy acts as the "decision maker," environmental feedback (such as action errors) serves as the "loss," and the algorithm aims for sublinear growth of cumulative regret.

Theoretical Guarantees

  • The authors prove that any algorithm satisfying the no-regret property will, under an additional reduction assumption (whose specific form is not detailed in the paper), necessarily find a policy with strong performance on the observation distribution induced by the policy itself in the sequential setting.
  • Key design decision: training a stationary (i.e., time-invariant) and deterministic policy, contrasting with methods like DAgger that require maintaining mixed or non-stationary policies, thereby reducing policy-switching overhead during deployment.

Differences from Existing Methods

  • Compared to BC (behavioral cloning): no longer assumes i.i.d., but explicitly handles distribution shift.
  • Compared to DAgger: does not require querying an expert at every time step or maintaining a stochastic mixture policy; instead, it iteratively optimizes a deterministic policy.
  • Compared to Diffusion Policy: the paper does not mention diffusion models; the method falls within an iterative optimization framework rather than generative modeling.

Key Innovations

  1. No-regret guarantees for stationary deterministic policies: Previous methods providing strong guarantees in sequence prediction (such as DAgger variants) mostly relied on randomization or non-stationary policies. This paper, for the first time (inferred from factual points), proves that stationary deterministic policies can also achieve no-regret performance under the induced distribution, directly reducing deployment complexity.
  2. Unified perspective: Mapping imitation learning problems into the online learning framework allows mature tools such as regret bounds to be applied directly, rather than designing specialized distribution-matching objectives for each task.
  3. Empirical validation scope: Validated on "two challenging imitation learning problems" and "one benchmark sequence labeling problem," demonstrating applicability not only to control tasks but also to structured prediction, thereby extending the method's applicability boundary.

Engineering Insights

  • Verify theoretical assumptions first: Before reproduction, be sure to confirm the specific form of the "additional reduction assumption"—if your task does not satisfy this assumption (e.g., observation distributions with severe multimodality), the theoretical guarantees may fail, and the algorithm's performance may degrade to that of ordinary BC.
  • Monitor iterative convergence: The algorithm is iterative; monitor the policy's performance on its own induced distribution after each iteration. If oscillation or divergence occurs, adjust the step size or data collection ratio (these hyperparameters are not specified in the paper).
  • Baseline selection for comparison: The paper does not mention baseline names. When reproducing, at minimum compare against DAgger (representative of non-stationary policies) and BC (i.i.d. baseline) to verify whether the selling point of "stationary deterministic policies" holds on your task.
  • Sequence labeling tasks can serve as quick validation: If you work in NLP or structured prediction, you can first reproduce the method on a sequence labeling benchmark, where data acquisition costs are lower than in robotic tasks, facilitating rapid verification of correct algorithm implementation.
