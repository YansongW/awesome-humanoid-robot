Summary

In many real-world scenarios, rewards extrinsic to the agent are extremely sparse, or absent altogether. In such cases, curiosity can serve as an intrinsic reward signal to enable the agent to explore its environment and learn skills that might be useful later in its life.

Overview

This paper proposes a reinforcement learning algorithm based on intrinsic curiosity rewards. It learns a feature space through a self-supervised inverse dynamics model and uses the prediction error of a forward dynamics model as the reward signal, addressing the exploration problem in sparse-reward environments with high-dimensional visual inputs. The method was proposed by researchers from UC Berkeley and other institutions. Its core contribution lies in bypassing the difficulty of pixel prediction, enabling agents to learn navigation and exploration in 3D environments and games using only curiosity signals.

What It Changed

In reinforcement learning scenarios with sparse or no rewards, random exploration almost never reaches goal states, while traditional intrinsic reward methods either require statistical models of the environment state distribution or require building dynamics models in high-dimensional continuous spaces (such as images), both of which are difficult to achieve. Direct pixel prediction has a fundamental flaw: uncontrollable randomness in the environment (e.g., TV static, swaying leaves) causes persistently high prediction errors, trapping agents in a "curiosity trap" and halting exploration. What this paper truly changes is the design paradigm of intrinsic reward signals—instead of attempting to model the entire environment's dynamics, it focuses only on state changes relevant to the agent's actions, achieving "selective curiosity" at the feature level and making exploration signals computable and robust under high-dimensional visual inputs.

Method Breakdown

Core Architecture: ICM (Intrinsic Curiosity Module)

  • Inverse Dynamics Model: Given current state \(s_t\) and next state \(s_{t+1}\), predict action \(\hat{a}_t = g(s_t, s_{t+1}; \theta_I)\). Trained by minimizing loss \(L_I(\hat{a}_t, a_t)\), outputting a soft-max distribution for discrete actions, equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation under a multinomial distribution. This model forces the feature space \(\phi(s_t)\) to retain only action-relevant information while ignoring irrelevant environmental changes.
  • Forward Dynamics Model: Given current state features \(\phi(s_t)\) and action \(a_t\), predict next state features \(\hat{\phi}(s_{t+1}) = f(\phi(s_t), a_t; \theta_F)\). The loss function is \(L_F = \frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\phi}(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_{t+1})\|_2^2\).
  • Intrinsic Reward: \(r_t^i = \frac{\eta}{2}\|\hat{\phi}(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_{t+1})\|_2^2\), where \(\eta > 0\) is a scaling factor. The larger the prediction error, the higher the curiosity reward.
  • Overall Optimization Objective: \(\min_{\theta_P, \theta_I, \theta_F} [-\lambda \mathbb{E}_{\pi(s_t;\theta_P)}[\sum_t r_t] + (1-\beta)L_I + \beta L_F]\), where \(\beta = 0.2\) is the weight between inverse and forward model losses, and \(\lambda = 0.1\) is the weight between policy gradient loss and intrinsic reward learning importance.
  • Policy Learning: Uses the A3C (Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic) algorithm with 20 parallel workers, ADAM optimizer, and learning rate 1e-3.

Key Design Decisions

  • Feature space rather than pixel space: Predicting in raw pixel space is undesirable not only because prediction is difficult, but also because pixel prediction errors cannot distinguish between "controllable changes" and "irrelevant changes" (e.g., leaves moving in the wind).
  • Inverse model as feature learner: By predicting actions, the network is forced to ignore environmental factors that do not affect the agent, thereby constructing "causally relevant" feature representations.
  • State change classification: A good curiosity feature space should model (1) changes controllable by the agent and (2) uncontrollable changes that affect the agent, while ignoring (3) uncontrollable changes that do not affect the agent.

Key Innovations

  1. Causal selectivity in feature space: Learning feature representations through the inverse dynamics task makes curiosity rewards sensitive only to state changes controllable by or affecting the agent. This is the first explicit distinction between "causally relevant" and "irrelevant interference" in intrinsic reward design, solving the "curiosity trap" problem of pixel prediction.
  2. Forward model prediction error as reward: Unlike Agrawal et al. (2016), which used the forward model only as a regularizer for inverse model training, this paper directly uses forward model prediction error to drive policy learning, making curiosity signals a direct driver of exploration.
  3. Generalization in reward-free scenarios: In Mario and VizDoom, agents trained with only curiosity signals can learn complex behaviors such as navigation and enemy avoidance, and pretrained policies can transfer to new scenarios (outperforming training from scratch after fine-tuning), demonstrating the strong exploration capability of intrinsic rewards without external supervision.

Mario Reward-Free Exploration

  • Trained with only curiosity signals, the agent crosses over 30% of Level-1.
  • Generalization experiment: After pretraining on Level-1, the fine-tuned policy achieves an average distance of 466 ± 37.9 on Level-2, outperforming training from scratch at 399.7 ± 22.5 (1.5M iterations) and 455.5 ± 33.4 (3.5M iterations).
  • "As is" runs perform well on Level-3 (319.3 ± 9.7) but poorly on Level-2 (31.9 ± 4.2), attributed to the significantly different visual appearance of Level-2 (night world).

Generalization to New Scenarios

  • On VizDoom test maps (new textures), ICM + A3C with pretraining followed by fine-tuning on extrinsic rewards significantly outperforms training from scratch, while ICM-pixels fails completely.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Sparse interaction scenarios: The method does not directly extend to scenarios where "interaction opportunities are also sparse." The authors leave replay memory-guided exploration as future work.
  • Pit at 38% of Mario Level: Requires 15-20 specific key sequences to jump across; failure results in death with no gradient information. The authors consider this orthogonal to curiosity modeling, but it poses a challenge for policy learning.
  • What was not done: No validation on real robot platforms, no continuous action space experiments, no 3D physical world validation, no specific value for \(\eta\), no ablation studies for \(\lambda\) and \(\beta\), no interpretability analysis of ICM feature space, no analysis of curiosity saturation, and no systematic tuning of random seeds (the paper explicitly states "We did not perform any tuning of random seeds").
  • Generalization limitations: The poor "as is" performance on Mario Level-2 is only speculated to be due to visual appearance differences, not fully verified; whether the specific mechanism of curiosity blocking during Level-3 fine-tuning can be avoided is not mentioned.

Engineering Insights

  • Reproduction checkpoints: First verify whether the inverse model truly learns action-relevant features—visualize the feature space during training and check whether irrelevant interference (e.g., white noise) is suppressed; then check the scale of forward model prediction errors and adjust the \(\eta\) scaling factor if necessary.
  • Most common pitfalls: ICM-pixels may succeed on "sparse" tasks without noise, but is significantly impaired once irrelevant interference is added, so evaluation must include interference tests; "as is" generalization in Mario is highly affected by visual appearance differences, and fine-tuning requires attention to the "curiosity blocking" phenomenon—once the policy cannot pass a difficult point, the learned parts almost stop generating rewards and the policy degrades.
  • Engineering recommendations: For downstream teams, ICM is the first choice when the task environment contains large amounts of uncontrollable visual noise (e.g., leaves, shadows); if interaction opportunities are sparse, additional replay memory mechanisms are needed; the pretraining + fine-tuning paradigm works well in scenarios with small visual differences, but retraining or increased fine-tuning iterations are needed when visual differences are large.
