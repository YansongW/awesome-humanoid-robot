Overview

Hardware emergency stop and safety chain is a critical process node in the humanoid robot electronics and energy systems (P13). Its core design logic is: any software failure can be cut off through hardware, and the overall design meets functional safety requirements. What it truly changes is not "installing a red button," but making "the robot can be physically forced to stop under any abnormal condition" an independent safeguard path that does not rely on software correctness.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The hardware emergency stop and safety chain is an electrical safety mechanism independent of the main control system, composed of hardware emergency stop switches, watchdogs, fuses, safety PLCs/relays, dual-channel redundancy, and other components. Its responsibility is: when the system detects an anomaly (or the operator actively triggers it), it cuts off the power source through a purely hardware path, causing the robot to enter a safe stop state, and this path does not depend on whether the main control software is running normally.

In the P13.3.1 process of WBS V3, this node is expanded into five level-4 subtasks: input review and target quantification (P13.3.1.1), solution/method design (P13.3.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P13.3.1.3), verification and issue closure (P13.3.1.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P13.3.1.5). Each subtask follows a unified engineering action template, ensuring full traceability of the safety chain from requirements to delivery.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The safety design of humanoid robots faces a fundamental contradiction: the more complex the software, the more failure modes there are, but the higher the safety requirements become. In early robot development, the emergency stop function was often simply implemented as "read a button in software, then send a command to stop"—this led to three systemic pain points:

First, software failure means safety failure. If the main control chip crashes, the operating system freezes, or the communication bus is interrupted, the software emergency stop logic cannot execute at all. At this point, the robot may continue moving with power, causing personal injury or equipment damage.

Second, single-point failures lack redundancy. If an emergency stop circuit has only one relay and one cable, the failure of any single component will cause the entire emergency stop function to be lost. This is unacceptable under functional safety standards.

Third, response time is uncontrollable. The latency of a software emergency stop depends on CPU load, interrupt priority, and task scheduling, making the worst-case response time difficult to guarantee. In contrast, the response time of a hardware safety chain is determined by physical parameters such as relay actuation time and contactor opening time, which are deterministic and measurable.

The historical positioning of the hardware emergency stop and safety chain is precisely to address the above pain points as an independent "last line of defense" separate from the main controller. It does not replace software-level safety functions (such as collision detection, torque limiting), but rather ensures that power can still be physically cut off when all software fails.

Principle Breakdown

① Basic Topology of the Safety Chain: Series Equals Safety

The core topology of the hardware safety chain is a series circuit: emergency stop buttons, safety relays, contactor coils, watchdog outputs, and other components are connected in series in a single loop. During normal operation, all contacts are closed, the contactor is energized, and the power supply is connected; when any component triggers (button pressed, watchdog timeout, relay opened), the loop is broken, the contactor releases, and the power supply is cut off.

\[ V_{\text{coil}} = V_{\text{supply}} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} S_i \]

where $\(S_i \in \{0, 1\}\)$ represents the state of the i-th safety component (1 = closed/normal, 0 = open/triggered). If any $\(S_i = 0\)$, the coil voltage $\(V_{\text{coil}} = 0\)$, and the contactor releases. This is the physical essence of "series equals safety"—the reliability of the safety chain does not depend on software logic, but on the physical reliability of each series component.

② Dual-Channel Redundancy: Single-Point Failure Does Not Cause Loss of Function

Functional safety requires the safety chain to have redundancy capability. The dual-channel design divides the safety circuit into two independent channels (e.g., two sets of emergency stop button contacts, two sets of safety relays), and the signals from both channels are evaluated with an "AND" logic in the safety PLC. Power is cut off only when both channels are triggered; if only one channel is triggered, the system enters a degraded state and alarms, but does not cause a false shutdown.

③ Watchdog: The Hardware Sentinel for Software Failure

The watchdog is the bridge connecting software and the hardware safety chain. The main control software periodically "feeds the dog" (outputs a pulse signal); if the software crashes or a task hangs, the feed signal stops, the watchdog timer times out, its output contact opens, and it directly triggers the safety chain to cut off power. This achieves the automatic conversion of "software failure → hardware action."

Who Uses It: Application Cases

In the P13 electronics and energy systems phase, the hardware emergency stop and safety chain is a cross-cutting concern that every work package must cover. Specific application scenarios include:

  • Joint modules: The power input of each joint driver is connected to the safety chain contactor; during an emergency stop, all joints are de-energized simultaneously, and the robot loses power and falls naturally (or is held in position by brakes).
  • Battery management system (BMS): The BMS fault outputs (such as over-temperature, overcurrent) are connected to the safety chain, allowing the battery main circuit relay to be cut off under abnormal conditions.
  • Whole-machine debugging: Debugging personnel hold an emergency stop button beside the robot; once an abnormal motion trend appears, they press it immediately, and the hardware circuit cuts off power within milliseconds.

Limitations and Boundaries

The hardware emergency stop and safety chain is not a panacea; its limitations include:

First, it can only cut off power, not guarantee posture. After an emergency stop, the robot may fall due to gravity, requiring additional mechanical braking or fall protection measures.

Second, response time has a physical lower limit. The mechanical actuation time of the contactor and the response time of the relay determine that an emergency stop cannot be "instantaneous," which may not be fast enough for high-speed moving parts.

Third, false triggering is a design trade-off. An overly sensitive safety chain will cause frequent false shutdowns, reducing availability; an overly sluggish one loses its protective purpose. This trade-off must be resolved through quantitative scoring with an evaluation matrix during the P13.3.1.2 solution design phase.

Fourth, it cannot replace software safety functions. The safety chain is the "last line of defense," not the "only line of defense." Software safety functions such as collision detection, torque limiting, and speed monitoring remain necessary; the two are complementary.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Installing an emergency stop button means it's safe"—Wrong. The emergency stop button is only one component of the safety chain. If the subsequent relays, contactors, and cables lack redundant design, the entire chain can still fail at a single point. The reliability of the safety chain depends on the weakest series link.

  2. "The watchdog is a software function, not hardware safety"—Wrong. Although the watchdog is "fed" by software, its timeout detection and contact output are purely hardware implementations, serving as the key bridge connecting software failure to hardware cutoff.

  3. "The faster the response time, the better"—Not entirely correct. Response time must be weighed against the false trigger rate. Overly aggressive watchdog timeout settings can cause false shutdowns under normal operating conditions, reducing system availability.

  4. "Safety chain design is done once and for all"—Wrong. P13.3.1.4 verification and issue closure requires formulating test/review plans and acceptance criteria, executing tests and recording raw data, and outputting an issue list and improvement measures. The safety chain requires continuous verification and iteration.
