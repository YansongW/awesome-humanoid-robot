Overview

The Life Cycle Cost Model v0 is a methodological entity used to establish the overall machine cost baseline during the project initiation and commercial baseline phase (P0), and belongs to ent_process_p0_1_4. Through BOM target decomposition, TCO modeling, and break-even volume calculations, it translates target selling price and gross margin constraints into quantifiable subsystem cost ceilings, and outputs the overall machine BOM target and break-even production volume. What it truly changes is not the financial act of "running the numbers," but the engineering decision sequence where "cost targets must precede design freeze."

Content

What it is: Precise Definition

The Life Cycle Cost Model v0 is the 4th sub-task entity within the 1st work package (Commercial and Strategic Justification) of the P0 phase (Project Initiation and Commercial Baseline), numbered ent_process_p0_1_4. It belongs to the method type, with domains including Applications and Markets (11_applications_markets), Policy, Regulation, and Ethics (12_policy_regulation_ethics), and its value chain level is Validation and Markets (validation_markets).

The core task of this model is to establish a life-cycle cost estimation framework covering materials, manufacturing, supply chain, and mass-production amortization before the product design is frozen. It is not a static financial spreadsheet, but an iterative process spanning the five fourth-level sub-tasks of the P0 phase (P0.1.4.1 to P0.1.4.5): from input analysis, solution design, implementation modeling, validation loop closure, to documentation delivery—each step converges toward an executable cost baseline.

In terms of process dependencies, this model is a key output of the commercial justification within the P0 phase. Its outputs—the overall machine BOM target and subsystem cost ceilings—directly constrain the design space of the subsequent P1 (Requirements Definition) phase. The design thinking logic that "cost targets must precede design freeze" determines the model's upstream position in the process.

Why it exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In humanoid robot product development, cost overruns are one of the most common causes of project failure. Traditional development processes often prioritize technical solutions first and calculate costs later, only to find that costs cannot be contained after design freeze, forcing painful trade-offs between performance and price. The Life Cycle Cost Model v0 exists precisely to address this pain point at its source.

Its historical positioning can be summarized in three points. First, it is the implementation tool for the "cost-first" philosophy—establishing a cost baseline during the P0 phase rather than realizing cost issues only at the prototype stage. Second, it is the quantitative basis for "make-or-buy decisions"—through BOM target decomposition, it clarifies which components must be developed in-house and which can be outsourced, avoiding resource dispersion caused by "wanting to do everything ourselves." Third, it is the calculation framework for "break-even volume"—answering the fundamental business question of "how many units must be sold to break even," providing data support for mass-production decisions.

From a process perspective, this model resides within the P0.1 (Commercial and Strategic Justification) work package, progressing in parallel with intellectual property strategy (P0.3). What it truly changes is not the awareness issue of "whether to conduct cost analysis," but the process sequence that "cost analysis must be completed before design freeze."

Principle Breakdown

① BOM Target Decomposition: From Overall Machine Target to Subsystem Ceilings

BOM (Bill of Materials) target decomposition is the first mechanism layer of the cost model. Its core logic is: first determine the overall machine target cost (derived from target selling price and gross margin), then decompose it layer by layer by subsystem (e.g., joint modules, structural parts, electronics, sensors, batteries, etc.), forming a cost ceiling for each subsystem.

\[ C_{\text{Overall Machine Target}} = P_{\text{Target Selling Price}} \times (1 - \text{Gross Margin}) \]

Here, \(P_{\text{Target Selling Price}}\) is determined by market and commercial justification, and the gross margin is determined by corporate strategy. The decomposed cost ceiling for each subsystem \(C_i\) satisfies:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i \leq C_{\text{Overall Machine Target}} \times (1 + \text{Reserve Margin}) \]

The reserve margin covers manufacturing expenses, packaging, transportation, and after-sales costs beyond the BOM. This decomposition process is not one-off but is iteratively refined as the design deepens.

② TCO Model: From Per-Unit Cost to Life-Cycle Cost

The TCO (Total Cost of Ownership) model extends the perspective from per-unit manufacturing cost to the entire product life cycle—including R&D amortization, manufacturing, supply chain, after-sales maintenance, energy consumption, and end-of-life disposal. In the humanoid robot context, the TCO model particularly focuses on mass-production amortization effects: R&D and tooling costs are gradually amortized as production volume increases, making TCO a function of volume.

\[ TCO(Q) = \frac{C_{\text{R&D}} + C_{\text{Tooling}}}{Q} + C_{\text{Per-Unit Manufacturing}} + C_{\text{After-Sales}} \times Q \]

Where \(Q\) is the cumulative production volume. This formula reveals the core tension in the cost structure: at low volumes, R&D and tooling amortization dominate; at high volumes, per-unit manufacturing and after-sales costs become dominant. This is precisely the mathematical foundation for "break-even volume calculation."

③ Break-Even Volume Calculation: Answering "How Many Units to Break Even"

Break-even volume is the intersection point of the cost model and the business model. It calculates the cumulative production volume at which cumulative revenue exactly covers cumulative costs, given a specified selling price and cost structure.

\[ Q_{\text{Break-Even}} = \frac{C_{\text{R&D}} + C_{\text{Tooling}}}{P_{\text{Target Selling Price}} - C_{\text{Per-Unit Manufacturing}} - C_{\text{After-Sales}}} \]

This calculation directly supports the break-even production volume metric in the "Completion Criteria / Deliverables." It also serves as an input for supply chain minimum order quantity (MOQ) constraints—if the break-even volume falls below the supply chain MOQ requirement, the cost structure or pricing strategy must be adjusted.

Horizontal Comparison

Compared to other methodological entities in the P0 phase, the unique aspect of the Life Cycle Cost Model v0 is its quantitative output attribute. Business canvases (e.g., commercial justification tools in P0.1) provide qualitative frameworks, while the cost model provides numbers directly usable as design constraints—overall machine BOM target, subsystem cost ceilings, and break-even volume.

Compared to the requirements management toolchain (P0.2), the cost model focuses more on "how much it costs" rather than "what features are needed," but the two are process-dependent: requirements definition (P1) must proceed within cost constraints, and those constraints are precisely the output of this model.

Compared to intellectual property and compliance strategy (P0.3), the cost model is closer to engineering implementation, but its outputs equally influence compliance decisions—for example, if the in-house development cost of a critical component is too high, the choice may be to outsource and pay patent licensing fees, which must be reflected in the cost model.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Primary Users: Project Manager (Owner), Cost Engineer, Supply Chain Engineer, Financial Analyst.

Typical Application Scenarios:

  1. Project Initiation Review: At the end of the P0 phase, report the overall machine BOM target and break-even volume to management as key inputs for the project initiation decision.
  2. Make-or-Buy Decisions: Through BOM target decomposition, compare in-house development costs against outsourced prices to determine procurement strategies for critical components such as joint modules, reducers, and sensors.
  3. Supply Chain Negotiation: Use subsystem cost ceilings as bottom lines when negotiating prices with suppliers, ensuring procurement prices do not breach cost constraints.
  4. Mass-Production Planning: Based on break-even volume, determine the initial production batch size and capacity ramp-up plan.

Application Case: A bipedal humanoid robot project set a target selling price during the P0 phase that requires verification (specific figures not provided in the source material). Through BOM target decomposition, the joint module cost ceiling was set at a proportion of the overall machine BOM target that requires verification (derived from source data). Based on this, the reducer was decided to be outsourced, while the motor and driver were developed in-house. Ultimately, cost convergence was achieved at a break-even volume requiring verification (engineering judgment).

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Static Model Timeliness: The Life Cycle Cost Model v0 is a "v0" version, meaning it establishes an initial baseline during the P0 phase. As the design deepens (P1 to P4), the BOM will continuously change, and the cost model requires iterative updates—it cannot be set once and forgotten.
  2. Strong Data Dependency: Model accuracy heavily depends on the quality of input data—target selling price, gross margin, supply chain quotes, etc. If upstream inputs are inaccurate, the outputs lose their reference value.
  3. Incomplete Coverage of Late Life-Cycle Costs: The v0 version primarily focuses on manufacturing costs and mass-production amortization, with limited coverage of after-sales maintenance, energy consumption, and end-of-life disposal costs (to be refined in subsequent versions).
  4. Not Applicable to Disruptive Technology Paths: If the technical approach undergoes fundamental changes (e.g., switching from hydraulic to electric actuation), the original cost model must be rebuilt rather than simply adjusted with parameter changes.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The cost model is the finance department's job"—Wrong. The Life Cycle Cost Model v0 is an engineering decision tool whose outputs directly constrain the design space. Cost engineers and R&D engineers must participate jointly; otherwise, cost targets cannot be realized.
  2. "BOM target decomposition is done in one pass"—Wrong. BOM decomposition is an iterative process that requires continuous refinement as the design deepens. The v0 version is only a starting point, not the endpoint.
  3. "The lower the break-even volume, the better"—Wrong. A low break-even volume may indicate insufficient R&D investment or an unreasonable cost structure, requiring comprehensive judgment in conjunction with market size and competitive landscape.
  4. "The cost model can replace supply chain negotiation"—Wrong. The cost model provides targets and bottom lines; actual procurement prices still need to be achieved through negotiation. The model is the basis for negotiation, not the negotiation itself.
