Overview

Whole-machine integration and verification & validation (Integration & V&V) is the 15th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, expanded into several third-level subtasks in WBS V3. This stage covers the complete engineering actions of input review, solution design, implementation/prototyping, verification closure, and documentation delivery, and is a key node for ensuring downstream dependents receive qualified inputs. What it truly changes is not the assembly action of "putting parts together," but the qualification process of "using DV/PV verification to push the product from prototype to mass production."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Whole-machine integration and verification & validation (Integration & V&V) is the 15th stage among the 17 stage entities in the knowledge graph (research/methods/ent_process_p0 … p16), located after P14 software integration and before P16 pilot production and mass production preparation. Its core task is to assemble the completed subsystems—legs, arms and hands, and the perception-planning-control closed loop—into a complete humanoid robot prototype, and to verify through systematic testing whether it meets design specifications and safety requirements.

In WBS V3, the P15 stage is decomposed into three first-level subtask groups: P15.1 subsystem integration (leg system integration and debugging P15.1.1, arm and hand integration P15.1.2, perception-planning-control closed-loop integration P15.1.3), P15.2 whole-machine functional and performance testing (safety function testing P15.2.1, performance benchmark testing P15.2.2, environmental adaptability testing P15.2.3), and P15.3 durability and certification testing (joint and whole-machine durability testing P15.3.1, software stability and regression testing P15.3.2, certification preparation and pre-audit P15.3.3).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before P15, each subsystem (joint modules, structural components, perception units, control algorithms) had completed independent verification on its own test bench. But a subsystem passing individually does not mean the whole machine passes—after leg unit tests pass, once the torso and arms are mounted, the center of gravity distribution changes, and the original standing control parameters may become completely ineffective; arm grasping calibration may be accurate on a single-arm test bench, but after whole-machine vibration coupling, the error may double. This is the "integration hell": the problem lies not in any single part, but in the interfaces, timing, and energy interactions between parts.

The historical positioning of P15 in the process is the "watershed from prototype to product." The goal of stages P0–P14 is to "prove the solution is feasible," while the goal of P15 is to "prove the product is reliable." What it truly changes is not the assembly action of "putting parts together," but the qualification process of "using DV/PV verification to push the product from prototype to mass production"—the output of P15 directly determines whether P16 pilot production can start.

Principle Breakdown

① Subsystem Integration: The Verification Logic of Local First, Then Whole The three subtasks of P15.1 follow the engineering principle of "local first, then whole." Leg system integration and debugging (P15.1.1) verifies standing, stepping, and joint coordination on a single-leg test bench, using safety harnesses and limit protection as key constraints; arm and hand integration (P15.1.2) verifies typical actions and grasping calibration in single-arm functional tests, with constraints of workspace, collision, and load; perception-planning-control closed-loop integration (P15.1.3) connects the three together, verifying system-level behavior through end-to-end task testing. Each level of integration is carried out only after the previous level has passed verification, avoiding difficulty in locating problems when issues are coupled.

② Whole-Machine Testing: Progression from Safety to Performance to Environment The testing order of P15.2 is not arbitrary. Safety function testing (P15.2.1) is executed first because all subsequent tests are built on the premise that "the machine will not harm people"; performance benchmark testing (P15.2.2) is conducted after safety confirmation, measuring indicators such as motion accuracy, load capacity, and response latency; environmental adaptability testing (P15.2.3) is executed last, verifying the robot's performance under external conditions such as temperature, humidity, and vibration. This order ensures that the conclusions of each testing layer are credible.

③ Durability and Certification: The Final Gate for Mass Production The three subtasks of P15.3 are oriented toward the product lifecycle rather than prototype demonstration. Joint and whole-machine durability testing (P15.3.1) exposes wear and fatigue issues through long-duration operation; software stability and regression testing (P15.3.2) ensures that iterations do not introduce new defects; certification preparation and pre-audit (P15.3.3) checks compliance against regulations and standards. Only after this group of tasks is completed does the product qualify to enter P16 pilot production.

Horizontal Comparison

Compared with adjacent stages, the uniqueness of P15 lies in the hierarchy of the verification object. P4–P5 (joint modules and drive systems, body structure engineering) verify part-level performance, P6–P9 (URDF calibration, simulation, structural and thermal iteration) verify model-level predictions, P10–P14 (control, dexterous hands, AI, electrical/electronic and software integration) verify subsystem-level functions, while P15 verifies system-level behavior and product-level reliability. P16 pilot production and mass production preparation (Pilot & Production Ramp) goes a step further, verifying the manufacturing process itself—assembly line planning and SOP (P16.1.3), quality control system establishment (P16.2.2), pilot production (P16.3.1), and so on.

From a verification methodology perspective, P15 and P8 (structural strength simulation and iteration) complement each other: P8 uses simulation to predict structural performance under extreme conditions, while P15 uses physical testing to confirm whole-machine behavior in real environments. Simulation cannot fully replace testing because simulation models themselves require experimental data for calibration.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The P15 stage is a mandatory path for all humanoid robot product development projects. In the process modeling of the knowledge graph, P15 is explicitly marked as the "DV/PV verification" stage (inferred from corpus data), meaning it serves both design verification (DV) and product validation (PV) purposes.

At the subtask level, the typical application scenario of leg system integration and debugging (P15.1.1) is: verifying standing stability on a single-leg test bench, checking joint coordination through stepping tests, with all tests conducted under safety harness and limit protection. The typical application of perception-planning-control closed-loop integration (P15.1.3) is end-to-end task testing—having the robot complete the full task chain of "walk over, grasp the object, and place it back at the designated position," recording success rate and latency data. These test data are not only used for acceptance at the current stage but also serve as the baseline for establishing the quality control system in the P16 stage.

Limitations and Boundaries

The core limitation of the P15 stage is that "testing can never prove the absence of defects; it can only prove that no defects have been found." Even if the whole machine passes all test items, the following boundaries remain:

  • Limited test coverage: Performance benchmark testing (P15.2.2) can only cover the operating conditions defined in the design specification and cannot exhaustively cover all real-world scenarios. The temperature and humidity range of environmental adaptability testing (P15.2.3) is limited by test equipment capabilities.
  • Differences between prototype and mass production: The prototypes used in the P15 stage are typically hand-assembled or produced in small batches, which differ from the whole machines produced on assembly lines in the P16 stage in terms of assembly quality consistency. Passing P15 verification does not guarantee that mass production batches will necessarily be qualified.
  • Time and cost constraints: Durability testing (P15.3.1) requires long-duration operation and may be compressed under project schedule pressure, causing fatigue issues to be insufficiently exposed.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "P15 is the final test"—Wrong. P15 is the system-level verification before mass production, but the P16 pilot production stage still includes quality control actions such as incoming material and process anomaly handling (P16.2.3) and PPAP/mass production readiness assessment (P16.3.3). Testing runs throughout the entire product lifecycle.
  2. "If all subsystems pass, the whole machine will definitely be fine"—Wrong. The very existence of subsystem integration (P15.1) indicates that whole-machine behavior is not a simple superposition of subsystem behaviors. System-level issues such as center-of-gravity changes, vibration coupling, and communication latency can only be exposed after whole-machine integration.
  3. "Safety testing can be done last"—Wrong. Safety function testing (P15.2.1) must be executed first because all subsequent tests are built on the premise that "the machine will not harm people." The priority of safety testing is determined not by schedule but by risk.
