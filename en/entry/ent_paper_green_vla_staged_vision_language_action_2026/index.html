Summary

We introduce Green-VLA, a staged Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework for real-world deployment on the Green humanoid robot while maintaining generalization across diverse embodiments. Institutions per source list: Sber Robotics Center.

Overview

Green-VLA is a vision-language-action (VLA) model with approximately 4B parameters, proposed by the Green team for general-purpose robot manipulation. Its core contribution lies in proposing a five-stage training curriculum (L0→L1→R0→R1→R2) that systematically integrates internet-scale pretraining, cross-embodiment robot pretraining, embodiment-specific fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning alignment, accompanied by engineering techniques such as a unified action space, a data quality pipeline (DataQA), and target-balanced sampling. It significantly outperforms strong baselines such as π₀ and OpenVLA across multiple benchmarks including ALOHA, SimplerEnv, and humanoid robots.

What It Changes

What it truly changes is the underlying logic of the "VLA training paradigm"—it no longer treats data scale as the primary driving force, but instead regards "quality alignment, action unification, and RL optimization" as levers that are on par with or even prioritized over scale. Previously, mainstream approaches in the field (e.g., π₀, OpenVLA) relied on large-scale heterogeneous data mixing plus behavioral cloning (BC), but BC saturates quickly, and during multi-embodiment mixed training, the momentum buffers of high-momentum optimizers (β₁=0.95–0.98) become dominated by gradients from large datasets, causing rare embodiments to be "washed out." Green-VLA mitigates mode forgetting mechanistically via target-balanced sampling (αₜ linearly increasing from 0 to 1), rather than merely tuning sampling weights.

At the same time, it elevates "inference latency" and "system throughput" from engineering details to first-class citizens alongside success rate. Existing explicit-reasoning VLAs (e.g., EO-1, WALL-OSS) rely on autoregressive loops, which incur high latency; Green-VLA uses a flow-matching action expert plus an SDPA attention kernel and reduced denoising steps to compress inference overhead while maintaining high success rates. Furthermore, the RL alignment approach in the R2 stage (trajectory optimization + source distribution optimization) circumvents the log-probability estimation difficulties and value-gradient backpropagation instability of flow-matching models in traditional on-policy/off-policy RL, offering a pragmatic answer to the open question of "how to do RL for VLAs."

Method Breakdown

Five-Stage Training Curriculum

  • L0 (Base VLM): Underlying vision-language model (PaliGemma 3B), without robot action capabilities.
  • L1 (Web Pretraining): 24M non-robot multimodal samples, building physical world understanding and semantic foundations.
  • R0 (General Robot Pretraining): 184M robot-domain samples (>3,000 hours, spanning humanoids and manipulators), learning cross-embodiment general priors. Trained on 48 H100 GPUs for >10⁵ steps.
  • R1 (Embodiment-Specific Fine-Tuning): SFT on high-quality target-embodiment datasets, converting general capabilities into concrete skills.
  • R2 (RL Alignment): Combining BC priors with RL objectives to improve long-horizon success rates, robustness, and average chain length (ACL).

Unified Action Space (𝒜ᵤ)

  • Replaces naive padding with a 64-dimensional unified action space with a fixed semantic layout, enabling multi-embodiment control via a mapping function Φₑ and binary mask mₑ, eliminating spurious gradients from padding.
  • Dynamic embodiment and control type prompts cₑ (number of arms, number of hands, gripper/dexterous hand, joint/Cartesian, mobile/static, slot usage) make action mapping explicit.

Target-Balanced Sampling

  • Sampling weight: Wᵢ^(t) = wᵢ^αₜ / Σⱼ wⱼ^αₜ, αₜ ∈ [0,1], α₀=0, α_T=1.
  • Gradually converges from uniform mixing (α=0) to a skewed distribution (α=1), preventing early collapse to the dominant embodiment and allowing the model to first learn shared structure before specializing.

Action Alignment

  • Uses monotone cubic splines to resample trajectories and interpolate actions, normalizing per-step effective motion.
  • Estimates execution speed for each dataset via average optical flow magnitude from wrist cameras: low-frequency datasets (Bridge, Fractal) have large inter-frame optical flow and require denser actions; high-frequency but slow datasets (AgiBot DexHand) are downsampled and accelerated.

Temporal Scale Conditioning

  • Draws a scalar speed factor v ~ p(v) for each sample, warping the target trajectory via interpolation/resampling: v>1 slows down, v<1 compresses.
  • Applies RMS-style modulation to action expert hidden states: ĥₜ = γ(v)RMSNorm(hₜ) + β(v), where γ and β are small learned functions of v.
  • At inference, v is a hyperparameter balancing local precision and long-horizon efficiency.

Episode Progress

  • The flow-matching action expert jointly predicts a normalized progress signal ρ̂ₜ ∈ [0,1], with training target ρₜ = t/T, exposed to downstream planners to request updated instructions.

OOD Detector

  • The online detector is modeled as a Gaussian mixture model over training states: p_train(s) = Σₖ φₖ 𝒩(s|μₖ, Σₖ).
  • If the predicted action leads to p_train(s) < τ_ood, the action is recomputed toward a corrected state s + α∇p_train(s), with α=0.2.

Joint Prediction Module (JPM)

  • A dedicated VLM predicts 2D actionable points (u,v), lifted to 3D via depth d, intrinsics K, and camera pose T_c^w: [p⋆; 1] = T_c^w [d(u,v)K⁻¹[u,v,1]ᵀ; 1].
  • Solves IK to obtain feasible joint configurations q⋆, using pseudo-inverse guidance (ΠGDM) to modify the velocity field at each denoising step, preserving the prior while satisfying target constraints.

R2 RL Fine-Tuning—Trajectory Optimization

  • Trains a small independent critic (Implicit Q-Learning) on R1 data: L_V(ψ) = 𝔼[L₂^τ(Q_θ̂(s,a) − V_ψ(s))], L_Q(θ) = 𝔼[(r + γV_ψ(s′) − Q_θ)²].
  • After the base model generates trajectories, computes ∇ₐQ(s,a), normalizes it, scales by η, and adds it to the action: a ← a + η·∇ₐQ/‖∇ₐQ‖, repeated N times.
  • Since Q is not optimal, optimized trajectories must be validated in the environment (operator resets the initial state, executes the improved trajectory, and saves it).

R2 RL Fine-Tuning—Source Distribution Optimization

  • Trains a small independent actor network via actor-critic to generate random noise fed into the base model, maximizing environment reward.
  • The base model's action generation tends to stay near training data, remaining relatively safe even if the noise distribution shifts.

Key Innovations

  1. Systematic Design of the Five-Stage Training Curriculum: Explicitly layers web pretraining (L1), cross-embodiment robot pretraining (R0), embodiment fine-tuning (R1), and RL alignment (R2), with clear data scales and objectives at each stage. This is not simple stacking but decoupling "general priors" from "embodiment-specific" knowledge—R0 uses 184M samples to learn cross-embodiment commonalities, R1 uses high-quality data to convert skills, and R2 uses RL to align long-term objectives. Compared to π₀'s end-to-end mixed training, this layering allows each stage to be independently debugged and evaluated.

  2. Target-Balanced Sampling (αₜ Annealing): Directly targets the "mode forgetting" problem in multi-embodiment mixed training. Starting from uniform mixing (α=0) lets rare embodiments contribute representations early, then gradually transitions to a skewed distribution (α=1). This is a targeted design for the behavior of high-momentum optimizers (β₁=0.95–0.98)—early balance ensures the momentum buffer is not dominated by large datasets.

  3. "Non-Invasive" RL Fine-Tuning in R2: Both trajectory optimization (∇ₐQ guidance) and source distribution optimization (actor-generated noise) bypass directly modifying base model weights via RL gradient paths. This avoids two core difficulties of flow-matching models in traditional RL: log-probability estimation in on-policy methods (PPO/GRPO) and instability of value-gradient backpropagation through iterative generation in off-policy methods. It is more robust engineering-wise and preserves BC priors.

Experiments and Results

The comparative experiments cover a multi-embodiment range from single-arm manipulators and dual-arm mobile platforms to full-size humanoid robots, with baselines including π₀, GR00T N1, AgiBot GO-1, WALL-OSS, OpenVLA, RT-1-X, Flower, and DB-MemVLA. Evaluation metrics focus on success rate (SR), supplemented by average completion time and average chain length (ACL). Training follows the five-stage curriculum: L0 (base VLM) → L1 (web pretraining, 24M non-robot multimodal samples) → R0 (general robot pretraining, 184M robot-domain samples totaling 3,000 hours) → R1 (embodiment SFT) → R2 (RL alignment). R0 is trained on 48 H100 GPUs for over 10⁵ optimization steps, with significantly less data than π₀'s over 10,000 hours (the paper does not specify π₀'s exact data volume, only giving > 10,000 hours). Key Takeaways from Results:

  • R0 pretraining already exhibits strong generalization: In ALOHA table cleanup, Green-VLA(R0) achieves a first-item success rate of 69.5%, significantly higher than π₀'s 35.6% (calculated from table values 35.6→69.5, a relative improvement of ~95%), with an average completion time of 1m35s far faster than π₀'s 2m59s. On Simpler Google Robot, R0's average success rate of 60.2% already exceeds π₀'s fine-tuned 56.8%, without additional embodiment-specific tuning. This indicates that the unified action space and masked BC objective effectively eliminate the "pseudo-penalty" gradients of naive zero-padding, allowing multi-embodiment pretraining data to mutually reinforce rather than interfere.

  • R1→R2 RL alignment brings sustained and significant gains: On Simpler WidowX, from R0 to R1 to R2, average grasp success rises from 75.0% to 76.1% and then jumps to 91.7%, while average task success rises from 45.0% to 55.2% and then jumps to 79.1%. In the Simpler BRIDGE WidowX environment, R2 alignment improves the R1 model's success rate by an absolute 24% (paper states an absolute 24%). On Google Robot, R2's average success rate of 71.8% shows a clear improvement over R1's 61.3% (calculated from table values 61.3→71.8). The authors note that behavioral cloning saturates quickly in long-horizon and contact-rich manipulation, and R2's reward-shaping refinement effectively overcomes this bottleneck, particularly in long-horizon consistency, error recovery, and precise task following.

  • Data scale and diversity are key levers for pretraining quality: R0 uses 3,000 hours of unified multi-embodiment data, significantly less than π₀'s over 10,000 hours (the paper does not specify π₀'s exact data volume, only giving > 10,000 hours), yet achieves or exceeds π₀'s fine-tuned performance on multiple benchmarks. In the data mix, AgiBot twofinger receives the highest sampling weight of 0.210 due to its largest size (774 hours) and highest visual diversity (D_vis=0.149); DROID follows with 501 hours, D_vis=0.140, and a sampling weight of 0.129; Galaxea, with 477 hours and D_vis=0.168, has the highest visual diversity among ALOHA-type data. Self-collected Green Humanoid data, augmented via mirroring and time reversal, expands from 48 raw hours to 167 training hours, participating with a sampling weight of 0.089. This diversity-based weighted sampling strategy ensures broad coverage of tasks and skill distributions even with limited data volume.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Retargeting fidelity dependence: Performance remains limited by retargeting fidelity, residual dataset bias, and dexterous skill coverage. If the mapping from source robot actions to target embodiment is imprecise, priors learned in R0 may bias R1.
  • Missing multilingual and reasoning modules: No multilingual instruction following, no integrated lightweight task decomposition reasoning module (combining chain-of-thought planning with real-time execution), and no embodied memory or trajectory replay.
  • v as a hyperparameter: The temporal scale factor v at inference requires manual setting; future work will have high-level planners control it automatically.
  • R2 trajectory optimization does not guarantee improvement: The authors explicitly acknowledge that the Q function is suboptimal and hyperparameters (η, N) are heuristically tuned; optimized trajectories may worsen and require environment validation.
  • Unspecified parameters: The specific VLM model in JPM, OOD threshold τ_ood, exact values of η and N, and the sampling distribution p(v) for v are not provided in the paper.

Engineering Insights

  • First verify the sampling annealing curve: Target-balanced sampling (αₜ from 0 to 1) is key to preventing multi-embodiment mode forgetting. When reproducing, first confirm that the αₜ schedule steps match total training steps—if α grows too fast, rare embodiments will still be washed out; too slow, and large embodiments underfit.
  • Action alignment is a hidden bottleneck: Differences in execution speed across datasets (estimated via wrist-camera optical flow) directly affect mixed training quality. When reproducing, first compute average optical flow magnitude per dataset, then decide interpolation/downsampling strategies; otherwise, the model's expectations of dynamics magnitude become confused.
  • The R2 validation loop cannot be skipped: Trajectory optimization (∇ₐQ guidance) does not guarantee improvement; an environment validation step (resetting initial state, executing improved trajectories, saving results) is mandatory. Skipping it may mix worsened trajectories into the R1 dataset, causing cumulative contamination.
  • Mask design in the unified action space: The 64-dimensional unified action space relies on correct implementation of Φₑ and mₑ. Naive padding produces spurious gradients; when reproducing, first verify that semantic alignment of action slots is consistent across embodiments.
  • Inference optimization focus: The SDPA attention kernel and reduced flow-matching denoising steps are the primary means of compressing latency. If downstream applications are latency-sensitive, prioritize tuning these two over model scale.
  • DataQA metrics: Thresholds for jitter (J), sharpness (S), diversity (D), and state variance (σ²) need adjustment per embodiment—for example, AgiBot twofinger's D_state=3975 is far higher than DROID's 3.10, and directly applying a unified threshold would incorrectly discard valid data.
