Overview

Fatigue and life prediction is a critical process node in the structural strength simulation and iteration (Structural FEA) phase. For high-cycle fatigue locations subjected to cyclic loading, such as joint lugs and connecting rod hole edges, life assessment is performed using S-N curves, Miner's cumulative damage rule, and rainflow counting methods. What it truly changes is not just the single point of "calculating a number of cycles," but rather establishing a quantifiable, traceable, and verifiable engineering closed loop among "load spectrum definition—material data—life indicators."

Content

What It Is: A Paradigm Shift from "Infinite Life Design" to "Finite Life Management"

Fatigue and life prediction is a collection of quantitative assessment methods for failure modes under cyclic loading in mechanical structure design. Its core task is not to answer "will this part fail?" but rather "how many cycles can this part safely operate under a given load spectrum?" In the humanoid robot development process, this process is carried out under the number P8.2.2, belonging to the structural strength simulation and iteration (P8) phase, and its output directly determines the maintenance intervals, replacement strategies, and safety margins of critical structural components.

This methodology consists of three core tools: the S-N curve describes the fatigue life of a material under constant stress amplitude; Miner's cumulative damage linearly superimposes damage under variable amplitude loading; rainflow counting extracts complete stress cycles from complex load-time histories. Together, these three form a complete computational chain from "material data" to "life under actual operating conditions."

Unlike static strength analysis, fatigue analysis focuses on the relationship between stress amplitude and number of cycles, rather than whether a single maximum stress exceeds the yield strength. This means that even if all stresses are far below the material's yield strength, the structure can still fail due to cumulative cyclic damage—this is the most counterintuitive and dangerous characteristic of fatigue problems.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the field of humanoid robots, the uniqueness of fatigue problems stems from their distinctive load characteristics. Unlike industrial robots that are fixed to a base with relatively deterministic load paths, humanoid robots experience loads on joint lugs and connecting rod hole edges during walking, running, and jumping that exhibit significant variable-amplitude, multiaxial, and non-proportional characteristics. Every footfall constitutes a load cycle, and variations in gait, uneven ground, and fluctuating payloads all alter the statistical properties of the load spectrum.

Historically, early humanoid robot development often adopted the "static strength check + safety factor" approach from industrial robotic arms, dismissing fatigue issues with the rationale that "the material fatigue limit is far higher than the working stress." However, as humanoid robots evolve toward high dynamic performance (running, jumping), joint peak torques have multiplied, and the rate of cycle accumulation far exceeds that of industrial scenarios—a robot walking continuously at a 3 Hz step frequency can accumulate hundreds of thousands of load cycles per day. At this point, fatigue transitions from a "theoretical possibility" to an "engineering certainty."

The fundamental reason this process exists is the dual challenge of load spectrum uncertainty and material fatigue data scatter. The former stems from the complexity and variability of humanoid robot operating conditions, while the latter arises from the statistical nature of fatigue test data. These two uncertainties determine that fatigue life prediction cannot provide a "precise value" but only a "probabilistic life interval"—this is the fundamental reason why this process requires defined inputs, quantified objectives, and the establishment of a risk register.

Principle Breakdown

① S-N Curve: Fundamental Characterization of Material Fatigue Performance

The S-N curve (Wöhler curve) is obtained through fatigue tests on standard specimens under constant stress amplitude, with the horizontal axis representing the number of cycles N (logarithmic scale) and the vertical axis representing stress amplitude S. In log-log coordinates, the high-cycle fatigue region (N > 10⁴ cycles) is typically approximated as a straight line:

\[ \log N = \log C - m \log S \]

where m is the curve slope (a material constant, typically between 3–10 for metallic materials) and C is a material constant. When the stress amplitude falls below the fatigue limit S_e, the number of cycles theoretically approaches infinity. For aluminum alloys commonly used in humanoid robots (e.g., 7075-T6), the fatigue limit is approximately 30%–40% of the tensile strength (engineering judgment; specific material grade test data should be consulted).

② Rainflow Counting: Extracting Stress Cycles from Complex Load Histories

In actual operating conditions, the loads on joint lugs are not constant-amplitude sine waves but rather random time histories formed by the superposition of walking, turning, ascending/descending slopes, impacts, and other events. The rainflow counting method, through the analogy of "raindrops flowing down a roof," decomposes any arbitrarily complex load-time history into several complete stress cycles (amplitude S_a and mean S_m), with each cycle corresponding to a damage contribution on the S-N curve.

③ Miner's Cumulative Damage: A Linear Superposition Engineering Approximation

Miner's rule assumes that each stress cycle independently contributes to damage, with total damage being the linear superposition of individual cycle damages:

\[ D = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{n_i}{N_i} \leq 1 \]

where n_i is the actual number of cycles at the i-th stress amplitude level, and N_i is the number of cycles to fatigue failure for that stress amplitude (obtained from the S-N curve). When the cumulative damage D reaches 1, the structure is determined to have undergone fatigue failure. The engineering value of Miner's rule lies in its simplicity—it transforms the complex variable-amplitude loading problem into an arithmetic problem of "curve lookup + summation," at the cost of neglecting load sequence effects and stress interactions.

Horizontal Comparison

Three levels of methods exist for structural life assessment in humanoid robots:

Static strength check (traditional method): Only verifies that the maximum stress is below the yield strength, without addressing the number of cycles. Its advantage is simplicity and speed; its disadvantage is the inability to answer "how long can it last?" It is sufficient for scenarios with low cycle counts and high stress amplitudes (e.g., impact loads) but severely inadequate for high-cycle fatigue scenarios.

Nominal stress method (adopted in this process): Based on S-N curves and Miner's cumulative damage, applicable to high-cycle fatigue (N > 10⁴ cycles). Its advantages are high engineering maturity and moderate data requirements; its disadvantage is that it does not account for local plastic deformation at notch roots, resulting in insufficient accuracy for low-cycle fatigue (N < 10⁴ cycles).

Local strain method (advanced method): Based on Neuber's correction or elastic-plastic FEA to obtain local stress-strain at the notch root, combined with strain-life (ε-N) curves for assessment. It offers higher accuracy but requires more detailed material data and finer finite element models, with significantly increased computational cost.

For humanoid robot joint lugs, connecting rod hole edges, and similar locations, the nominal stress method achieves the best balance between engineering accuracy and computational cost—this is why it has been selected as the standard method for this process.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

In the humanoid robot development process, the direct users of the fatigue and life prediction process are structural simulation engineers and reliability engineers. Typical application scenarios include:

Joint lug life assessment: The lug structures of hip and knee joints endure periodic alternating loads from gait cycles. Stress cycles are extracted from the load spectrum via rainflow counting, combined with S-N curves to calculate cumulative damage and determine the expected cycle life of the lugs. If the life does not meet the target value, structural geometry modifications (increasing fillet radius, increasing wall thickness) or material changes are required.

Connecting rod hole edge fatigue verification: The hole edges at both ends of connecting rods are typical stress concentration regions, prone to fatigue crack initiation under alternating loads. FEA is used to obtain the stress distribution at the hole edge, combined with fatigue analysis to determine crack initiation life, providing a basis for maintenance interval planning.

Load spectrum uncertainty management: Due to the complexity of humanoid robot operating conditions, deviations exist between actual and design load spectra. This process quantifies load spectrum uncertainty into confidence intervals for life prediction through a risk register mechanism, providing a basis for design margins.

Limitations and Boundaries

Fatigue and life prediction methods have clear applicability boundaries:

High-cycle fatigue assumption: The S-N curve method is applicable to high-cycle fatigue scenarios with cycle counts greater than 10⁴. For low-cycle, high-stress scenarios such as impacts and collisions, the local strain method or fracture mechanics approaches are required.

Linear approximation of Miner's rule: Miner's cumulative damage ignores load sequence effects (the different impacts of high-to-low versus low-to-high sequences on damage accumulation) as well as the influence of stress ratio. In scenarios with highly non-uniform load spectra, prediction results may deviate from reality.

Material data scatter: Fatigue test data inherently exhibit significant statistical scatter; the life of the same material at the same stress level can vary by severalfold. Engineering practice typically employs P-S-N curves (probabilistic S-N curves) to characterize this scatter, but this requires more extensive test data support.

Load spectrum uncertainty: The load spectrum of actual humanoid robot operating conditions is difficult to predict precisely, constituting the largest source of uncertainty in life prediction. This process manages this uncertainty through a risk register mechanism but cannot completely eliminate it.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Fatigue life is a precise value"—Incorrect. Due to material data scatter and load spectrum uncertainty, fatigue life is fundamentally a statistical quantity. Engineering practice should focus on the distribution characteristics and confidence intervals of life, rather than pursuing a single precise value.

  2. "Static strength satisfaction eliminates the need for fatigue analysis"—Incorrect. Fatigue failure occurs in scenarios where stresses are far below the yield strength, and static strength checks cannot cover cumulative damage issues under cyclic loading. This is the fundamental reason this process exists independently.

  3. "Miner's rule D=1 means absolutely safe"—Incorrect. Miner's rule is a linear approximation, and actual damage accumulation exhibits nonlinear effects. Engineering practice typically adopts D < 0.5–0.8 as a safety criterion (engineering judgment) to cover load sequence effects and data scatter.

  4. "Rainflow counting is just a data processing technique"—Incorrect. The correctness of rainflow counting directly affects the accuracy of damage calculations; incorrect counting methods can lead to life prediction deviations of severalfold. It is a critical link in the entire fatigue analysis chain that directly corresponds to physical failure mechanisms.
