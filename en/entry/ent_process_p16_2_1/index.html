Overview

Supplier selection and audit is the P16.2.1 process node in the humanoid robot product development WBS, belonging to the supply chain and quality system subtask family within the Pilot & Production Ramp phase. What it truly changes is not "getting quotes from more suppliers," but rather bringing external manufacturing resources into an engineering verification loop as rigorous as internal R&D—from quantitative evaluation of candidate solutions, sample approval, to freezing the Approved Supplier List, ensuring that key components such as motors, reducers, batteries, and computing boards achieve multi-source backup under the triple constraints of delivery lead time, quality consistency, and price. This process employs supplier audits, sample approval, IQC, and second supplier development as core methods, ultimately outputting the supplier list, sample test reports, and the Approved Supplier List.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Supplier selection and audit is a systematic evaluation and qualification process for component manufacturers during the production ramp-up preparation phase. In WBS V3, it is positioned as the first third-level subtask under P16.2 Supply Chain and Quality Management, with upstream linkage to P16.1 Manufacturing Engineering Preparation (DFM/DFA reviews, mold and tooling design), and downstream delivery to the PPAP/Production Readiness Assessment (P16.3.3) in the P16.3 Pilot Production and Ramp-Up phase.

It is not an isolated "procurement action," but rather a set of engineering closed loops following a five-step paradigm: Input Review and Target Quantification (P16.2.1.1) → Candidate Solution Development and Evaluation (P16.2.1.2) → Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication (P16.2.1.3) → Verification and Issue Closure (P16.2.1.4) → Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery (P16.2.1.5). Each subtask contains several level-4 key actions, forming a traceable and quantifiable management trajectory.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

At the critical juncture where humanoid robots transition from prototypes to mass production, supply chain management is the hidden battlefield that determines success or failure. During the prototype phase, engineers can manually adjust every screw; during mass production, hundreds or thousands of robots require perfectly consistent component quality—this is precisely the core contradiction that supplier audits aim to resolve.

Historical Positioning: In the traditional automotive industry, supplier quality management has evolved over decades into mature systems such as PPAP (Production Part Approval Process). As an emerging field, the humanoid robot industry directly inherits this methodology but faces more severe challenges: the number of suppliers for core humanoid robot components (such as high-torque-density motors and precision reducers) is far smaller than in the automotive industry, and technology iteration is rapid, meaning supplier process stability is often not fully validated. Therefore, P16.2.1 is not just about "selecting suppliers," but about "verifying whether suppliers can maintain quality consistency at mass production scale."

The Triple Constraint Game: Delivery lead time, quality consistency, and price form an impossible triangle. Shortening delivery lead time often requires suppliers to increase capacity investment, driving up prices; lowering prices may force suppliers to sacrifice material or process quality. The P16.2.1 evaluation matrix is precisely designed to find the optimal balance point under these triple constraints.

Principle Breakdown

① Input Review and Target Quantification (P16.2.1.1)—Translating "Completion Criteria" into Measurable KPIs

The essence of this step is converting the vague notion of "suppliers must be reliable" into quantifiable acceptance indicators. Specific actions include: listing all upstream input items and confirming versions (ensuring design drawings, technical specifications, and quality requirements are the latest versions); converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs (such as on-time delivery rate, batch pass rate, PPM defect rate); establishing task owners, timelines, and risk registers (clarifying responsible parties and milestones, identifying supply chain risks, and registering them for tracking).

② Candidate Solution Development and Evaluation (P16.2.1.2)—Replacing "Connections" with an Evaluation Matrix

This step requires forming no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, establishing an evaluation matrix, and scoring quantitatively. Evaluation dimensions include cost, performance, supply chain, and maintainability. The key to quantitative scoring lies in weight design—different components have different sensitivities to the four dimensions: motors prioritize performance and consistency, structural parts prioritize cost and delivery lead time, and batteries require additional attention to safety compliance. After organizing the review and freezing the solution, the candidate pool is formally established.

③ Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication (P16.2.1.3)—Letting Samples Speak

This step requires suppliers to fabricate prototypes or samples according to the design solution and record key parameters. Core actions include: building models/prototypes and recording key parameters (dimensions, materials, performance indicators); executing simulations or prototype verification (such as transmission efficiency testing for reducers, charge-discharge cycle testing for batteries); recording anomalies and deviations (any phenomenon deviating from design specifications must be recorded and root causes analyzed).

④ Verification and Issue Closure (P16.2.1.4)—No Closure Means It Didn't Happen

This step verifies sample outputs to check whether completion criteria are met. Specific actions: developing test/review plans and pass criteria (clarifying test items, methods, and acceptance standards); executing tests and recording raw data (ensuring data traceability); outputting issue lists and improvement measures (every issue must be closed—either the supplier rectifies and passes retesting, or the supplier is downgraded or eliminated).

⑤ Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery (P16.2.1.5)—Letting Knowledge Flow

This step outputs final reports/drawings/specifications and updates the ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chain. Specific actions: writing documents according to templates and citing raw data (ensuring conclusions are evidence-based); completing internal reviews and version control (preventing "final version" from becoming "final version 2.0"); publishing and notifying downstream dependents (ensuring the P16.3 pilot production phase receives a usable Approved Supplier List).

Horizontal Comparison

Comparison with Automotive Industry PPAP: P16.2.1 is essentially an inheritance and adaptation of automotive industry supplier quality management methods. Traditional PPAP requires 18 elements (design records, engineering change documents, process flow charts, PFMEA, control plans, MSA, full-dimensional reports, initial process studies, etc.), while P16.2.1 compresses this into a five-step closed loop, placing greater emphasis on "rapid verification and rapid iteration." This reflects the characteristics of the humanoid robot industry: rapid product iteration, relatively small batch sizes, and immature supplier systems, requiring a more agile qualification process.

Comparison with P16.2.2 Quality Control System Establishment: P16.2.1 addresses the question of "who to select," while P16.2.2 addresses "how to manage continuously." The former is a one-time qualification assessment; the latter is ongoing quality control after mass production (IQC, IPQC, OQC). The connection point between the two: the Approved Supplier List output by P16.2.1 serves as the input to the P16.2.2 quality control system.

Comparison with P16.2.3 Incoming Material and Process Anomaly Handling: P16.2.1 is "prevention beforehand," while P16.2.3 is "correction afterward." The former reduces the probability of incoming material anomalies through audits and sample approval; the latter initiates corrective actions (such as 8D reports, supplier rectification) when anomalies occur.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Primary Users: Supply chain management departments, quality engineers, and procurement engineers at humanoid robot OEMs. In terms of organizational structure, the supply chain lead typically serves as the Owner of P16.2.1, quality engineers are responsible for sample testing and audits, and procurement engineers handle commercial negotiations and contract signing.

Typical Application Scenarios:

  1. Motor Supplier Selection: Joint motors for humanoid robots (such as frameless torque motors) are core components, typically requiring 2-3 backup suppliers. Evaluation focuses on torque density, temperature rise characteristics, and consistency (batch-to-batch variation). The sample approval phase requires torque-speed curve testing, temperature rise testing, and life testing.

  2. Reducer Supplier Audit: The precision of harmonic reducers or planetary reducers directly affects joint control accuracy. Audits must focus on materials (flexspline material), heat treatment processes, and gear tooth machining precision. Sample testing must include transmission error, backlash, efficiency, and noise indicators.

  3. Battery Supplier Certification: Humanoid robot batteries must satisfy multiple constraints of energy density, charge/discharge rates, and safety. Audits must focus on cell consistency, BMS protection logic, and safety tests such as nail penetration and overcharge. Supplier qualification is particularly important in this scenario—conducting quality, capacity, traceability, and compliance audits of component manufacturers before mass production to ensure external supply chains are integrated into a quality system consistent with internal standards.

Limitations and Boundaries

Limitation 1: Sample Approval ≠ Mass Production Stability. Sample approval only proves that a supplier "has the capability to produce qualified samples," but does not guarantee that "every batch during mass production will be qualified." This is the fundamental limitation of P16.2.1, which must be compensated for through the P16.2.2 quality control system (IQC batch inspection, SPC statistical process control).

Limitation 2: Subjectivity of Evaluation Matrix Weights. The weight allocation across the four dimensions of cost, performance, supply chain, and maintainability lacks objective standards, and different evaluators may reach different conclusions. Engineering judgment: it is recommended to use the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) or entropy weight method for weight calculation to reduce subjective bias.

Limitation 3: Cost and Time of Second Supplier Development. Developing a second supplier requires going through the entire audit and sample approval process again, which is costly and time-consuming. For low-volume products, this may not be economical. Engineering judgment: multi-source backup should only be mandated for key components (motors, reducers, batteries, computing boards), while non-critical components may use a single source.

Boundary: P16.2.1 does not cover post-mass-production supplier performance management (which falls under P16.2.2), nor does it cover supplier elimination and replacement processes (which are actions escalated from P16.2.3 anomaly handling). Its boundary ends at "freezing the Approved Supplier List."

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The more suppliers, the better"—Wrong. Increasing the number of suppliers dilutes purchase volume, reduces bargaining power, and raises management costs. The correct approach is: 2-3 backup suppliers for key components, single source for non-critical components, balancing risk and cost.

  2. "Passing sample testing means we can place mass production orders"—Wrong. Sample testing is small-batch verification and does not represent mass production consistency. A small-batch pilot production (P16.3.1) must be established as an intermediate verification step to confirm the supplier's yield and consistency under mass production conditions.

  3. "Price is the primary consideration"—Wrong. In the humanoid robot industry, quality consistency is often more critical than price. A single batch of quality fluctuation can lead to full-machine rework, with costs far exceeding procurement savings. Engineering judgment: in the evaluation matrix, the weight of quality consistency should be no less than 30%.

  4. "Supplier audit just means visiting the factory"—Wrong. On-site auditing is only one part of the process. A complete supplier audit also includes: quality system documentation review (ISO 9001, etc.), process capability analysis (CPK), historical performance investigation, and financial health assessment. All five steps of the P16.2.1 closed loop are indispensable.
