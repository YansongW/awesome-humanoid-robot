Overview

Thermal Simulation is a computational method that uses numerical techniques to predict temperature distribution, heat flux density, and cooling requirements in electronic and mechanical systems under operating loads. In the field of humanoid robots, it is used to predict critical chip junction temperatures, motor winding temperature rises, and shell surface temperatures, guiding the design of heat sinks, airflow channels, thermal interface materials, and power management strategies to avoid overheating-induced frequency throttling or lifespan degradation.

Content

What It Is: A Leap from "Empirical Estimation" to "Numerical Pre-Validation"

Thermal simulation essentially replaces physical experiments of "build first, then measure" with mathematical models. In humanoid robots—highly integrated, space-constrained, and heat-dense systems—what it truly changes is not whether temperatures can be calculated, but rather shifting thermal design from a remedial step after product finalization to a decision-making basis during the conceptual design phase.

Whole-system thermal simulation typically follows these steps:

  1. Establish a power consumption model: List the steady-state power, peak power, and duty cycle of each component.
  2. Construct a geometric model: Use CAD models or simplified geometries to represent the robot shell, heat sinks, and airflow channels.
  3. Set material properties: Thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, density, and emissivity.
  4. Generate a mesh: Refine critical regions and coarsen distant regions.
  5. Define boundary conditions: Ambient temperature, convection coefficients, fan curves, and contact thermal resistance.
  6. Solve steady-state/transient temperature fields: Evaluate critical chip junction temperatures and shell temperatures.
  7. Iterate and optimize: Adjust heat sinks, airflow channels, TIM, and power allocation until thermal targets are met.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robots integrate computing units, motor drives, batteries, and power electronics, resulting in high heat density and limited space. The traditional approach—"build hardware first, measure temperature, add a fan if it exceeds limits"—fails on three levels:

  • Spatial dimension: Joint motors, computing boards, and battery packs are crammed into narrow cavities, so localized hot spots cannot be resolved by simply "enlarging the shell."
  • Temporal dimension: Robot power consumption is dynamic during operation—motor drives are fully loaded while walking, and computing units are under high load during standby—making transient temperature rises more dangerous than steady-state ones.
  • Cost dimension: Each physical prototype iteration takes weeks, whereas a single thermal simulation solve takes only hours.

Thermal simulation is positioned in the industry chain as a "virtual test bench during the design phase." It allows engineers to answer three critical questions before the CAD model is frozen: Will the chip junction temperature exceed 125°C? Will the shell surface burn users? How large should the heat sink fin area be?

Principle Breakdown

① Three Physical Mechanisms of Heat Transfer

Thermal simulation solves the three fundamental equations of heat transfer—Fourier's law of conduction, Newton's law of cooling (convection), and the Stefan-Boltzmann law (radiation). At the robot scale, conduction and convection dominate, while radiation is non-negligible on high-temperature surfaces (e.g., motor housings).

② The Divide Between Lumped Parameter Methods and Numerical Simulation Methods

Thermal modeling approaches can be divided into lumped parameter methods and numerical simulation methods:

  • Lumped parameter method: Simplifies complex systems into a small number of nodes and thermal resistance/capacitance elements, using RC thermal networks to describe transient responses.
  • Numerical simulation method: Based on CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) or finite element analysis, solving continuous temperature fields.

The RC thermal network is the key to understanding thermal simulation. Thermal capacitance \(C_{th}\) represents a body's ability to store heat, and thermal resistance \(R_{th}\) represents the resistance to heat transfer. For a simplified "chip-heat sink-ambient" system, the transient thermal equation can be written as:

\[ C_{th} \frac{dT}{dt} = P_{\text{loss}} - \frac{T - T_{\text{amb}}}{R_{th}} \]

At steady state, \(dT/dt = 0\), and the junction temperature is \(T = T_{\text{amb}} + P_{\text{loss}} \cdot R_{th}\). This linear relationship reveals the essence of thermal design: there are only two ways to reduce temperature rise—decrease thermal resistance (better heat dissipation paths) or reduce power consumption (smarter power management).

③ Discretization Logic in Numerical Simulation

CFD employs the finite volume method to divide the continuous solution domain into mesh cells. Mesh quality directly determines solution accuracy: critical regions (chip junctions, heat sink fins) require refinement, while distant regions (air outside the shell) can be coarsened. Mesh convergence is the standard procedure for validating numerical solution credibility—when results no longer change significantly after mesh refinement, the discretization error is deemed acceptable.

Key Parameters and Specifications

Parameter Category Typical Parameters Engineering Significance
Power consumption model Steady-state power, peak power, duty cycle Determine the magnitude and temporal distribution of thermal loads
Material properties Thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, density, emissivity Determine heat transfer and storage capabilities
Boundary conditions Ambient temperature, convection coefficient, fan curve, contact thermal resistance Determine boundary constraints on heat dissipation paths
Mesh parameters Mesh density, convergence criteria Determine numerical solution accuracy and computational cost
Solver settings Steady-state/transient, time step Determine whether dynamic temperature rises can be captured

Among these, the duty cycle is a key parameter unique to robot thermal simulation—the proportion of time a component operates at high power. Motor drives have high duty cycles while walking and low ones during standby; computing units have high duty cycles during perception and inference and low ones when idle. The duty cycle directly determines the equivalent thermal power and is a core input to the power consumption model.

Horizontal Comparison

Engineering judgment: Humanoid robot thermal design typically adopts a two-tier strategy—"RC thermal networks for system-level rapid scanning + CFD for detailed local hot spot analysis." RC networks are used to evaluate whole-system power allocation schemes, while CFD is used to optimize heat sink fin shapes and airflow channel routing.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Case 1: Computing unit thermal design. The main control board of a humanoid robot integrates CPU/GPU/NPU, with peak power consumption reaching tens of watts. Engineers use thermal simulation to evaluate chip junction temperatures under different heat sink schemes, combined with DVFS (Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling) strategies, to ensure no frequency throttling is triggered during sustained inference tasks.

Case 2: Joint motor thermal management. Frameless torque motors exhibit significant winding temperature rises during peak torque output. Thermal simulation is used to evaluate winding temperature changes over time, guiding limits on peak torque duration and heat dissipation structure design.

Case 3: Battery thermal safety. Lithium-ion batteries generate concentrated heat during fast charging and high-rate discharging. Thermal simulation, combined with electrochemical models, predicts internal temperature distribution in battery packs to avoid thermal runaway risks.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Model accuracy is limited by input data: The accuracy of the power consumption model directly determines the credibility of simulation results. If duty cycle estimates deviate significantly, even the finest mesh is useless.
  2. Contact thermal resistance is difficult to model precisely: The contact thermal resistance of TIM (Thermal Interface Material) is affected by assembly pressure and surface roughness. Simulations typically use empirical values, which may deviate by 20%-50% from actual values (engineering judgment).
  3. CFD computational cost is high: Transient simulations with fine meshes can take days, making them unsuitable for rapid iteration in early design phases.
  4. Radiation heat transfer is often simplified: Radiation can be neglected in low-temperature-difference scenarios, but its proportion increases when motor housings are at high temperatures, and simplified treatment underestimates heat dissipation capability.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Finer meshes mean higher accuracy"—Wrong. Beyond a certain point, mesh refinement yields diminishing accuracy gains while computational costs rise sharply. The correct approach is to conduct mesh convergence studies to find the balance between accuracy and cost.
  2. "Steady-state simulation is sufficient"—Wrong. Robot power consumption is dynamic, and transient temperature rises can exceed steady-state values. Transient simulations combined with duty cycles are essential for evaluating peak temperatures.
  3. "Simulation results equal measured values"—Wrong. Simulations are based on idealized assumptions (constant material properties, ideal contacts), and measured values typically exceed simulation results. Engineering practice should reserve a 10-15°C temperature rise margin (engineering judgment).
  4. "Thermal simulation is just a heat sink design tool"—Wrong. It is also a validation platform for power management strategies—through thermal-mechanical-electrical co-simulation, thermal bottlenecks can be identified early in whole-system design and layouts optimized.
