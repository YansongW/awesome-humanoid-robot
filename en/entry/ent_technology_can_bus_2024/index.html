Overview

The CAN bus (Controller Area Network) is a multi-node shared serial fieldbus designed specifically for distributed control systems with high real-time requirements. In humanoid robots, it connects joint motor drivers with the central controller, carrying critical command streams such as force control loops with deterministic latency and strong anti-interference capability. What it truly changes is not the upper limit of data transmission bandwidth, but rather making "multiple actuators operating cooperatively under strict timing" an engineering-reliable reality.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The CAN bus is a multi-master serial communication protocol proposed by Bosch in 1986, originally used in automotive electronic systems. In the context of humanoid robots, it belongs to the fieldbus category—a real-time communication network deployed at the device level, connecting sensors/actuators with controllers.

From a protocol layering perspective, CAN only defines the physical layer and data link layer; upper-layer protocols (such as CANopen and higher layers of CAN-FD) are determined by specific applications. Its core characteristics are:

  • Multi-master contention: Any node can initiate transmission when the bus is idle, with conflicts resolved through identifier arbitration.
  • Hardware arbitration of message priority: The smaller the identifier value, the higher the priority; lower-priority messages automatically back off.
  • Differential signal transmission: Two wires, CAN_H and CAN_L, provide strong common-mode interference rejection.

In humanoid robots, the CAN bus typically handles joint-level real-time control—distributing torque commands from the central controller (such as real-time computing platforms like ODRI Bolt) to each joint motor driver at 1 kHz or higher frequencies, while collecting encoder position and current feedback.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robot communication needs involve a conflict between two time scales. The upper layer consists of AI tasks—visual perception, path planning, large model inference—running at 10–100 Hz, allowing millisecond-level or even hundred-millisecond-level latency jitter; the lower layer consists of joint control loops—torque loop, velocity loop, position loop—requiring update rates above 1 kHz, with latency jitter controlled to the microsecond to tens-of-microseconds level (engineering judgment, subject to validation with specific control laws).

Before CAN, internal robot communication relied either on point-to-point analog/digital cables (4–8 wires per joint, with wiring harness weight and routing complexity exploding linearly with joint count) or on master-slave polling protocols like RS-485/Modbus (the master must query slaves one by one, with latency growing linearly with node count and no priority concept).

CAN's breakthrough lies in hardware arbitration: all nodes listen to the bus simultaneously, and when conflicts occur, higher-priority messages (such as emergency stops or torque commands) win without waiting. This makes "determinism" no longer dependent on software scheduling but guaranteed by the physical layer. For bipedal robots—a typical system may simultaneously run multi-camera perception streams at tens of hertz, kilohertz-level joint force control loops, and high-level planning above ten hertz—CAN provides a predictable upper bound on latency at the joint layer, something general Ethernet (non-TSN) cannot promise.

Principle Breakdown

① Message Format and Arbitration Mechanism

CAN messages come in standard frames (11-bit identifier) and extended frames (29-bit identifier). The data field is up to 8 bytes (classic CAN) or 64 bytes (CAN-FD). During transmission, a node sends the identifier bit by bit while listening to the bus; if it detects that the bus level differs from what it is sending (a recessive bit being overwritten by a dominant bit), it immediately stops transmitting—this is non-destructive bit-wise arbitration.

\[ \text{Priority} \propto \frac{1}{\text{ID}_{\text{value}}} \]

The smaller the identifier value, the higher the priority. Emergency stop messages are typically assigned the lowest ID (e.g., 0x000) to ensure they win in any contention.

② Bit Stuffing and Error Handling

CAN mandates that after five consecutive identical bits, an inverted stuffing bit must be inserted to maintain receiver clock synchronization. Error detection includes CRC checks, bit errors, stuffing errors, ACK errors, and format errors. Any node detecting an error sends an error frame, forcing the sender to retransmit. This distributed error-handling mechanism makes CAN reliable even in industrial environments with severe electromagnetic interference.

③ Essential Differences from EtherCAT

EtherCAT employs "processing on the fly"—slaves read and write data immediately as the frame passes through, without needing to fully receive the frame before forwarding. Its latency can be as low as microseconds, and it supports distributed clocks (DC) for nanosecond-level synchronization. In contrast, CAN's arbitration mechanism inherently introduces non-determinism: higher-priority messages may block lower-priority ones. Therefore:

  • Joint count < 20, control frequency ≤ 1 kHz, loose synchronization requirements: CAN is sufficient, with low cost and simple wiring.
  • Joint count > 30, nanosecond-level synchronization needed, high bandwidth demands: EtherCAT is more suitable (engineering judgment).

Selection Key Points (engineering judgment):

  • Cable: A 120 Ω termination resistor must be connected at both ends; otherwise, signal reflection causes bit errors.
  • Transceiver: Classic models such as TJA1050, MCP2551; for CAN-FD, choose transceivers supporting 5 Mbit/s (e.g., TJA1044).
  • Isolation: It is recommended to use isolated transceivers (e.g., ISO1050) between the motor driver side and controller side to prevent motor noise from coupling into the control circuit.
  • Topology: Bus topology; keep stub lengths as short as possible (< 30 cm), avoiding star connections.

Conclusion: CAN's niche is low-cost, medium real-time performance, and limited node count for joint control scenarios. For humanoid robots, it often coexists in layers with EtherCAT or TSN Ethernet—CAN handles small joints like fingers and wrists, while EtherCAT handles large joints like hips, knees, and ankles (engineering judgment).

Who Uses It: Application Cases

  • ROBOTIS DYNAMIXEL series servos: Models such as the XL330-M288-T feature built-in TTL communication (a UART-based CAN variant), connected via daisy chaining, widely used in small humanoid robot fingers and dexterous hands. Its 288.4:1 reduction ratio and 0.52 N·m stall torque suit low-load joints.
  • MIT Mini Cheetah / ODRI Bolt: The ODRI Bolt real-time computing platform supports CAN-FD interfaces for driving joint motors in quadruped/biped robots.
  • Industrial collaborative robots: Some wrist force/torque sensors (e.g., Bota Systems MiniONE) transmit via EtherCAT, but the joint drive layer still retains CAN as a backup or auxiliary channel.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Bandwidth bottleneck: At 1 Mbit/s (classic CAN) and a 1 kHz control frequency, each 8-byte frame can only carry approximately 125 messages/ms (estimated from corpus data), limiting the practical number of nodes.
  2. Insufficient synchronization accuracy: CAN has no built-in distributed clock; multi-joint synchronization relies on external PTP/gPTP or hardware trigger lines, with accuracy affected by jitter.
  3. Cable length limitation: At 1 Mbit/s, the maximum bus length is approximately 40 m; inside a humanoid robot this is typically far smaller, but stub lengths still need attention.
  4. No built-in security mechanisms: CAN provides no encryption or authentication; safety-critical scenarios require an additional safety layer (e.g., CANopen Safety).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "CAN is a real-time bus"—Not entirely correct. CAN guarantees priority determinism, not temporal determinism. Higher-priority messages do preempt, but the upper bound on latency for lower-priority messages depends on bus load. Strict real-time scenarios require TSN or EtherCAT.
  2. "CAN-FD is just faster CAN"—CAN-FD not only increases the data segment rate (up to 5 Mbit/s) but also extends the data field (up to 64 bytes); however, the arbitration segment remains at 1 Mbit/s, and new transceivers are required.
  3. "The CAN bus does not need termination resistors"—Wrong. The 120 Ω termination resistor is mandatory; otherwise, signal reflection leads to unstable communication.
  4. "CAN is only for automotive use"—CAN is equally widespread in industrial automation, medical devices, and robotics; joint control in humanoid robots is a typical application.
