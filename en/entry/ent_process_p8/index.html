Overview

Structural FEA is the 8th stage in the full humanoid robot product development process, expanded into several level-3 subtasks in WBS V3. This stage validates critical cross-sections through finite element analysis (FEA), and its mesh and load assumptions must match the wall thicknesses, fillets, and ribs achievable by subsequent manufacturing processes. What it truly changes is not just "whether the calculation is accurate," but rather establishing a traceable, iterative closed-loop relationship among "design—simulation—manufacturing."

Content

What It Is: Strength Validation from "Arbitrary Thickening" to "Data-Driven Decisions"

Structural FEA is a critical validation stage in the product development process, positioned between body structure engineering (P5) and pilot production (P16). Its core task is not "performing an analysis" but establishing a complete engineering closed loop covering load definition—model preparation—solving and validation—documentation delivery. In WBS V3, this stage is designated as P8, and its outputs directly determine the feasibility of subsequent DFM/DFA reviews, mold design, and mass production process routes.

This stage covers complete engineering activities including input review, solution design, implementation/prototyping, validation closed-loop, and documentation delivery, serving as a key node ensuring downstream dependents receive qualified inputs. Unlike P5, which focuses on "designing the structure," P8 answers the question "can this structure withstand real operating conditions"—and this question must be answered before design freeze; otherwise, any modification will incur mold-level costs.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the humanoid robot field, pain points in structural strength validation have long existed. In early development, structural design often relied on a "double safety net" of empirical formulas and safety factors—thicker materials, larger fillets, denser ribs. The direct consequence of this approach is weight失控, and for humanoid robots requiring single-leg support and dynamic walking, weight失控 means reduced endurance, degraded dynamic performance, and overloaded joint loads.

A deeper pain point lies in the disconnect between design and manufacturing. A thin-walled structure that passes simulation perfectly may require rework because actual manufacturing processes cannot achieve it; a fillet that appears reasonable in CAD may be impossible to form in casting or injection molding. The historical positioning of P8 is precisely to bridge these two—its mesh and load assumptions must match the wall thicknesses, fillets, and ribs achievable by subsequent processes; otherwise, no matter how precise the simulation results, they are merely numerical games.

Principle Breakdown

① Load Case Definition: The Most Severe Case Determines Structural Design

P8.1.1 Load Case Definition is the starting point of the entire simulation effort. Its methods/tools include statics, dynamic impact, drop, typical motion reaction forces, and fatigue load spectra. The core design logic is: the most severe case determines structural design. For humanoid robots, this means covering typical scenarios such as single-leg support, fall impact, and maximum payload grasping.

Key constraints include load transfer paths, safety factors, and test validation. The completion standard/output is the "Load Case Table," which must specify load magnitude, direction, point of application, and occurrence probability. This table is not a static document but the "constitution" for all subsequent simulation, testing, and validation work—any omission of a load case could lead to structural failure in real-world use.

② FEA Model Preparation: Dimensionality Reduction from CAD to Computable Models

Methods/tools for FEA model preparation include HyperMesh / ANSA / Abaqus / ANSYS, along with mesh convergence analysis. The design logic emphasizes simplifying non-load-bearing features, reasonable mesh generation, material properties, and contact/bonding settings. Key constraints are mesh quality, computation time, and resources.

The essence of this step is "dimensionality reduction"—simplifying complex geometric features in CAD (small fillets, threads, chamfers, tiny holes) while preserving structural features that dominate the mechanical response. Mesh convergence analysis is used to confirm that the influence of mesh density on results has been reduced to an acceptable range, avoiding the intuitive misconception that "finer mesh means more accurate results."

③ Validation Closed Loop: Simulation Is Not the Endpoint; Iteration Is

The validation and issue closure (P8.1.1.4) in the P8 stage requires formulating test/review plans and acceptance criteria, executing tests and recording raw data, and outputting issue lists and improvement measures. This means simulation results must be experimentally validated, and issues must be tracked to closure. What it truly changes is not just "whether the calculation is accurate," but establishing a traceable, iterative closed-loop relationship among "design—simulation—manufacturing."

Who Uses It · Application Cases

The primary users of the P8 stage are structural simulation engineers and mechanical design engineers. In humanoid robot development, typical application scenarios include:

  • Single-leg support case validation: Single-leg support during walking is the most common load-bearing state for humanoid robots, requiring verification that stresses at hip, knee, and ankle joint connections remain within safe limits.
  • Fall impact analysis: Fall impact is a typical representative of dynamic impact loads, with peak loads far exceeding static loads, often determining the minimum cross-sectional dimensions of structures.
  • Maximum payload grasping validation: When the robotic arm grasps maximum payloads, the bending moment at the connection between the end effector and linkage is greatest, requiring verification of strength compliance.

The common characteristics of these application scenarios are: load cases must cover extreme conditions in real-world use, simulation results must match downstream manufacturing capabilities, and issues must be closed-loop tracked.

Limitations and Boundaries

The P8 stage has clear boundaries and limitations. First, FEA accuracy is limited by the degree of model simplification—removing features such as small fillets, threads, and chamfers improves computational efficiency but may underestimate local stress concentrations. Second, the accuracy of material properties directly affects result credibility; for composite materials, anisotropic parameters must also be defined, increasing modeling complexity. Third, simulation results must be experimentally validated; otherwise, they are merely "digital safety."

Additionally, P8 outputs must match manufacturing capabilities—mesh and load assumptions must align with the wall thicknesses, fillets, and ribs achievable by subsequent processes. This means simulation engineers cannot focus solely on mechanical performance but must also understand the constraints of CNC, injection molding, die casting, and additive manufacturing. This is the key boundary distinguishing P8 from generic FEA analysis.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Finer mesh means more accurate results"—Wrong. Beyond a certain point, increasing mesh density yields converging results (which is precisely the purpose of mesh convergence analysis), but computation time and resource consumption continue to grow. The reasonable approach is to perform mesh convergence validation to find the balance between accuracy and efficiency.

  2. "Passing simulation equals safety"—Wrong. Simulations are based on simplified models and assumptions and cannot fully replicate real-world conditions. P8.1.1.4 Validation and Issue Closure requires formulating test/review plans and executing tests precisely to bridge the gap between simulation and reality.

  3. "The more load cases, the better"—Wrong. Load case definition should focus on the most severe cases—single-leg support, fall impact, maximum payload grasping. Blindly increasing the number of load cases dilutes design attention and increases validation costs. The determining factors are the key constraints: "load transfer paths, safety factors, and test validation."

  4. "FEA is a validation tool used after design completion"—Wrong. The core value of the P8 stage lies in iteration—identifying issues through simulation, modifying designs, and re-validating through simulation. What it truly changes is not just "whether the calculation is accurate," but establishing a traceable, iterative closed-loop relationship among "design—simulation—manufacturing."
