Overview

Workspace and accessibility analysis is a core procedure in the URDF modeling and kinematic verification stage (P6.2.2) of the humanoid robot development process. It generates reachable space cloud maps for the foot end/hand end using methods such as Monte Carlo sampling, verifying the coverage of the robot's workspace against the ergonomic requirements of P2.2.1. What it truly changes is not the act of "drawing a reachable domain cloud map" itself, but rather establishing, for the first time, a quantifiable and traceable acceptance loop between "ergonomic requirements" and "mechanism design parameters."

Content

What it is: Mapping visualization from joint space to Cartesian space

The essence of workspace analysis is solving the image set of the robot's forward kinematics in Cartesian space. For humanoid robots, two types of workspaces are typically considered: the foot-end workspace (determining the feasible region for gait planning) and the hand-end workspace (determining the coverage range for manipulation tasks). Given a joint angle vector \(\boldsymbol{q} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), the end-effector position is given by the forward kinematics mapping \(f(\boldsymbol{q})\), and the workspace is defined as:

\[ \mathcal{W} = \left\{ \boldsymbol{p} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \boldsymbol{p} = f(\boldsymbol{q}),\; \boldsymbol{q}_{\min} \leq \boldsymbol{q} \leq \boldsymbol{q}_{\max},\; \text{no self-collision} \right\} \]

where \(\boldsymbol{q}_{\min}\) and \(\boldsymbol{q}_{\max}\) are joint limits, and the self-collision constraint is implemented through collision geometry pair detection in the URDF. The Monte Carlo sampling method generates a large number of random joint angle combinations (typically tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands), computes end-effector positions, and plots point clouds to approximate the workspace boundary.

Why it exists: Reachability blind spots caused by the "high degrees of freedom" of humanoid robots

Humanoid robots possess 20–40 degrees of freedom, far exceeding the 6 axes of industrial manipulators. The higher the degrees of freedom, the more sensitive end-effector reachability becomes to joint configurations—a certain position may only be reachable with a specific elbow posture, while completely unreachable with another. If reachability is not verified during the design phase, three typical problems arise:

  1. Mechanism interference: Improper joint limit settings for the hip or shoulder joints prevent the end-effector from reaching operation areas specified by ergonomic requirements (e.g., kitchen countertop height, top shelf levels).
  2. Posture degradation: The end-effector position is reachable, but the wrist or ankle posture exceeds the allowable range, making grasping or stepping postures infeasible.
  3. Self-collision hazards: At the edges of the reachable domain, links may collide with each other, and pure kinematic planners will not actively avoid this.

P6.2.2 exists precisely to eliminate these blind spots: it transforms the ergonomic requirements of P2.2.1 (e.g., "the hand end must cover a height range of 800–1600 mm (example value, engineering judgment)") into quantifiable coverage metrics and provides acceptance criteria.

Principle breakdown

① Monte Carlo sampling: Approximating deterministic boundaries with randomness

The core idea of the Monte Carlo method is to randomly sample \(N\) sets of joint angles uniformly in joint space, substitute them into forward kinematics to obtain end-effector positions, and count the proportion of points falling within the target region. The larger the sample size \(N\), the higher the approximation accuracy, but the computational cost grows linearly. In engineering practice, \(N = 10^4 \sim 10^5\) is typically used, yielding coverage estimates with errors within 1% (engineering judgment).

② Self-collision detection: Hierarchical acceleration of collision geometries

Each link in the URDF can define <collision> geometries (typically simplified spheres, cylinders, or meshes). Self-collision detection is performed through intersection tests between collision geometry pairs of two links. To accelerate computation, BVH (Bounding Volume Hierarchy) or the FCL library is commonly used for two-level filtering: broad-phase and narrow-phase detection. In Monte Carlo sampling, each set of joint angles requires one self-collision check, so the degree of simplification of collision geometries directly affects analysis speed.

③ Coverage calculation: Intersection metric between the requirement region and the reachable domain

Let the requirement region be \(\mathcal{R}\) (defined by the ergonomic requirements of P2.2.1, such as a rectangular box or sphere) and the reachable domain point cloud be \(\mathcal{W}_s\). The coverage is then defined as:

\[ \text{Coverage} = \frac{\left| \mathcal{W}_s \cap \mathcal{R} \right|}{\left| \mathcal{R} \right|} \times 100\% \]

where \(|\cdot|\) denotes volume or point count. The completion standard requires key posture coverage > 95%, meaning at least 95% of the volume within the requirement region is covered by the reachable domain.

Horizontal comparison: Monte Carlo sampling vs. analytical boundary method vs. grid scanning method

Method Accuracy Computational cost Applicable scenarios
Analytical boundary method Exact Low Low-DOF (≤6) manipulators
Grid scanning method High High High-DOF but requires regular grids
Monte Carlo sampling Statistical approximation Medium High-DOF humanoid robots

The advantages of the Monte Carlo method lie in its simple implementation, insensitivity to the number of joints, and natural support for embedding self-collision constraints; its disadvantages are that boundary accuracy is limited by sample size and it cannot provide an analytical expression of the workspace. For high-DOF systems like humanoid robots, the Monte Carlo method is the most pragmatic engineering choice (engineering judgment).

Who uses it: Application cases

Position of P6.2.2 in the development process: This procedure belongs to the URDF modeling and kinematic verification stage (P6). Upstream, it receives quality attribute data from P3.1.2/P3.1.3 and structural design outputs from the P5 stage; downstream, it provides a simulatable model foundation for P10 motion control algorithm development.

Typical application scenarios:

  1. Foot-end workspace verification: Before gait planning, verify whether the ankle joint's reachable range during the stance and swing phases covers the expected step length and foot lift height.
  2. Hand-end manipulation domain verification: For typical home scenarios such as kitchens and shelves, verify whether the hand-end reachable domain covers the operation areas defined by P2.2.1.
  3. Joint limit verification: If coverage is insufficient, iteratively adjust joint limits or link lengths until the > 95% acceptance threshold is met.

Limitations and boundaries

  1. Limitations of static analysis: Workspace analysis only considers kinematic reachability and does not include dynamic constraints (such as torque limits or inertial effects). A kinematically reachable point may be unreachable during dynamic motion due to insufficient torque.
  2. The sampling density dilemma: Too few samples lead to high variance in coverage estimates, while too many make computation time unacceptable. Engineering requires a trade-off between accuracy and speed (engineering judgment).
  3. Collision geometry simplification errors: Collision geometries in the URDF are simplified shapes that deviate from the actual CAD model, potentially causing self-collision detection results to differ from reality.
  4. Exclusion of environmental obstacles: Workspace analysis only considers the robot itself and does not include external environmental obstacles. Environmental reachability must be verified separately during the path planning stage.

Common misconceptions

  1. "Coverage > 95% means all key postures are reachable"—Wrong. Coverage is a volume ratio and does not guarantee that every discrete posture point is reachable. Key postures must be verified point-by-point individually.
  2. "The more Monte Carlo samples, the better"—Wrong. Beyond a certain threshold, accuracy improvement plateaus while computational cost grows linearly. In engineering, the appropriate sample size should be determined through convergence testing (engineering judgment).
  3. "Workspace analysis can replace kinematic simulation"—Wrong. Workspace analysis is static and statistical; it cannot replace dynamic kinematic simulation for verifying velocity, acceleration, and singular configurations.
  4. "Self-collision detection only needs to be performed once during sampling"—Wrong. Self-collision states differ across joint configurations, and each sample must independently undergo detection.
