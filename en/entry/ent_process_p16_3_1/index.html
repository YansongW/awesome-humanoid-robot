Overview

Pilot Run is a mandatory verification process for humanoid robots transitioning from design freeze to mass production ramp-up, and it is a core node within the P16.3 trial production and mass production ramp-up phase. It uses a trial production scale of 10–50 units to validate the real state of the entire supply chain, assembly, testing, and software pipeline. Through issue tracking, ECN (Engineering Change Notice), and first article inspection, it translates design intent into quantifiable first-pass yield and issue closure rate metrics. What it truly changes is not "producing a few prototypes," but rather enabling the team to examine the product from a mass production perspective for the first time—using a limited number of trial units to expose batch-related issues, thereby avoiding paying a higher price at a larger scale.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Pilot Run refers to a production node conducted at a scale of 10–50 units before formal mass production. It differs from single-unit prototype validation in the R&D phase—prototype validation answers "does the design work," while Pilot Run answers "can the design be manufactured stably, repeatably, and at an acceptable cost."

In WBS V3, P16.3.1 Pilot Run is broken down into five level-3 subtasks: input review and target quantification (P16.3.1.1), solution/method design (P16.3.1.2), implementation/prototype/sample fabrication (P16.3.1.3), verification and issue closure (P16.3.1.4), and documentation output and downstream delivery (P16.3.1.5). Each subtask is further subdivided into several level-4 key actions, forming a complete closed loop from input to delivery.

Its core methods/tools include: 10–50 unit trial production, issue tracking, ECN, and first article inspection. The design thinking logic is to validate the entire supply chain, assembly, testing, and software pipeline; the key constraints are time, resources, and prototype cost; the completion criteria/deliverables are the trial production summary report, first-pass yield, and issue closure rate.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In the manufacturing process system, subsystem design freeze only locks in "geometry and interfaces"—it does not answer "what equipment, what takt time, and what cost are needed to produce it." The manufacturing process system is precisely the bridge connecting design and mass production: it transforms CAD models into process routing, tooling and fixtures, inspection specifications, and cost structures, and it undergoes yield and consistency validation during the Pilot Run.

If Pilot Run is skipped and mass production is entered directly, the team will face a series of typical problems: inconsistent incoming materials from suppliers causing assembly difficulties, mismatches between tooling/fixtures and design causing takt times far below expectations, test programs producing false positives/negatives in batch environments, and software versions incompatible with hardware batches causing functional anomalies. These issues are often masked by "manual debugging" during the single-unit prototype stage and only erupt collectively in a batch context.

The historical positioning of Pilot Run is precisely the turning point where the manufacturing process system transitions from "design verification" to "mass production verification." It uses a limited quantity of 10–50 units to gain systematic insight into the entire pipeline state, serving as the last systemic defense before mass production ramp-up.

Principle Breakdown

① Input Review and Target Quantification (P16.3.1.1)

The core of this subtask is to transform vague "completion criteria" into quantifiable acceptance metrics. Specific actions include: listing all upstream input items and confirming versions, converting acceptance criteria into quantifiable KPIs, and establishing task owners, timelines, and risk registers.

Upstream inputs typically include: frozen design drawings and 3D models, draft BOM, draft process routing, test specifications, software versions, supplier lists, and incoming material inspection standards. The significance of confirming versions lies in: any input changes during the Pilot Run will affect issue attribution—if the design is still being modified, it becomes impossible to determine whether assembly issues stem from design defects or process defects.

The quantification logic for KPIs can be expressed as:

\[ \text{First-Pass Yield (FPY)} = \frac{\text{Number of units passing all inspections on first attempt}}{\text{Total units投入 trial production}} \times 100\% \]

First-pass yield is one of the most critical quantitative indicators for Pilot Run, directly reflecting the stability of the entire pipeline. The issue closure rate, meanwhile, measures the execution efficiency of issue tracking and ECN.

② Solution/Method Design (P16.3.1.2)

This subtask requires forming no fewer than 2 candidate solutions, establishing an evaluation matrix with quantitative scoring, organizing reviews, and freezing the solution. Differences among candidate solutions may manifest in: trial production batch division (one batch of 50 units vs. two batches of 25 units each), assembly sequence (full-unit assembly vs. modular parallel assembly), and testing strategy (full testing vs. sampling testing).

Quantitative scoring in the evaluation matrix must comprehensively consider the three constraints of time, resources, and prototype cost. For example, splitting into two trial batches may expose issues earlier but extends the overall timeline; full testing is more thorough but consumes more test equipment and manpower.

③ Implementation/Prototype/Sample Fabrication (P16.3.1.3)

Execute trial production according to the frozen solution, fabricate prototypes/samples, and record process data. Key actions include: establishing models/prototypes and recording key parameters, executing simulations or prototype validation, and recording anomalies and deviations.

At this stage, First Article Inspection is particularly important—the first unit produced in the trial run undergoes full dimensional and functional inspection to confirm that tooling, fixtures, programs, and materials are correct before continuing batch assembly. The quality of process data recording directly determines the accuracy of subsequent issue attribution.

④ Verification and Issue Closure (P16.3.1.4)

Verify the trial production outputs, check whether completion criteria are met, record issues, and track them to closure. Key actions include: developing test/review plans and pass criteria, executing tests and recording raw data, and outputting issue lists and improvement measures.

The core mechanism for issue closure is ECN (Engineering Change Notice). Any design, process, or material change must be released under controlled conditions through the ECN process, ensuring traceability and verifiability. The issue closure rate is calculated as:

\[ \text{Issue Closure Rate} = \frac{\text{Number of closed issues}}{\text{Total number of issues}} \times 100\% \]

⑤ Documentation Output and Downstream Delivery (P16.3.1.5)

Output final reports/drawings/specifications, update ICD/BOM/SOP/requirements traceability chains, and complete formal delivery to downstream stages. Key actions include: writing documents per templates with references to raw data, completing internal reviews and version control, and publishing and notifying downstream dependents.

Compared with the upstream P5 ontology structure engineering and prototype phase, Pilot Run shifts focus from "is the structural design correct" to "is the manufacturing process stable." The core constraints in the P5 phase are stiffness, strength, weight, and assembly accessibility, while the core constraints in Pilot Run are time, resources, and prototype cost—reflecting a paradigm shift from engineering verification to production verification.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Pilot Run is a standard process node in the full humanoid robot product development workflow, applicable to any product development project transitioning from design to mass production. In WBS V3, it sits under P16.3 trial production and mass production ramp-up and is a mandatory step before mass production release.

Typical application scenarios include: full-unit trial production (validating the entire pipeline), joint module trial production (validating supply chain and assembly), and software version trial production (validating test program and hardware compatibility). In full-unit trial production, the team validates four dimensions: supply chain (incoming material consistency), assembly (tooling/fixture fit), testing (test program effectiveness), and software (version and hardware batch compatibility).

Limitations and Boundaries

The boundary of Pilot Run lies in: it cannot fully simulate all mass production conditions. The scale of 10–50 units means certain batch-related issues (such as supplier capacity fluctuations or long-term equipment stability) cannot be fully exposed at this stage. Additionally, the cost and time constraints of Pilot Run mean test coverage cannot be infinitely comprehensive—certain extreme operating conditions or long-term reliability issues still require subsequent mass production ramp-up phases for continued validation.

Another boundary is: the conclusions of Pilot Run are highly dependent on input quality. If upstream designs are not yet frozen, BOM versions are chaotic, or supplier incoming materials are unstable, the credibility of trial production data will be significantly compromised. Therefore, P16.3.1.1 input review and target quantification is a prerequisite for the effectiveness of the entire process.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Pilot Run is just making a few more prototypes"—Wrong. Prototype validation answers "does the design work," while Pilot Run answers "can the design be manufactured stably." The purposes, methods, and deliverables of the two are completely different.

  2. "Passing trial production means ready for mass production"—Not entirely. Passing Pilot Run only indicates pipeline stability at a scale of 10–50 units; mass production ramp-up still requires addressing scaling issues such as capacity, yield, and cost.

  3. "Issue tracking is just recording bugs"—Inaccurate. Issue tracking in Pilot Run covers all dimensions—design, process, materials, software, and testing—and must be closed under controlled conditions via ECN, not merely recorded.

  4. "Higher first-pass yield is always better"—Needs context. Low first-pass yield indicates many issues, but issues exposed during Pilot Run are "good"—they prevent issues from erupting at greater cost during mass production. The key is whether issues can be effectively closed.
