Summary

A fundamental calculus rule that gives the derivative of a composition of functions as the product of the derivatives of the composed functions.

Overview

The chain rule is a fundamental rule in calculus used to compute the derivative of composite functions. Its core statement is: the derivative of a composite function equals the product of the derivatives of its constituent functions at that point. It is an indispensable mathematical tool in modern scientific computing, optimization algorithms, and robot control, playing a central role especially in the backpropagation algorithm.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The chain rule is a fundamental theorem in differential calculus used to find the derivative of composite functions. If \( y = f(u) \) and \( u = g(x) \), then the derivative of the composite function \( y = f(g(x)) \) with respect to \( x \) is:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \]

More generally, for a multivariate composite function \( z = f(y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_m) \), where each \( y_i = g_i(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) \), the partial derivative form of the chain rule is:

\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial x_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i} \cdot \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j} \]

This form has broad application value in robot kinematics, dynamics modeling, and neural network training. Essentially, the chain rule describes: when an input passes through a series of functions, the sensitivity (derivative) of the output to the input is the product (or weighted sum) of the local sensitivities of each layer.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Before the chain rule was systematized, solving derivatives of composite functions required limit operations from the definition, which was tedious and error-prone. In the late 17th century, when Leibniz and Newton independently developed calculus, Leibniz's notation \( \frac{dy}{dx} \) naturally suggested that "the ratio of differentials" could be canceled like fractions, providing a symbolic basis for the intuitive expression of the chain rule. What truly made the chain rule an "algorithmic cornerstone" was the proposal of the backpropagation algorithm in the 1980s—it transformed the chain rule from a purely mathematical tool into the core engine of machine learning.

In the field of humanoid robots, the pain points addressed by the chain rule are more specific: in robot kinematics, the pose of the end effector is a composite function of joint angles; in dynamics, joint torques are composite functions of position, velocity, and acceleration. Without the chain rule, solving the derivatives of these composite relationships (i.e., the Jacobian matrix) would require manual derivation term by term, and it would be difficult to generalize to high-degree-of-freedom systems. What it truly changed is not the mathematical operation of "how to differentiate" but the engineering paradigm of "how to make machines differentiate automatically"—from handwritten derivatives to automatic differentiation, the chain rule is the underlying logic of it all.

Principle Breakdown

① Local Linearization Perspective of Composite Functions The deep principle of the chain rule lies in the fact that differentiable functions can be approximated locally by linear functions. If \( f \) is differentiable at \( u_0 \), then \( f(u_0 + \Delta u) \approx f(u_0) + f'(u_0) \Delta u \). When \( u = g(x) \) is also differentiable at \( x_0 \), \( \Delta u \approx g'(x_0) \Delta x \). Combining the two yields:

\[ \Delta y \approx f'(g(x_0)) \cdot g'(x_0) \cdot \Delta x \]

This is the intuitive derivation of the chain rule: small changes propagate layer by layer, and the total rate of change is the product of the rates of change of each layer.

② Computational Graphs and Automatic Differentiation In modern computing frameworks (such as PyTorch, TensorFlow), the chain rule is automated through computational graphs. Each basic operation (addition, multiplication, trigonometric functions, etc.) is recorded as a node in the graph. Forward propagation computes the function value, while backpropagation starts from the output node and applies the chain rule layer by layer in reverse along the graph, accumulating gradients. This process is called automatic differentiation, which computes exact derivatives automatically via operator overloading or source code transformation, avoiding manual derivation of Jacobians.

③ Connection with the Jacobian Matrix In robotics, the chain rule directly leads to the chain multiplication of Jacobian matrices. If the relationship between end-effector velocity \( \dot{\mathbf{x}} \) and joint velocity \( \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \) is \( \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \), and the joint velocity is further transmitted from motor velocity \( \dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}} \) via the gear ratio \( \mathbf{G} \) (i.e., \( \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \mathbf{G} \dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}} \)), then:

\[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \mathbf{G} \dot{\boldsymbol{\phi}} \]

This is essentially the chain rule manifested at the velocity level—the Jacobian matrix of a composite mapping equals the product of the Jacobian matrices of the individual mappings.

Key Parameters and Specifications

The chain rule itself has no tunable parameters, but the following elements need attention when applying it:

Element Description Typical Value/Range
Number of composite layers Depth of function nesting Can reach tens to hundreds of layers in neural networks
Variable dimension Dimension of input/output space Humanoid robot joint count is typically 20–40 (engineering judgment)
Numerical precision Floating-point operation precision Single precision (32-bit) or double precision (64-bit)
Computational graph scale Number of nodes and edges Proportional to model complexity

At the implementation level, key specifications include: the choice of automatic differentiation framework (e.g., PyTorch, JAX), numerical stability handling (e.g., log-sum-exp trick), and memory usage (backpropagation requires storing intermediate activations).

Horizontal Comparison

Method Principle Advantages Disadvantages
Symbolic differentiation Symbolic transformation of expressions to find derivatives Exact, interpretable Expression explosion, difficult for complex functions
Numerical differentiation Approximates derivatives using finite differences Simple to implement Accuracy affected by step size, high computational cost
Manual derivation Human computes derivatives and encodes them Fully controllable Error-prone, difficult to maintain
Automatic differentiation (chain rule) Accumulates products backward along the computational graph Exact, efficient, general Requires storing intermediate results, higher memory overhead

In the robotics field, the combination of the chain rule and automatic differentiation has become mainstream. For example, Ceres Solver provides powerful AutoDiff functionality, while g2o and GTSAM support graph optimization through handwritten Jacobians and automatic differentiation, respectively—the core mathematical foundation of these tools is precisely the chain rule.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

① Backpropagation and Neural Network Training Backpropagation is the most famous application of the chain rule. It computes the gradients of the loss function with respect to all parameters of a feedforward neural network by applying the chain rule layer by layer in reverse order. In humanoid robots, this is used to train visual perception models (such as object detection, pose estimation) and reinforcement learning policy networks.

② Kinematics and Jacobian Computation In robot kinematics, the position of the end effector \( \mathbf{p} \) is a composite function of joint angles \( \boldsymbol{\theta} \). Through the chain rule, the Jacobian matrix \( \mathbf{J} = \partial \mathbf{p} / \partial \boldsymbol{\theta} \) can be efficiently computed for velocity control, singularity analysis, and manipulability assessment. Performing singular value decomposition on the Jacobian:

\[ \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^T, \quad \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r) \]

where the singular values \( \sigma_i \) reflect the manipulability in each motion direction—this relies on the Jacobian matrix correctly computed via the chain rule.

③ Dynamics and Torque Computation Robot dynamics equations (such as the Newton-Euler recursion) are essentially composite mappings from joint motion to joint torques. The chain rule is used to derive analytical expressions for the inertia matrix, Coriolis terms, and gravity terms, or to compute generalized forces directly from the energy function via automatic differentiation.

④ Visual Odometry and State Estimation In the front end of visual odometry (VO), the estimation of the essential matrix \( \mathbf{E} \) involves the normalized 8-point algorithm: 8 point pairs form an \( 8 \times 9 \) matrix, \( \mathbf{e} \) is its null space, and with more points, SVD can be used to find the least-squares solution. The solved \( E \) typically does not satisfy the intrinsic constraints of the essential matrix (two equal non-zero singular values), requiring another SVD and forcing the two singular values to be equal before reconstruction. The optimization problems in this process (such as bundle adjustment) rely on the chain rule to compute gradients.

Limitations and Boundaries

The applicability of the chain rule presupposes that the function is differentiable. For non-differentiable points (such as ReLU at zero), discrete variables, or piecewise constant functions, the chain rule cannot be applied directly and requires subgradients or relaxation techniques. Furthermore, the chain rule itself does not guarantee numerical stability—when the number of composite layers is extremely deep, gradients may decay exponentially (vanishing gradients) or explode (exploding gradients), which are classic challenges in deep network training.

In humanoid robots—a high-dimensional, underactuated, strongly coupled system—the chain rule typically requires a balance among real-time performance, accuracy, and robustness. For example, automatic differentiation based on the chain rule may incur computational latency in a 1 kHz control loop, necessitating precomputation, sparsification, or model simplification to meet real-time requirements. Additionally, the chain rule relies heavily on model accuracy—if the kinematic or dynamic model has errors, the gradient direction may deviate from the true optimal direction.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "The chain rule only applies to univariate functions"—Wrong. The multivariate chain rule (partial derivative form) is equally important and is the foundation of backpropagation and Jacobian matrix computation.
  2. "Automatic differentiation is numerical differentiation"—Wrong. Automatic differentiation computes derivatives exactly based on the chain rule, fundamentally different from numerical differentiation (finite differences); the former has no truncation error, while the latter is affected by step size.
  3. "The chain rule is only used for differentiation"—Not comprehensive. The chain rule is also used in integration by substitution, variable transformation in probability theory (such as the Jacobian determinant), and solving differential equations.
  4. "Vanishing/exploding gradients are a flaw of the chain rule"—Inaccurate. The chain rule merely reveals the multiplicative structure of gradient propagation; vanishing/exploding gradients are inherent properties of deep composite functions and need to be mitigated through architectural design (such as residual connections) and optimization strategies.
