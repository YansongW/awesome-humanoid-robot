Summary

Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) have demonstrated impressive whole-body agility for humanoid robots, yet ensuring safety and satisfying constraints -- particularly those specified after training -- remains a challenge. Institutions per source list: 斯坦福、英伟达研究院.

Overview

This paper proposes ConstrainedMimic, a framework that imposes arbitrary safety constraints (collision avoidance, joint limits, CoM stability) on pretrained RL whole-body controllers at runtime without modifying the policy itself. On the Unitree G1 simulation platform, the authors combine CBFs with contact-constrained dynamics/kinematics through three intervention points—constrained retargeting, kinematic filters, and dynamic filters—achieving real-time safety filtering at 250 Hz and reducing self-collision violation frame rates from approximately 37% to near 0%.

What It Changes

Existing CBF applications on humanoid robots either use reduced-order double-integrator models (ignoring real contact dynamics) or focus solely on lower-body locomotion rather than whole-body tracking. More critically, they attempt to modify the policy itself or require the policy to be constraint-aware, which contradicts the engineering need for "arbitrarily specifying constraints after training." What this paper truly changes is the location of safety intervention—moving it from inside the policy to outside the policy, filtering at both the input (kinematic reference) and output (joint commands) ports, thereby achieving policy independence and minimal invasiveness.

This addresses two practical issues: first, pretrained policies (e.g., TWIST2, SONIC) cannot be retrained at deployment time, yet safety constraints often become explicit only at deployment; second, existing CBF methods in contact-rich whole-body tasks fail to model contact constraints, leading to foot slipping and violations of kinematic consistency. By grounding CBFs on the contact-constrained null space, this paper ensures that safety filtering respects the current contact mode—a substantive response to the inherent contradiction that "safety filtering disrupts task execution."

Method Breakdown

Overall Architecture

Two intervention points are identified in the RL motion tracking stack: policy input (kinematic reference) and policy output (joint commands), corresponding to three methods: 1. Constrained Retargeting: Adds CBF inside the IK retargeter 2. Kinematic Filters: Filters the retargeted reference motion 3. Dynamic Filters: Filters the policy joint commands

Core Modeling

  • Contact-constrained kinematics: q̇ = N_c q̇₀, where N_c = I − J̄_c J_c is the contact null-space projection, ensuring filtered velocities do not violate contact (no foot slipping)
  • Contact-constrained dynamics: M q̈ + N_c^T(c + g) + J_c^T μ_{c,J̇} = N_c^T Γ, used for acceleration-level constraints in the dynamic filter
  • CBF condition: For a control-affine system ż = f(z) + g(z)u, requiring L_f h(z) + L_g h(z)u ≥ −α(h(z)) to guarantee forward invariance of the safe set

QP Structure of the Three Filters

  • Constrained Retargeting: Minimizes Σ ||W_i(J_{i|c} u − ν_i)||₂² with 14 pairwise weighted tasks, subject to CBF constraints, joint velocity bounds, and slack variable constraints
  • Kinematic Filter: Hierarchical objectives—primary task tracks non-contact end-effector and CoM operational-space motion, secondary task tracks null-space posture; QP is ||[W_t J_{t|c}; W_n N_{t|c}](q̇ − q̇_nom)||₂²
  • Dynamic Filter: Three-layer hierarchy—contact force tracking > motion tracking > posture tracking—solves for torques Γ_body*, but due to Unitree G1 interface limitations, maps back to PD targets (Equation 13) rather than sending torques directly

Key Design Decisions

  • Contact estimation heuristic: Determines contact mode (4 discrete modes) based on relative foot height and velocity thresholds, implicitly assuming mode 0 (no contact) does not occur
  • Discrete-time handling: Online control performs only a single-step differential IK per time step, avoiding multi-step integration that would misrepresent ḣ(z)
  • Slack variables: Slack variables t are introduced in the QP to handle constraint conflicts, with objective ‖u − u_nom‖₂² + ρ^T t, sacrificing strict guarantees for feasibility

Key Innovations

  1. Contact-constraint-consistent CBF design: Existing CBFs on humanoid robots use reduced-order models; this paper is the first to ground CBFs on full contact-constrained kinematics/dynamics (via N_c projection), ensuring safety filtering does not disrupt contact modes. This directly addresses the core issue of "safety filtering causing foot slipping."

  2. Three-port intervention framework: Provides safety intervention at three locations—policy input (retargeting), post-input (kinematic filtering), and post-output (dynamic filtering)—with composability. This "layered safety" design allows users to choose intervention depth based on computational budget and task requirements, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

  3. Task-consistent hierarchical QP structure: The filter QP objective aligns with the implicit task hierarchy of whole-body motion tracking (contact force > motion > posture), prioritizing modification of low-priority components when constraints activate, minimizing interference with task execution. This is the concrete implementation of the "minimal invasiveness" principle.

Experiments and Results

Experiments were conducted in Unitree G1 simulation using unmodified TWIST2 and SONIC policies, with 100 trials randomizing CBF parameters (table height, playback speed 0.5–2.0×).

Self-collision Base policy 37.59 / 37.68 97.43 / 100.41
Self-collision Kinematic safety 8.47 / 7.49 12.11 / 52.91
Self-collision Dynamic safety 0.35 / 1.24 0.50 / 0.43
Self-collision Kinematic + Dynamic 0.02 / 0.00 0.03 / 0.00

Karate Kinematic safety 12.55 / 7.59 100.28 / 64.59
Karate Dynamic safety 0.28 / 3.10 0.09 / 5.98
Karate Kinematic + Dynamic 0.05 / 0.29 0.02 / 0.25

Key conclusions: The dynamic filter alone reduces violations to below ~1% (calculated from table values 2.2÷2.3), and the kinematic + dynamic combination nearly eliminates violations (<0.3% (calculated from table values 3.1−3.4)). However, the kinematic filter alone performs poorly in the karate experiment (max violation 100 mm), indicating that dynamic-level safety intervention is indispensable for high-speed dynamic tasks.

Performance: constrained retargeting runs at up to 300 Hz, kinematic filter at 2000 Hz, dynamic filter at 500 Hz; in experiments, the dynamic filter ran at 250 Hz (5× policy frequency) and met real-time requirements even on a Jetson Orin Nano.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • No hardware experiments: Validated only in simulation, not tested on real Unitree G1; the authors acknowledge that imperfect actuators, inertial calibration errors, and unreliable contact estimation will degrade CBF hardware deployment performance
  • Dynamic filter evaluated only in double support (mode 3): Dynamic filtering under single-foot or no-contact modes is unexplored; the contact estimation heuristic implicitly assumes mode 0 does not occur
  • Disturbances not considered: The design goal is to minimize interference with RL policy recovery behavior, but external disturbances that could cause falls are not considered
  • Distribution shift: The dynamic filter may push policy inputs out-of-distribution (OOD); the authors do not quantify this effect
  • Onboard perception: Assumes known collision object states and geometry in the environment; onboard perception data is not used
  • QP feasibility: The QP may become infeasible when numerous CBF and input constraints conflict; slack variables only "execute without guaranteeing" safety; discrete-time sampling at low rates (~50 Hz) may cause boundary violations or chattering

Engineering Insights

When reproducing or deploying, first verify the following:

  1. Contact estimation is the biggest risk point: The heuristic based on relative foot height and velocity thresholds is reasonable for planar contacts and typical walking motions, but if tasks involve flight phases or non-planar contacts, a more robust estimator must be substituted. The dynamic filter is currently validated only in double support; under single-foot contact, S N_c may be rank-deficient (handled via pseudo-inverse), requiring additional attention to numerical stability.

  2. The torque mapping of the dynamic filter is platform-dependent: Unitree G1 interface limitations force mapping safe torques back to PD targets (Equation 13), introducing additional latency and approximation error. If the target platform supports direct torque commands (e.g., TORO at 1 kHz), direct torque mode should be preferred.

  3. Sampling rate determines CBF effectiveness: The authors note that at ~1000 Hz, discrete-time discrepancies are negligible, while at ~50 Hz, noticeable violations or chattering may occur. Running the dynamic filter at 250 Hz (5× policy frequency) is the safe lower bound; if computational budget is tight, prioritize the dynamic filter frequency over the kinematic filter.

  4. Combined use outperforms individual use: The kinematic + dynamic combination reduces violations to 0.00–0.29%, while the dynamic filter alone still has 0.35–3.10% violations. If task safety requirements are extremely high, do not omit the kinematic layer.

  5. JAX implementation details: Single-threaded execution (intra_op_parallelism_threads=1) performs better in ROS2 multi-node stacks; enable JAX_ENABLE_x64=1 to avoid numerical precision issues; all function calls require jax.block_until_ready() to avoid asynchronous scheduling timing errors.
