Summary

For robots to work safely in household environments, they need to be compliant and react to torque and force feedback during contact. However, the majority of existing data collection pipelines still lack the ability to capture force and torque data for learning active compliant policies. Institutions per source list: Ant Group、Stanford University.

Overview

UME (Universal Manipulation Exoskeleton) is a low-cost ($1900), 7-DoF wearable exoskeleton teleoperation system that achieves joint-level bidirectional torque feedback and cross-robot (OpenArm, Franka, X-ARM) teleoperation through coaxial actuator layout and a universal retargeting algorithm. The core contribution lies in incorporating torque data into the learning pipeline, enabling ACT policies to learn active compliant whole-arm behaviors, significantly outperforming torque-free baselines and the UMI paradigm on force-intensive tasks.

What It Changes

Existing teleoperation data collection pipelines (ALOHA, GELLO, UMI handheld) generally lack joint torque sensing. This is not merely a hardware gap but a ceiling on the learning paradigm—without torque data, policies can only learn positions/trajectories, not compliant behaviors such as "how much force to use" or "when to yield." What the authors truly change is upgrading teleoperation from a "motion capture tool" to a "force-sensing interaction interface": the operator feels resistance from arm-environment contact in real time while joint-level torque signals are recorded for policy learning. This directly addresses the root reason why VLA models and robot world models generally do not emphasize compliance—the lack of force and torque data.

Another pain point addressed is whole-arm configuration control. UMI-style handheld collection only records end-effector poses, relying on IK to resolve collision constraints during inference, which frequently fails in highly constrained spaces (e.g., GPU insertion, fridge retrieval). UME decomposes the exoskeleton and follower arm kinematic chains into three sub-manipulators—shoulder, elbow, and wrist—via universal retargeting, independently redirecting position, velocity, and torque. This avoids the singularity issues of 6D task-space control while allowing the operator to intuitively control the whole-arm configuration rather than just the end-effector.

Method Breakdown

Hardware Design

  • Coaxial actuator configuration: J1-J3 axes intersect at the shoulder ball joint center, J5-J7 axes intersect at the wrist ball joint center, and J4 corresponds to the elbow joint, replicating the full range of motion of the human shoulder/wrist.
  • Actuator selection: The shoulder uses the Damiao DM-J4340-2EC (9Nm, 362g, 40:1 reduction ratio), balancing portability, torque transparency, and the torque required for gravity compensation; cross-roller bearing versions (increased friction) and lower reduction ratio versions (excessively heavy stator) were rejected.
  • Total cost: $1900, weight approximately 12kg (PLA structure), comfortably wearable by a typical adult for 2 hours.

Universal Retargeting

The exoskeleton and follower arm are decomposed into three sub-manipulators: a virtual 3-DoF ball shoulder, a 1-DoF elbow, and a virtual 3-DoF ball wrist, each independently retargeted:

  1. Position feedforward: q_shoulder_exo is converted via FK to rotation matrix R_shoulder_exo, then mapped via IK to the follower arm q_shoulder_robot; the elbow is directly mirrored; the wrist follows the same procedure.
  2. Velocity feedforward: ω_shoulder = J_shoulder_exo · q̇_shoulder_exo, and the follower arm command is q̇_shoulder_robot = (J_shoulder_robot)† · ω_shoulder; for non-redundant 3-DoF sub-arms, the pseudoinverse simplifies to the standard inverse J† = J⁻¹.
  3. Torque feedback: Follower arm torque τ_shoulder_robot is mapped via the dynamically consistent transpose to spatial force F_shoulder = (J̄_shoulder_robot)^T · τ_shoulder_robot, where J̄ = A⁻¹J^T(JA⁻¹J^T)⁻¹; exoskeleton feedback torque is τ_shoulder_exo = (J_shoulder_exo)^T · F_shoulder; the elbow is directly mapped as τ_elbow_exo = τ_elbow_robot.
  4. Torque transparency: Command torque τ_command_exo = c(q, q̇) + g(q) + f(q̇) + τ_feedback_exo, including Coriolis, gravity, and friction compensation.

Mobile Base and Learning Framework

  • IMU control: A ~\(9 IMU's 3D Euler angles are linearly mapped to mobile base velocity [ẋ, ẏ, θ̇]; a VR headset (\)300) is an alternative.
  • Mobile base: An upgraded Tidybot++, replacing FRC swerve modules with Hexfellow PCW-25 powered casters ($900×4), where lower reduction ratios and reduced backlash improve compliance and odometry accuracy.
  • Learning: ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), ResNet18 backbone; torque data is processed identically to joint positions, with embeddings appended to image embeddings and fed into the Transformer encoder-decoder, outputting desired joint positions.

Key Innovations

  1. Teleoperation paradigm with joint-level bidirectional torque feedback: Unlike exoskeletons such as HOMIE and AirExo that only perform position teleoperation, UME lets the operator perceive resistance on the follower arm in real time, and torque data directly enters the learning pipeline. This is a paradigm shift from "motion data collection" to "force-sensing data collection," enabling learning of active compliant policies.

  2. Decoupled architecture for universal retargeting: The 7-DoF arm is decomposed into shoulder/elbow/wrist sub-manipulators that are independently retargeted, avoiding kinematic singularities in 6D end-effector task-space control, with each sub-arm's Jacobian inverse operating independently. This design allows the same exoskeleton to teleoperate robots with different kinematic configurations (OpenArm, Franka, X-ARM) without recalibration.

  3. Low-cost, high-transparency hardware design: Joint-level torque feedback is achieved at a total cost of $1900, balancing portability, torque transparency, and load support via quasi-direct-drive motors (Damiao/Unitree) and coaxial layout. Compared to approaches like AirExo, UME provides full bidirectional force interaction while maintaining low cost.

Experiments and Results

Table 1: Task success rates (20 trials each)

Method Box Pushing Box Flipping GPU Picking Fridge Drink Retrieval
No-torque 0.50 0 0.75 0.90
UMI 0.40 0 0 0
UME (Ours) 0.90 0.85 0.95 0.95
  • Box Pushing (26 demos): UME 0.9 vs No-torque 0.5 vs UMI 0.4, with torque feedback yielding an 80% relative improvement (calculated from table values 0.5→0.9).
  • Box Flipping (40 demos): UME 0.85, while No-torque and UMI both score 0—this is the most typical force-intensive task, where torque-free data is completely insufficient for learning.
  • GPU Picking (42 demos): Only 5mm clearance after gripper insertion; UME 0.95 vs No-torque 0.75, UMI 0—highly constrained spaces expose the fundamental flaw of the UMI end-effector pose + IK paradigm.
  • Fridge Drink Retrieval (157 demos): UME 0.95 vs No-torque 0.90, UMI 0; stage-wise success rates show UMI fails at the "take out drink" step (0.00), with all subsequent steps at 0.
  • Data collection throughput: On the box flipping task, UME achieves a 3.3× improvement over the torque-free baseline, running at 71% of human speed.
  • General teleoperation: Real-world successful teleoperation of dual-arm X-ARM picking up blocks, passing between arms, and placing into a cup; Franka demonstrated only in MuJoCo simulation.

Boundaries and Limitations

  • Hardware limitations: PLA links result in relatively high weight (~12kg) and lower payload than more expensive alternatives; link dimensions are fixed and non-adjustable, affecting reachability and ergonomics.
  • Franka simulation only: Due to delivery delays, Franka teleoperation was demonstrated only in MuJoCo, not validated on real hardware; the authors expect similar or better real-world performance (due to more accurate Franka joint torque sensing), but this expectation is unverified.
  • Not mentioned: Real Franka arm experiments, adjustable link versions, production-grade lightweight versions, inference frequency, and data volume statistics.
  • Conclusion boundaries: All success rate data is based on 20 trials, with statistical significance not reported; the data collection throughput improvement (3.3×) is measured only on the single box flipping task.

Engineering Insights

  • First verify torque data quality: UME's core value lies in torque feedback; when reproducing, prioritize validating exoskeleton torque transparency (whether Coriolis/gravity/friction compensation is sufficient), otherwise feedback torque noise will directly contaminate the learning signal.
  • Note the applicability conditions of sub-arm decomposition: Universal retargeting relies on the assumption that the shoulder/wrist are 3-DoF ball joints and the elbow is a 1-DoF revolute joint. If the target follower arm configuration does not satisfy this decomposition (e.g., a 4-DoF shoulder), the Jacobian and dynamically consistent inverse must be re-derived and cannot be directly applied.
  • Data volume reference: Force-intensive tasks (Box Flipping) require only 40 demos to reach 0.85 success rate, but Fridge Drink Retrieval requires 157 demos—task complexity and demo requirements grow nonlinearly; allocate data collection time based on task complexity.
  • Hardware selection pitfalls: Shoulder actuator selection is a key decision—the DM-J4340-2EC balances torque transparency and load support; choosing the cross-roller bearing version (DM-J4340P) reduces torque transparency due to increased friction, while the lower reduction ratio version (DM-J8006) sacrifices portability due to excessive stator weight.
  • Mobile base upgrade direction: The Tidybot++ swerve modules (\(300) were replaced with Hexfellow PCW-25 (\)900) due to lower gear ratios and reduced backlash—if budget-constrained, the original base can be retained but with degraded compliance and odometry accuracy.
  • Training configuration is directly reusable: The ACT hyperparameters (batch 200, ResNet18, 4 encoder layers, 7 decoder layers, 40k steps) are effective across all 4 tasks and can serve as a starting configuration for new tasks.
