Overview

Regional market certification is a combination of mandatory or de facto technical thresholds that humanoid robots must meet before entering the market of a specific country or region. It covers the EU CE marking, US UL certification and FCC certification, China CCC certification and CR robot certification, Japan PSE and Radio Law certification, among others. What it truly changes is not the testing process of a single product, but rather the elevation of safety, electromagnetic compatibility, and functional safety from "remedial items" after design to first-class inputs of the system architecture.

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Regional Market Certifications refer to the set of certification and marking requirements that products must satisfy when entering different geographic markets, based on local laws, regulations, and standardization systems. In the context of humanoid robots, this set typically includes mandatory certifications (such as CE marking, FCC, CCC) and voluntary but de facto threshold certifications (such as UL certification). Its essence is a "passport for market access"—if certification testing is not passed, the product cannot be brought to market.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Humanoid robots are mechatronic-software integrated products, involving mechanical motion, electrical systems, wireless communication, and software control simultaneously. If each market independently establishes its own safety rules, manufacturers would face duplicate testing, conflicting standards, and high compliance costs. Regional market certification exists precisely to strike a balance between consumer safety and trade facilitation.

Historically, robot safety standards have undergone a paradigm shift from "industrial fence isolation" to "human-robot collaboration." Early industrial robots (such as automotive production line manipulators) ensured safety through physical isolation, requiring no complex human-robot interaction certification. However, humanoid robots are naturally oriented toward unstructured environments such as homes, hospitals, and warehouses. Their bipedal dynamic balance, compliant control, and autonomous decision-making capabilities render traditional safety assessment frameworks ineffective. The current system applies a combination of ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13482, ISO 13849, IEC 61508, and regional market certifications (CE, UL, FCC, CCC), but bipedal dynamic safety, justification of learning-based components, and continuously updated systems remain standard gaps (engineering judgment: this gap remains a recognized industry compliance challenge around 2025).

Principle Breakdown

① The Layered Logic of Mandatory vs. Voluntary

Regional market certification is not monolithic. Taking the US market as an example, FCC Part 15 is mandatory—any device containing wireless transmission modules (all humanoid robot Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and remote control links fall into this category) must pass electromagnetic compatibility testing or it cannot be sold in the US. UL certification, on the other hand, is voluntary, but insurance companies typically only underwrite equipment that has passed UL certification, and retailers tend to only source UL-listed products, thus creating a "de facto threshold."

The EU CE marking employs a self-declaration + Notified Body model: manufacturers self-assess product compliance with relevant directives (Machinery Directive, Low Voltage Directive, EMC Directive, Radio Equipment Directive, etc.) and sign a Declaration of Conformity. For higher-risk products, third-party Notified Body involvement is required. The legal basis of CE marking is the "New Approach Directives" of EU regulations, whose core is the risk assessment documentation—manufacturers must demonstrate that reasonably foreseeable hazards have been identified and mitigated.

② Mapping Between Functional Safety Levels and Certification

Safety-related control systems of humanoid robots (such as emergency stop circuits, torque limiting, collision detection) must meet functional safety requirements. Functional safety levels are typically quantified using IEC 61508 SIL (Safety Integrity Level) or ISO 13849 PL (Performance Level). ISO 13849 primarily applies to safety-related control systems in machinery, and its PL levels correspond to the probability of dangerous failure per hour \(PFH_d\):

PL \(PFH_d\) (per hour) Typical Application
PL a \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-5}\) Low risk, tolerable failure
PL b \(3 \times 10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-5}\) Medium risk
PL c \(10^{-5}\) to \(3 \times 10^{-6}\) Higher risk
PL d \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-7}\) High risk (commonly used in human-robot collaboration)
PL e \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-8}\) Extremely high risk

(Note: The correspondence between PL levels and \(PFH_d\) comes from the ISO 13849 standard definitions, organized here based on corpus inference.)

Safety control systems of humanoid robots typically require PL d or above (engineering judgment), because they share workspaces with humans and the consequences of failure are severe. This level requirement directly determines the control system architecture—for example, dual-channel redundancy, diagnostic coverage, fail-safe design, etc.

③ The "Jigsaw Puzzle" Nature of Certification Combinations

For a humanoid robot to enter the EU market, it must simultaneously satisfy:

  • Machinery Directive (2006/42/EC): covering mechanical structure, guards, emergency stop functions;
  • Low Voltage Directive (2014/35/EU): electrical safety;
  • EMC Directive (2014/30/EU): electromagnetic compatibility;
  • Radio Equipment Directive (2014/53/EU): if wireless modules are included;
  • ISO 13482 (personal care robot safety) or ISO/TS 15066 (collaborative robot safety) as harmonized standards providing specific technical indicators.

These directives and standards do not exist in isolation but reference each other through the "harmonized standards" mechanism. Compliance with harmonized standards confers a presumption of conformity with directive requirements—this is the core logic of CE marking.

Horizontal Comparison

CE Marking vs. CCC Certification: Both are mandatory certifications, but their logic differs. CE marking is based on the "New Approach Directives" self-declaration model, where manufacturers bear primary compliance responsibility; CCC certification adopts a "designated certification body + type testing + factory inspection" model with deeper government involvement. CE marking covers the 27 EU member states, while CCC applies only to the mainland China market.

UL Certification vs. FCC Certification: UL is a voluntary safety certification, while FCC is a mandatory electromagnetic compatibility certification. UL focuses on the product's own safety performance (electrical, fire, mechanical), while FCC focuses on the product's interference with the wireless spectrum environment. The two are complementary and together form the market access combination for the US market.

ISO 13482 vs. ISO/TS 15066: ISO 13482 targets personal care robots (such as companion, transport, and mobility assistance), while ISO/TS 15066 targets industrial collaborative robots. The former emphasizes contact safety and speed/force limits, while the latter emphasizes maximum allowable pressure/force in collaborative scenarios. If a humanoid robot is used for home services, ISO 13482 is typically referenced; if used for industrial collaboration, ISO/TS 15066 is referenced.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Case One: Humanoid Robot Entering the EU Market

A Chinese OEM plans to sell bipedal humanoid robots to nursing homes in Germany. Its compliance path is: conduct risk assessment per ISO 13482, design a safety control system meeting PL d (dual-channel emergency stop, torque limiting), undergo Notified Body assessment for Machinery Directive compliance, complete EMC and Radio Equipment Directive testing, and finally affix the CE marking. The entire process takes 6-12 months (engineering judgment), with the risk assessment documentation being the core deliverable.

Case Two: Humanoid Robot Entering the US Market

A US startup develops warehouse logistics humanoid robots. Its compliance path is: pass FCC Part 15 certification (wireless remote control and communication modules), apply for UL 1740 certification (robot system safety assessment), and meet OSHA workplace requirements. Although UL certification is voluntary, warehouse customers (such as Amazon) typically require suppliers to provide UL listing proof; otherwise, procurement is not approved.

Case Three: Humanoid Robot Entering the Chinese Market

A Japanese robot company plans to sell care robots in China. Its compliance path is: obtain CCC certification (electrical safety) and apply for CR robot certification (robot-specific certification covering safety, EMC, performance, and reliability). CR certification is promoted by the China Robot Alliance and the Certification and Accreditation Administration, and is a voluntary certification for the robotics industry, but it has become a bonus point in government procurement and large project bidding.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Standard Gap: Bipedal Dynamic Safety

The current standard system primarily targets wheeled or fixed-base robots and lacks clear test methods for bipedal dynamic balance failures (such as falls, uncontrolled swinging). Neither ISO 13482 nor ISO/TS 15066 covers the dynamic safety scenarios of bipedal robots. This means manufacturers must define their own test protocols and bear the risk of interpretation (engineering judgment).

② The Challenge of Justifying Learning-Based Components

Humanoid robots increasingly employ machine learning (such as reinforcement learning control policies). Current functional safety standards (IEC 61508, ISO 13849) assume safety functions are implemented deterministically and cannot directly justify the safety of "learning-based components." How to demonstrate that a neural network controller's failure probability meets PL d requirements is an unresolved challenge in the current standard system.

③ Compliance Dilemma of Continuously Updated Systems

Humanoid robots update software functions via OTA, but certification testing is based on specific software versions. If an update changes safety-related behavior, is re-certification required? Current regulations lack clear guidance on this. Manufacturers typically adopt a strategy of "freezing safety functions + iterating application layers" (engineering judgment).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "CE marking is a quality certification"—Wrong. CE marking is a declaration of conformity, proving that the product meets the essential requirements of EU directives, not proof of quality superiority. Low-quality products can also bear the CE marking as long as they meet safety requirements.

  2. "UL certification is mandatory in the US"—Wrong. UL is a voluntary certification, but insurance, retail, and government procurement create a de facto threshold. Without UL certification, a product may not obtain commercial liability insurance or gain access to major retail channels.

  3. "CCC and CR certification are the same thing"—Wrong. CCC is China's compulsory product certification, covering general requirements such as electrical safety; CR is China's robot-specific certification, covering safety, EMC, performance, and reliability, and is voluntary. The two are complementary but different in nature.

  4. "Passing ISO 13482 is equivalent to satisfying the CE Machinery Directive"—Not entirely. ISO 13482 is a harmonized standard; meeting its requirements confers a presumption of conformity with relevant clauses of the Machinery Directive, but manufacturers must still complete the full risk assessment documentation and cover all applicable requirements of the directive.

  5. "FCC certification only applies to wireless modules"—Wrong. FCC Part 15 covers all devices containing digital circuits; even without wireless transmission, their electromagnetic radiation must remain below limits. The main control board and motor drivers of a humanoid robot must all meet FCC radiation limits.
