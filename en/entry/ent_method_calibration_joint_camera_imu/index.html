Overview

Joint-Camera-IMU Calibration is a key preliminary method in multi-sensor fusion for humanoid robots, used to precisely estimate the spatial transformation relationships (extrinsic parameters) between cameras, LiDAR, and IMUs. In humanoid robots, cameras provide dense texture and semantic information, LiDAR provides precise 3D geometry, and IMUs provide high-frequency motion states. Only when the three work together can functions such as RGB point cloud coloring, deep fusion, and 3D target localization be achieved. What it truly changes is not the calibration accuracy of a single sensor, but rather the fusion paradigm that moves heterogeneous sensors from "working independently" to a "unified spatiotemporal reference frame."

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition

Joint-Camera-IMU Calibration refers to the process of estimating the rigid body transformation relationships among cameras, LiDAR, and IMUs within the same reference frame. Specifically, it requires solving for the extrinsic parameters between the camera and LiDAR (rotation matrix \(\mathbf{R}_{CL}\) and translation vector \(\mathbf{t}_{CL}\)), between the camera and IMU (\(\mathbf{R}_{CI}, \mathbf{t}_{CI}\)), and between the LiDAR and IMU (\(\mathbf{R}_{LI}, \mathbf{t}_{LI}\)). These extrinsics form a closed-loop constraint: \(\mathbf{R}_{CL} = \mathbf{R}_{CI} \mathbf{R}_{IL}\), where \(\mathbf{R}_{IL}\) represents the rotation from the IMU to the LiDAR. The core idea of joint calibration is to use data synchronously collected from multiple sensors and, through feature correspondence and nonlinear optimization, solve for all extrinsics simultaneously rather than calibrating each one independently.

Key Terms: - Extrinsic parameters: The rotation and translation between two sensor coordinate systems. - Reprojection error: The distance between a LiDAR point projected onto an image and its corresponding image feature. - Point cloud registration: Algorithms that align multiple point clouds into the same coordinate system, such as ICP. - Calibration target: A board with known geometric features (checkerboard, circular holes, cutout patterns) used to extract corresponding features. - Hand-eye calibration: A classic method for estimating the transformation between a camera and a robot arm's end effector; joint calibration is its multi-sensor generalization.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

Before multi-sensor fusion systems emerged, calibration problems for single sensors (e.g., camera-only or LiDAR-only) were relatively independent. However, as humanoid robots need to simultaneously rely on visual, laser, and inertial information for navigation, obstacle avoidance, and manipulation tasks, spatial alignment between sensors became a bottleneck. If extrinsics are inaccurate, RGB point cloud coloring suffers from color misalignment, deep fusion produces ghosting artifacts, and 3D target localization can drift by tens of centimeters—which is fatal in precision manipulation scenarios for humanoid robots.

Historically, camera-LiDAR calibration has evolved from target-based methods to targetless automatic calibration. Early methods relied on checkerboards or circular hole boards, solving extrinsics by extracting 3D-2D correspondences; later, automatic calibration methods using natural scene features (such as edges and planes) were developed. The addition of IMUs further complicated the problem: IMUs provide high-frequency angular velocity and acceleration, which need to be aligned both temporally and spatially with the lower-frequency data from cameras and LiDAR. Joint calibration was born precisely to address this "multi-sensor, multi-modal, multi-frequency" alignment problem.

Principle Breakdown

① Coordinate Transformation Chain: From LiDAR to Pixels

Let a spatial point be \(\mathbf{P}_W\) in the world/calibration target coordinate system, \(\mathbf{P}_C\) in the camera coordinate system, and \(\mathbf{P}_L\) in the LiDAR coordinate system. The camera intrinsic matrix is \(\mathbf{K}\), and the camera-LiDAR extrinsics are \((\mathbf{R}_{CL}, \mathbf{t}_{CL})\). Then:

\[ \mathbf{P}_C = \mathbf{R}_{CL} \mathbf{P}_L + \mathbf{t}_{CL} \]
\[ \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{K} \mathbf{P}_C / Z_C \]

where \(\mathbf{p} = [u, v, 1]^T\) is the image pixel coordinate and \(Z_C\) is the depth in the camera coordinate system. The first equation establishes the rigid body transformation from LiDAR to camera, and the second completes the projection from the camera coordinate system to pixel coordinates. The essence of joint calibration is to solve for \((\mathbf{R}_{CL}, \mathbf{t}_{CL})\) by minimizing the reprojection error.

② Calibration Target Method: Establishing 3D-2D Correspondences

The most commonly used calibration targets are checkerboard boards or aluminum plates with circular holes. The steps are as follows:

  1. Camera corner detection: Detect checkerboard corners in the image to obtain pixel coordinates \(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\).
  2. LiDAR feature extraction: Extract the calibration target plane or circular hole edges from the LiDAR point cloud and fit to obtain 3D coordinates \(\mathbf{P}_{L,ij}\).
  3. Establish correspondences: Pair 3D points and 2D pixel points on the same calibration target to form a set of 3D-2D correspondences.
  4. Solve for extrinsics: Use the PnP (Perspective-n-Point) algorithm to obtain initial values, then refine with ICP (Iterative Closest Point) or nonlinear optimization (e.g., Levenberg-Marquardt).

③ Introduction of IMU: Dual Alignment in Time and Space

IMUs provide high-frequency (typically 100–1000 Hz) angular velocity and acceleration, while cameras and LiDAR typically operate at 10–30 Hz. Joint calibration needs to address two issues: temporal synchronization (interpolating IMU data to the camera/LiDAR acquisition timestamps) and spatial alignment (estimating extrinsics between the IMU and other sensors). Spatial alignment typically leverages motion excitation: during calibration, the operator moves the sensor platform with rotations and translations, and the attitude changes obtained by integrating IMU data should be consistent with those estimated from vision/LiDAR. The extrinsics are solved by minimizing the difference between the two.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Joint calibration is a standard prerequisite in the perception systems of humanoid robots and is widely applied in the following scenarios:

  1. RGB point cloud coloring: Project LiDAR point clouds onto camera images to assign RGB colors to each 3D point. Inaccurate extrinsics cause color misalignment, affecting subsequent semantic segmentation and object recognition.
  2. Deep fusion: Fuse camera depth maps with LiDAR point clouds to generate high-density, high-precision depth maps for obstacle avoidance and navigation.
  3. 3D target localization: Use cameras to detect targets (e.g., people, objects) and combine with LiDAR ranging to achieve precise 3D localization and tracking of targets.
  4. SLAM and mapping: Visual-laser-inertial fusion SLAM (e.g., VINS-Fusion, LIO-SAM) relies on accurate extrinsic initialization; joint calibration is the first step in system startup.
  5. Humanoid robot manipulation: In grasping and manipulation tasks, target positions identified by vision need to be transformed into the robot arm coordinate system; extrinsic accuracy directly affects manipulation success rates.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Limited calibration target coverage: The calibration target can only cover a portion of the sensor field of view. If sensors are installed with large positional differences (e.g., camera facing forward, LiDAR facing upward), the target may need to be placed multiple times.
  2. Temporal synchronization errors: Joint calibration assumes sensor data is temporally synchronized. If synchronization errors are large (e.g., > 10 ms), calibration results degrade significantly. Hardware synchronization or high-precision software synchronization is required in practice.
  3. Insufficient motion excitation: Motion-based methods require sufficient rotational and translational excitation. If motion amplitude is too small or degenerate (e.g., pure translation), certain extrinsic components become unobservable.
  4. Environmental sensitivity: Targetless methods rely on scene features such as edges and planes, which may fail in open spaces or environments with repetitive textures.
  5. Calibration drift: Over long-term use, mechanical vibration and thermal expansion/contraction can cause extrinsic drift, requiring periodic recalibration.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Calibrate once and it's done forever"—Wrong. Humanoid robots experience vibration and temperature changes during operation, and extrinsics may drift. In practice, recalibration is recommended at each deployment or periodically (e.g., monthly).
  2. "The smaller the reprojection error, the better"—Not entirely true. An excessively small reprojection error may indicate overfitting (e.g., too many target placements), which reduces generalization. Engineering judgment: errors between 0.5–1 pixel are typically sufficient.
  3. "Joint calibration is just hand-eye calibration"—Wrong. Hand-eye calibration only addresses the relationship between the camera and the robot arm's end effector, while joint calibration simultaneously handles the camera, LiDAR, and IMU, and also involves temporal synchronization, making it more complex.
  4. "IMU extrinsics can be ignored"—Wrong. Inaccurate IMU extrinsics directly affect the accuracy of VIO (Visual-Inertial Odometry) and LIO (LiDAR-Inertial Odometry), leading to localization drift.
