Overview

The center of mass (CoM) is the weighted average position of the mass distribution in a humanoid robot's multi-link system. It aggregates the linear and angular momentum of all robot links at the center of mass, forming a unified centroidal momentum description. Centroidal momentum is a powerful tool for analyzing dynamic motions such as walking, running, and jumping, compressing complex whole-body dynamics into a 6-dimensional momentum state at the CoM. This reduces motion planning and control problems from high-dimensional joint space to low-dimensional momentum space. The Centroidal Momentum Matrix (CMM) establishes a linear mapping from generalized velocities to centroidal momentum, serving as the bridge connecting joint space and momentum space.

Content

What it is: From multi-link to single point mass

A humanoid robot is fundamentally a multi-link system composed of dozens of rigid bodies connected by joints. Directly analyzing the independent motion state of each link requires handling dozens or even hundreds of degrees of freedom, resulting in extremely high computational complexity. The concept of the center of mass (CoM) provides a shortcut: regardless of the internal complexity of the system, its overall translational motion is equivalent to the motion of a single point mass with a mass equal to the total mass and a position at the CoM.

Centroidal momentum further extends this idea. It aggregates the linear and angular momentum of all robot links at the CoM, forming a 6-dimensional vector: the first 3 dimensions are the total linear momentum \(\mathbf{P}\), and the last 3 dimensions are the total angular momentum relative to the CoM (centroidal angular momentum) \(\mathbf{L}_G\). The core value of this description is: what it truly changes is not "how to describe each joint," but "how to describe the motion tendency of the entire system"—projecting problems from high-dimensional joint space to low-dimensional momentum space, making the analysis and planning of dynamic motions feasible.

Why it exists: Pain points and historical positioning

Before the centroidal momentum framework, dynamic motion analysis for humanoid robots faced a dilemma:

  • Full-order dynamics models (such as Euler-Lagrange equations) are accurate but computationally expensive, making them difficult to use for real-time control;
  • Simplified models (such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum, LIP) are computationally efficient but lose whole-body dynamics information, failing to capture the effects of arm swinging or torso rotation on overall motion.

The centroidal momentum framework fills this gap. It does not lose whole-body dynamics information—the momentum of all links is aggregated at the CoM; at the same time, it is low-dimensional—only 6 degrees of freedom. This makes it an ideal bridge between "whole-body dynamics" and "real-time control." In terms of historical positioning, the centroidal momentum framework is an intermediate layer between simplified models (such as LIP, ALIP) and whole-body models. It preserves the influence of whole-body dynamics on overall motion while keeping computational complexity within a real-time feasible range.

Principle breakdown

① Centroidal Momentum Matrix: Linear mapping from generalized velocities to momentum space

Define the robot's generalized coordinates \(\mathbf{q}\) and generalized velocities \(\mathbf{v}\). The Centroidal Momentum Matrix (CMM) \(\mathbf{A}_G(\mathbf{q})\) maps generalized velocities to the 6-dimensional centroidal momentum at the CoM:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{P} \\ \mathbf{L}_G \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A}_G(\mathbf{q}) \, \mathbf{v} \]

where \(\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the total linear momentum and \(\mathbf{L}_G \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is the total angular momentum relative to the CoM. The Centroidal Momentum Matrix has a clear physical meaning: its row vectors represent the contribution weights of each joint velocity to the overall linear and angular momentum. Each column of the matrix corresponds to a generalized velocity component, each row corresponds to a momentum component, and the magnitude of the matrix elements directly reflects the degree of influence of that joint velocity on the corresponding momentum component.

② Rate of change of momentum: The role of external forces and contact forces

The time derivative of centroidal momentum equals the sum of external forces:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{P} \\ \mathbf{L}_G \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m \mathbf{g} + \sum_j \mathbf{F}_j \\ \sum_j (\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_G) \times \mathbf{F}_j \end{bmatrix} \]

where \(\mathbf{F}_j\) is the \(j\)-th external contact force, \(\mathbf{r}_j\) is its point of application, \(\mathbf{r}_G\) is the CoM position, \(m\) is the total mass, and \(\mathbf{g}\) is the gravitational acceleration. This equation reveals the essence of dynamic motion: the change in linear momentum is determined by external forces (gravity + contact forces), and the change in angular momentum is determined by the moments of external forces about the CoM. Internal joint torques do not directly change centroidal momentum—they indirectly affect it by changing the joint configuration, which alters the Centroidal Momentum Matrix and thus the momentum distribution.

③ Connection between centroidal momentum and the Zero Moment Point (ZMP)

The centroidal momentum framework has a deep connection with the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) theory. The ZMP is the location of the equivalent point of application of the ground reaction force, and it must lie within the support polygon to guarantee walking stability. There is an integral relationship between the horizontal components of centroidal momentum and the ZMP position: the ZMP position determines the rate of change of the horizontal CoM momentum. This connection allows the centroidal momentum framework to describe "overall motion tendency" while seamlessly integrating with the classical stability criterion (ZMP criterion).

Key parameters and specifications

Parameter Symbol Physical meaning Typical characteristics
Total linear momentum \(\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) Overall translational motion state of the system Proportional to CoM velocity: \(\mathbf{P} = m \mathbf{v}_{\text{CoM}}\)
Centroidal angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}_G \in \mathbb{R}^3\) Rotational motion state of the system about the CoM Reflects the aggregation of whole-body rotational motion
Centroidal Momentum Matrix \(\mathbf{A}_G(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times n}\) Mapping from generalized velocities to momentum space Number of rows is fixed at 6; number of columns equals the number of generalized coordinates
CoM position \(\mathbf{r}_G \in \mathbb{R}^3\) Mass-weighted average position Determined by the mass and position of each link
Total mass \(m\) Total mass of the system Sum of the masses of all links

The dimension of the Centroidal Momentum Matrix depends on the number of generalized coordinates: for a robot with \(n\) degrees of freedom, \(\mathbf{A}_G\) is a \(6 \times n\) matrix. Each row corresponds to a momentum component (3 linear + 3 angular), and each column corresponds to a generalized velocity component. The matrix elements are functions of the joint configuration \(\mathbf{q}\) and vary with the robot's posture.

Horizontal comparison

Model Dimension Computational complexity Expressiveness Applicable scenarios
Full-order dynamics (Euler-Lagrange) \(n\) (degrees of freedom) High Complete Offline simulation, precise analysis
Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) 2-3 Very low CoM horizontal motion only Gait planning, real-time control
Centroidal momentum framework 6 Low-medium Whole-body momentum aggregation Dynamic motion analysis, MPC
Single Rigid Body model (SRB) 6 Low Ignores joint momentum contributions Simplified MPC

The unique positioning of the centroidal momentum framework is that it retains more whole-body dynamics information than the LIP model (momentum contributions from all links) while being far more computationally efficient than full-order models. Compared to the Single Rigid Body model, the centroidal momentum framework explicitly models the contribution of joint motion to overall momentum through the CMM, rather than simply ignoring it. This difference is particularly significant in high-speed dynamic motions (such as running and jumping), where the contribution of joint motion to overall angular momentum cannot be neglected.

Who uses it: Application cases

The centroidal momentum framework has broad applications in the field of humanoid robots:

  • Dynamic walking control: Centroidal momentum-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) is used for dynamic walking in robots such as Boston Dynamics Atlas and Unitree H1. By using centroidal momentum as a state variable, MPC can optimize ground reaction forces online to achieve disturbance-resistant stable walking.
  • Jumping and backflips: Atlas's backflip relies on precise control of centroidal momentum—during the flight phase, centroidal momentum is conserved (the only external force is gravity), and the robot adjusts its joint configuration to redistribute centroidal angular momentum, achieving in-air posture adjustment.
  • Whole-body motion imitation: In motion imitation tasks, centroidal momentum trajectories can serve as reference signals to guide policy learning for whole-body coordinated motion. The centroidal momentum framework converts the vague concept of "whole-body coordination" into a concrete 6-dimensional momentum tracking problem.
  • Disturbance-robust control: In hierarchical MPC frameworks, the high level runs a simplified-model NMPC at a lower frequency to plan centroidal momentum trajectories, while the mid level runs whole-body MPC at a higher frequency to track those trajectories, achieving a balance between computational efficiency and robustness.

Limitations and boundaries

The centroidal momentum framework is not a panacea; it has clear boundaries:

  • Loss of joint-space information: Centroidal momentum is a 6-dimensional aggregation and cannot express specific joint configurations. Two completely different joint postures can produce the same centroidal momentum, so the centroidal momentum framework cannot uniquely determine the robot's whole-body state.
  • Requires inverse mapping: Reconstructing joint motion from desired centroidal momentum requires solving an inverse mapping, which is typically an underdetermined problem (6 equations, \(n\) unknowns) requiring additional optimization criteria (such as minimum kinetic energy or minimum joint torque) to solve.
  • Contact force assumptions: The centroidal momentum dynamics equations assume that contact forces are the only external forces (besides gravity), neglecting secondary factors such as air resistance and friction. These assumptions may fail in high-speed motions or extreme postures.
  • Computational real-time performance: Computing the Centroidal Momentum Matrix \(\mathbf{A}_G(\mathbf{q})\) requires iterating over the mass and position of all links, which still incurs some computational overhead on robots with high degrees of freedom. Real-time performance on specific hardware platforms must be verified independently.

Common misconceptions

  1. "Centroidal momentum is just CoM velocity times mass"—inaccurate. \(\mathbf{P} = m \mathbf{v}_{\text{CoM}}\) only describes the linear momentum component; centroidal momentum is a 6-dimensional vector that also includes the angular momentum relative to the CoM, \(\mathbf{L}_G\). The angular momentum component reflects the aggregation of whole-body rotational motion and is an indispensable part of dynamic motions (such as turning and jumping).

  2. "Conservation of centroidal momentum means the robot cannot rotate"—incorrect. When centroidal momentum is conserved (the resultant external force is zero and the resultant external moment is zero), the robot can still redistribute the ratio between linear and angular momentum by changing its joint configuration. For example, during the flight phase, a robot can swing its arms to change its body posture while maintaining conservation of centroidal momentum.

  3. "The Centroidal Momentum Matrix is a constant matrix"—incorrect. \(\mathbf{A}_G(\mathbf{q})\) is a function of the joint configuration \(\mathbf{q}\) and varies with the robot's posture. This means that the same joint velocities produce different centroidal momentum in different postures, and this nonlinear characteristic is one of the core challenges in dynamic motion control.

  4. "The centroidal momentum framework can replace whole-body dynamics"—incorrect. The centroidal momentum framework is a dimensionality reduction tool that sacrifices joint-space information for computational efficiency. For tasks requiring precise joint-level control (such as dexterous manipulation), full-order dynamics models are still necessary.
