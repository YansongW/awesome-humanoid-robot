Overview

Concept scheme divergence and convergence (P1.2.1) is a core process in the system scheme design phase of humanoid robot development. It systematically evaluates various whole-machine layouts, actuation schemes, and sensing schemes using tools such as brainstorming, morphological sketches, Morphological Matrix, and Pugh Matrix, ultimately converging on at least 3 concept schemes and freezing the selected one. What it truly changes is not the number of schemes, but the decision-making approach—from "guesswork" to "traceable."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Concept scheme divergence and convergence is a third-level subtask under the P1.2 System Scheme Design work package within the Requirements Definition and System Scheme (Concept / Pre-A) phase, with entity ID ent_process_p1_2_1. Its upstream input comes from the product specifications and platform strategy frozen by P1.1 Requirements Engineering, while its downstream deliverables connect to the mechanical, electrical, and software interface definitions in the detailed design phase.

In WBS V3, this process is broken down into five fourth-level key actions: input review and target quantification, scheme/method design, implementation/prototype/sample fabrication, verification and issue closure, and documentation output and downstream delivery. Each action has clear inputs, methods, outputs, and acceptance criteria, forming a complete closed loop.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

In early humanoid robot development, teams often make two types of errors: first, insufficient divergence—relying solely on an engineer's personal experience to directly select a layout, thereby missing better actuation schemes or sensing configurations; second, delayed convergence—oscillating between multiple schemes, leading to repeated rework during the detailed design phase.

The existence of concept scheme divergence and convergence is precisely to use structured methods to exhaustively explore possibilities and then use quantitative scoring matrices to compress uncertainty at the stage of maximum design freedom and lowest change cost. What it truly changes is not the number of schemes, but the decision-making approach—from "guesswork" to "traceable"—where every trade-off is documented, and every rejected scheme has a clear rationale.

Principle Breakdown

① Divergence Phase: Combinatorial Explosion Control with Morphological Matrix

The Morphological Matrix decomposes the system into several independent functional dimensions (e.g., whole-machine layout, actuation scheme, sensing scheme, cooling method, cable routing), listing all feasible implementation options under each dimension. Theoretically, the total number of combinations is the product of the number of options across dimensions:

\[ N_{\text{total}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} m_i \]

where \(m_i\) is the number of options for the \(i\)-th dimension and \(n\) is the total number of dimensions. Engineering judgment: if \(N_{\text{total}}\) exceeds 50, constraints (e.g., technology maturity, cost limits) must be introduced for pre-screening; otherwise, the evaluation workload becomes unmanageable.

② Convergence Phase: Weighted Scoring with Pugh Matrix

The Pugh Matrix uses a baseline scheme as a reference, scoring each candidate scheme against each evaluation criterion (+1, 0, -1), then multiplying by weights and summing:

\[ S_j = \sum_{k=1}^{p} w_k \cdot r_{jk} \]

where \(S_j\) is the total score of the \(j\)-th scheme, \(w_k\) is the weight of the \(k\)-th criterion, and \(r_{jk}\) is the relative score of the \(j\)-th scheme under the \(k\)-th criterion. The scheme with the highest total score wins, but a written rationale must accompany the weight allocation.

③ Closed-Loop Logic of the Five-Step Method

Each fourth-level key action is not isolated: input review ensures a clear starting point, scheme design ensures a clear path, prototype fabrication ensures testable assumptions, verification closure ensures no issues are missed, and documentation delivery ensures knowledge transfer. These five steps form a complete PDCA cycle.

Horizontal Comparison

The differences between concept scheme divergence and convergence and adjacent processes are:

  • Compared to P1.1 Requirements Engineering: Requirements engineering answers "what to do" (What), while this process answers "how to do it" (How). Requirements engineering freezes product specifications; this process freezes implementation schemes.
  • Compared to P1.2.2 Detailed Design: This process only conducts scheme-level论证, without involving specific part dimensions, circuit schematics, or code implementation; detailed design is a deep expansion based on the selected scheme.
  • Compared to P0 Project Initiation: P0 determines target cost, target volume, and target market; this process performs technical selection under P0 constraints without altering the commercial baseline.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

Application Scenario 1: Whole-Machine Layout Scheme Selection

The team proposed three whole-machine layouts during the divergence phase: traditional upright, wheel-leg hybrid, and quadruped deformable. Using the Morphological Matrix, they decomposed the problem into four dimensions—DOF allocation, center-of-gravity height, foot sensing, and joint module selection—generating 12 candidate schemes. After weighted scoring with the Pugh Matrix using technology maturity, cost, and manufacturability as criteria, the upright layout was selected, with the rationale being its lowest technical risk and highest compatibility with the existing supply chain.

Application Scenario 2: Actuation Scheme Comparison

In the actuation scheme dimension, candidates included hydraulic actuation, electro-hydraulic hybrid actuation, direct-drive motors (QDD), and motor + harmonic reducer. Scoring results showed that motor + harmonic reducer was optimal in balancing cost and precision, while QDD performed better in transparency (force control capability). The final trade-off was determined based on product positioning (education/research vs. industrial application) and documented accordingly.

Limitations and Boundaries

  1. Subjectivity of the scoring matrix: The weight allocation in the Pugh Matrix relies on team experience, and different teams may reach different conclusions. Mitigation: introduce cross-functional reviews (mechanical, electrical, software, manufacturing, and cost engineers participating together).
  2. Risk of insufficient divergence: If the dimension decomposition in the Morphological Matrix is incomplete, key schemes may be missed. Engineering judgment: it is recommended to cover at least five dimensions—whole-machine layout, actuation scheme, sensing scheme, energy scheme, and cooling scheme.
  3. Limited depth of prototype verification: Prototype fabrication in this process focuses on "key step verification" and does not require a full prototype. For high-risk innovations, dedicated pre-research may need to be initiated early.
  4. Does not address requirement changes: If upstream requirements undergo major changes after scheme freezing, the outputs of this process may be invalidated, requiring re-entry into the divergence and convergence process.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "More divergence is always better"—Wrong. The purpose of divergence is to cover the possibility space, not to pile up quantity. When the number of combinations exceeds 50, evaluation costs rise sharply, and constraint-based pre-screening is necessary.
  2. "The Pugh Matrix is objective decision-making"—Wrong. Weight setting itself is subjective judgment; the matrix merely makes subjective judgment explicit and traceable, not eliminates it.
  3. "Once a scheme is frozen, it cannot be changed"—Wrong. Freezing means changes require a formal change process (updating documents, re-review, notifying downstream), not a complete ban on changes.
  4. "Skip prototype verification and converge directly"—Wrong. For key technical assumptions (e.g., peak torque of a new joint module), simulation or simple prototype verification is mandatory; otherwise, the scoring matrix is built on a house of cards.
  5. "Documentation output is done at the end"—Wrong. Writing documents per templates and citing raw data should be recorded synchronously during each fourth-level action, not retroactively.
