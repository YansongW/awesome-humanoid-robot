Summary

A high-ratio, zero-backlash gear reducer based on a flexible spline, circular spline, and wave generator, valued in humanoid joints for compact size, high torque capacity, and precise motion transmission.

Sources

  • Identification of a Physics-Based Electrical Power Consumption Model for the Unitree G1 Humanoid Arm

Overview

A harmonic drive (also known as strain wave gearing) is a precision transmission device that achieves speed reduction through the controlled elastic deformation of a thin-walled flexible gear. It consists of three core components: a wave generator, a flexspline, and a circular spline. A single stage can achieve reduction ratios of 30:1–160:1 while maintaining near-zero backlash and high torque density, making it the mainstream precision reduction solution for industrial robot wrists, collaborative robots, and high-torque joints in humanoid robots (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The three core components of a harmonic drive (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2):

  • Wave generator: An elliptical cam + thin-wall bearing, serving as the input, responsible for deforming the flexspline into an ellipse;
  • Flexspline: A thin-walled cup-shaped external gear that undergoes periodic elastic deformation with the wave generator, typically serving as the output;
  • Circular spline: A rigid internal gear with more teeth than the flexspline (typically 2 more), usually fixed.

Its fundamental difference from traditional gear transmission: it does not rely on direct pushing of rigid tooth surfaces but on the elastic deformation traveling wave of metal to transmit torque—this principle was proposed by American inventor C. Walton Musser and commercialized by United Shoe Machinery Corporation (USM, predecessor of Harmonic Drive LLC) and Hasegawa Gear Works (predecessor of Harmonic Drive Systems) (Source: Harmonic Drive official website, Musser inventor page).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

Before harmonic drives emerged, the only approach to achieving precision and high reduction ratios in gear mechanisms was to "make gears more rigid and more precise"—multi-stage planetary gear trains stacked to 100:1 caused size, weight, and backlash to spiral out of control. Musser's insight was to reverse the thinking: leverage metal's flexibility rather than fight it, allowing the gear ring to elastically deform to achieve simultaneous meshing of multiple teeth (Source: Harmonic Drive official website, Musser page, describing this shift in principle).

The robotics industry provided the decisive application: joints needed to output tens to hundreds of N·m within a space the size of a fist to a melon, with backlash small enough not to compromise trajectory accuracy, and a flat coaxial form factor for cable routing. Harmonic drives uniquely satisfy all three requirements—"single-stage high reduction ratio + zero backlash + hollow flat design"—whereas planetary/RV solutions incur size or structural penalties for these features. What it truly changed was not the reduction ratio number, but the form factor of joints—robot joints could now be "pancakes" rather than "sugar skewers."

Principle Breakdown

① Kinematics of Strain Wave Reduction

Let the circular spline tooth count be \(z_c\) and the flexspline tooth count be \(z_f\). For each revolution of the wave generator, the flexspline "lags" behind the circular spline by \(\Delta z = z_c - z_f\) tooth pitches due to having fewer teeth. Therefore, the reduction ratio is (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2):

\[ G = \frac{\omega_{in}}{\omega_{out}} = \frac{z_c}{z_c - z_f} \]

With \(z_c = 200\), \(z_f = 198\) (tooth difference of 2), \(G = 100\). Common single-stage reduction ratios are 30:1–160:1 (same source).

② Source of Zero Backlash and High Precision

There are two meshing zones at the ends of the wave generator's elliptical major axis, propagating circumferentially like a "strain wave" as it rotates; the number of simultaneously meshing teeth can reach 25%–30% of the total tooth count. Multi-tooth load sharing + flexspline preload bring backlash near zero and average out individual tooth errors (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2). The official product page specifies standard accuracy of 1 arc min (Source: Harmonic Drive official website, CSF-32-100-2A-GR product page).

③ Flexspline Stress and Fatigue—Physical Constraints on Lifespan

The wave generator deforms the flexspline into an ellipse, subjecting it to cyclic hoop tensile stress \(\sigma_{hoop} \approx E\,e/r_f\) (\(e\) is ellipticity, \(r_f\) is the flexspline neutral circle radius); more refined shell analysis adds bending stress \(\sigma_{bend} \approx Ete/(2r_f^2)\). Fatigue cracks most easily initiate in the diaphragm region at the transition between the flexspline cup bottom and the output flange. High-end products therefore apply shot peening, tooth root fillet optimization, and heat treatment to the flexspline; Harmonic Drive technical documentation gives typical flexspline fatigue life of \(10^7\)–\(10^8\) revolutions (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2 and its cited Harmonic Drive technical documentation).

④ Piecewise Torsional Stiffness Characteristics

A harmonic drive is not an ideal rigid body: output torque vs. torsional angle is piecewise linear—stiffness \(K_1\) in the low-torque segment, \(K_2\) in the middle, and \(K_3\) in the high-torque segment. For Size 32 as an example: \(K_1 = 6.7\times10^4\) N·m/rad within \(T_1 = 29\) N·m, \(K_2 = 11\times10^4\) within \(T_2 = 108\) N·m, and \(K_3 = 12\times10^4\) N·m/rad above that (Source: Harmonic Drive official website, CSF-32-100-2A-GR product page). This means "zero backlash ≠ zero transmission error"—elastic deformation and hysteresis always exist.

Who Uses It / Application Cases

  • Industrial robots: The de facto standard precision reduction solution for six-axis robot wrist and forearm joints (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2 application scenarios);
  • Humanoid robots: Tesla Optimus rotary joints reportedly adopt an integrated "frameless torque motor + harmonic drive" solution (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 citing Tesla AI Day 2022 public information); Honda ASIMO (2011) used servo motors + harmonic drives (same chapter, joint comparison table);
  • Collaborative robots: Zero backlash + flat hollow design (allowing cable routing) makes harmonic drives the mainstream choice for collaborative arm joints;
  • Semiconductor/Medical/CNC: Wafer turntables, surgical robot joints, fourth/fifth-axis rotary tables (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.3.2);
  • Major manufacturers: Harmonic Drive Systems/Harmonic Drive LLC (Japan/US, origin of the invention), with Chinese manufacturers such as Leaderdrive and Lafleet already supplying in volume (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.8 supply chain; graph: ent_company_harmonic_drive_systems_2024, ent_company_leaderdrive_2024).

Limitations and Boundaries

  • Fatigue life is a hard constraint: The flexspline undergoes a complete stress cycle per revolution, with lifespan designed for 10⁷–10⁸ revolutions; high-load, high-speed conditions require verification against the supplier's life curves;
  • Efficiency drifts with operating conditions: The 70–90% efficiency range means heat generation and energy loss are non-negligible, and starting torque rises significantly at low temperatures;
  • Poor back-drivability: High reduction ratio + internal friction make the output difficult to back-drive (back-drive torque of 18–20 N·m, see parameter table), unsuitable for joints requiring force transparency—this is precisely why the QDD approach exists;
  • Nonlinear torsional stiffness: Piecewise stiffness + hysteresis complicate high-bandwidth servo tuning;
  • Concentrated supply chain: The high-end harmonic drive market has long been dominated by a few Japanese companies; domestic alternatives have only recently scaled up (Source: Project Wiki Chapter 4 §4.8).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Zero backlash = zero error"—Near-zero backlash does not mean zero transmission error: elastic deformation, piecewise stiffness, and hysteresis still limit dynamic accuracy.
  2. "Higher reduction ratio is always better"—Reduction ratio simultaneously amplifies reflected inertia (∝G²) and friction; high-dynamic joints are actually shifting toward lower reduction ratios (QDD).
  3. "Harmonic drives are fragile because they fear impact"—Instantaneous peak torque can reach 4.7× the rated L10 (647/137, calculated from the parameter table); short-term overload capacity is actually strong. The real weakness is fatigue under long-term cyclic stress.
  4. "Efficiency is a nameplate constant"—70–90% is an operating range, not a fixed value; efficiency drops significantly at low speed, high torque, and low temperatures.
  5. "Domestic harmonic drives = low-end substitutes"—Leaderdrive and others are already supplying humanoid robot supply chains in volume, and the accuracy gap is narrowing (engineering judgment; specific metrics should be verified against each manufacturer's datasheet).
