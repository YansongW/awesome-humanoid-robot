Summary

A foundational feedback control method that computes a control signal as a linear combination of proportional, integral, and derivative terms of the tracking error.

Sources

  • K. J. Åström and T. Hägglund, Advanced PID Control, ISA, 2006
  • K. Ogata, Modern Control Engineering, 5th ed., Prentice Hall, 2010

Overview

PID control (Proportional-Integral-Derivative control) is the workhorse algorithm for joint servos: the proportional term provides immediate correction, the integral term eliminates steady-state error, and the derivative term provides damping to suppress overshoot. In humanoid robots, it operates in a cascade structure—the innermost current loop (i.e., torque loop, with bandwidth in the kilohertz range), the middle velocity loop, and the outermost position loop, with inner-loop bandwidth 3–10 times higher than the outer loop layer by layer. Between textbook PID and production-ready PID lie three engineering details: anti-windup, derivative filtering, and feedforward compensation (source: Project Wiki Chapter 14 §14.2).

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

The continuous-form PID control law (source: Project Wiki Chapter 14 §14.2.2):

\[ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(s)\, ds + K_d \frac{d e(t)}{dt}, \qquad e(t) = r(t) - y(t) \]

Digital controllers sample at period \(T_s\), and in engineering the incremental form (velocity form) is more commonly used:

\[ \Delta u[k] = K_p \left(e[k] - e[k-1]\right) + K_i T_s e[k] + \frac{K_d}{T_s}\left(e[k] - 2e[k-1] + e[k-2]\right) \]

The incremental form outputs the control increment, naturally avoiding explicit accumulation in the integrator, and enables bumpless transfer between manual and automatic modes (Wiki §14.2.2).

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The vitality of PID stems from an engineering fact: most single-joint tracking problems do not require a model. The empirical tuning method given by Ziegler and Nichols in 1942 (DOI 10.1115/1.4019264) allowed PID to be deployed without a model. In humanoid robots, it is not a "low-level algorithm" but the foundation of the control stack: whether the upper layer is WBC, MPC, or RL policies, everything ultimately executes through the joint servo loop; the "PD target joint angle" in RL papers is a variant of PID with the integral term removed.

Its true value lies in hierarchical decoupling: the inner loop "flattens" dirty dynamics such as motor dynamics, friction, and backlash into an approximately ideal torque/position source, allowing upper-layer controllers to work on a clean abstraction. Understanding the limitations of PID is understanding where the assumptions of all upper-layer methods come from.

Principle Breakdown

① Cascade Control Structure (Wiki §14.2.1)

Joint servos universally adopt a three-level nested structure of current loop/velocity loop/position loop. The inner-loop bandwidth must be significantly higher than the outer loop—typically decreasing 3–10 times per outward layer—otherwise the inner and outer loop dynamics interfere. The current loop's plant is the motor's electrical dynamics: after FOC transformation, the q-axis current approximates a first-order model \(L_q \frac{di_q}{dt} = v_q - R_s i_q - k_e \omega_m\), with the electrical time constant \(L_q/R_s\) typically in the sub-millisecond range, allowing current-loop bandwidth up to several kilohertz. The joint output torque is \(\tau_j = N\eta k_t i_q\), so the current loop is essentially the torque loop.

② Reflected Inertia and the Dividing Line of Gear Ratios

The plant for the velocity/position loops is the electromechanical dynamics \(J_{eff}\,\dot\omega = \tau_j - \tau_g - \tau_f - \tau_{ext}\), where \(J_{eff} = J_m N^2 + J_l\). Note the \(N^2\): in high-ratio joints (harmonic drives with N typically 50–160), load-side inertia and disturbances are reduced by \(N^2\) when reflected to the motor side—"naturally disturbance-rejecting but naturally opaque"; QDD deliberately uses low gear ratios (typically N=6–12) for torque transparency, at the cost of the current loop directly bearing load disturbances (Wiki §14.2.1).

③ The Engineering Trio (Wiki §14.2.3)

  • Anti-windup: When the actuator saturates, the error continuously accumulates in the integrator, causing large overshoot upon desaturation. Back-calculation anti-windup feeds the saturation difference back to the integrator via a gain: \(I[k+1] = I[k] + K_i T_s e[k] + K_t T_s (u_{sat}[k] - u_{unsat}[k])\);
  • Derivative filtering: Differentiation amplifies noise and must be cascaded with a low-pass filter \(D(s) = \frac{K_d s}{1 + s/N_f}\), with filter coefficient \(N_f\) typically 5–20; derivative-on-measurement (acting only on the measured value) is also used to avoid "derivative kick" from setpoint steps;
  • Feedforward compensation: \(\tau_{cmd} = \underbrace{J_{eff}\ddot q_d + \tau_g(\hat q) + \hat\tau_f(\hat\omega)}_{\text{model feedforward}} + \underbrace{K_p e + K_d \dot e + K_i \int e}_{\text{feedback}}\)—the model feedforward handles the predictable part, and feedback only corrects residuals.

Who Uses It / Application Cases

  • The bottom layer of all joints: From Dynamixel servos (ent_component_dynamixel_xm430_w210_t) to Unitree's in-house joints (ent_oem_unitree_robotics), cascade PID is the default implementation;
  • Execution interface for RL policies: The "PD target joint angle" in humanoid RL papers is PID with the integral term removed (works such as JAEGER all output PD targets);
  • FOC current loop: ent_method_field_oriented_control together with PID forms the core of the motor servo.

Limitations and Boundaries

  • Single-input single-output: PID does not understand joint coupling; whole-body coordination in humanoids must be handled by the upper layer (WBC/MPC/policy);
  • No concept of constraints: Limits and torque saturation can only be remedied by saturation clipping and anti-windup;
  • Fixed gains: Under nonlinear conditions (varying loads, contact switching), gain scheduling or feedforward compensation is needed;
  • The integral term is dangerous in force control: Use the integral term cautiously in the torque loop to avoid slow oscillations with contact stiffness (engineering judgment).

Common Misconceptions

  1. "PID is outdated"—It is the foundation of the control stack; the more advanced the upper-layer method, the more it relies on inner-loop PID to compress joints into ideal sources.
  2. "Cranking up the three gains makes response faster"—Tuning beyond the bandwidth ratio causes cascade interference oscillations; inner-to-outer tuning with a 3–10 times bandwidth ratio is the discipline.
  3. "Anti-windup is optional"—Missing anti-windup in saturation scenarios is a catastrophic-level hazard.
  4. "Use raw error differences for the derivative"—Unfiltered differentiation amplifies noise and causes derivative kick on setpoint steps; derivative filtering plus derivative-on-measurement is the default configuration.
