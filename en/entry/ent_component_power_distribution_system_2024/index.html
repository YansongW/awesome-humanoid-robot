Overview

The power distribution system is an assembly of DC-DC converters, busbars, fuses, and contactors that safely and reliably distributes battery electrical energy to computing units, actuators, and sensors. What it truly changes is not "power supply" itself, but enabling kilowatt-level instantaneous power and dozens of low-voltage subsystems to coexist on the same robot without interfering with each other.

Content

What It Is: A Precise Definition of the Power Distribution System

The power distribution system in humanoid robots plays a dual role of energy routing and fault isolation. It consists of the following core components:

  • DC-DC converters: Step down the battery bus voltage (e.g., 48 V) to the low-voltage rails (e.g., 12 V, 5 V, 3.3 V) required by computing units and sensors.
  • Busbars: Low-impedance copper bars or PCB copper layers used to carry high currents and reduce line voltage drops.
  • Fuses: One-time protection components that interrupt the circuit by melting their fusible element during overcurrent conditions.
  • Contactors: High-current electromagnetic switches used to connect/disconnect the main circuit, typically equipped with arc-extinguishing capability.
  • Relays: Electrically controlled switches used to control high-current circuits with low-current signals.

Unlike consumer electronics, the power distribution network of a humanoid robot simultaneously carries kilowatt-level instantaneous power, dozens of low-voltage subsystems, and high-frequency PWM motor drive currents. Any misoperation or failure of a single protection device can lead to complete system shutdown or even safety incidents.

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Context

In early robot prototypes, developers often used multiple independent batteries or single regulated power supplies. This approach worked for small loads, but as the number of actuators in humanoid robots increased (e.g., Tesla Optimus Gen 3 features 25 actuators per hand, 50 actuators across both hands), three systemic pain points emerged:

  1. Uncontrolled voltage drop: Long wiring harnesses produce significant voltage drops under high currents, leading to insufficient power supply at end actuators.
  2. Fault propagation: A single short circuit could burn out an entire power rail without isolation measures.
  3. Chaotic power budgeting: Independent power supplies for each module prevent unified power allocation and monitoring.

The emergence of the power distribution system is precisely to upgrade "a pile of batteries and wires" into "a designable, calculable, and protectable energy network."

Principle Breakdown

① Voltage Drop and Wire Gauge Calculation: The Starting Point of All Distribution Design

The first principles of power distribution design are Ohm's law and Joule's law. Given a load current \(I\), wire length \(L\), and allowable voltage drop \(\Delta V\), the minimum wire cross-sectional area is determined by:

\[ A_{\text{min}} = \frac{2 \rho L I}{\Delta V_{\text{max}}} \]

where \(\rho\) is the resistivity of copper, and the factor 2 accounts for the round-trip circuit length. For example, a 48 V system allowing a 2% voltage drop (i.e., \(\Delta V_{\text{max}} = 0.96\) V), a load current of 20 A, and a one-way wire length of 2 m yields a minimum cross-sectional area of approximately 0.73 mm². In engineering practice, a standard gauge of 2.5 mm² is typically selected to provide margin (calculated from corpus data).

② Protection Coordination: Ensuring the Right Fuse Blows at the Right Time

The power distribution system is not simply about inserting fuses into circuits. Protection coordination requires that upstream and downstream protection devices operate in the correct sequence during faults, avoiding nuisance tripping. Design verification includes:

  • Fuse \(I^2t\) characteristics: The thermal integral required to melt the fuse must be greater than the \(I^2t\) of normal startup inrush but less than the withstand capability of the wire or load.
  • Breaking capacity: The maximum fault current that a protection device can safely interrupt. If the short-circuit current exceeds the breaking capacity, the fuse may explode rather than melt.

③ Hot-Swap and Inrush Suppression

Hot-swap allows batteries or modules to be replaced without shutting down the system, but the inrush current at the moment of insertion can damage connectors or trigger protection. Design measures such as pre-charge circuits or soft-start MOSFETs are required to suppress inrush.

Who Uses It: Application Cases

Power distribution systems are widely used in prototype validation, academic research, and early commercial products of humanoid robots. Typical scenarios include:

  • Tesla Optimus: Relying on its self-developed FSD vision system and high-density electric drive integration, the power distribution system must support the instantaneous power demands of 50 hand actuators and full-body joints (engineering judgment; specific distribution architecture not publicly disclosed).
  • Academic research platforms: Laboratories commonly use a 48 V bus with distributed DC-DC modules for rapid iteration.
  • Industrial inspection robots: Must comply with the UL 2580 electric vehicle battery safety standard, requiring the power distribution system to pass rigorous short-circuit and thermal runaway tests.

Limitations and Boundaries

The power distribution system is not a panacea; its design has clear physical boundaries:

  1. Wiring harness weight and volume: Larger cross-sectional areas mean heavier and thicker wires. In humanoid robots pursuing lightweight design, cables above 2.5 mm² significantly increase overall weight (engineering judgment).
  2. Thermal management coupling: Losses in the power distribution system (\(P_{loss} = I^2 R\)) ultimately convert to heat and must be incorporated into the overall thermal design. The corpus notes that computing, power, and thermal are coupled: power consumption varies with load and frequency, temperature varies with power consumption, and high temperatures in turn limit available performance.
  3. Complexity of protection coordination: Coordinating cascaded protection devices requires precise timing and \(I^2t\) matching; improper design can lead to nuisance tripping or protection blind spots.

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Bigger fuses are safer" — Wrong. The fuse rating must match the wire ampacity and load characteristics. An oversized fuse may not blow during faults, causing wire overheating and fire.
  2. "Voltage drop doesn't matter since we have regulators" — Wrong. DC-DC converters have input voltage ranges; excessive line voltage drop can cause the converter to shut down due to undervoltage, especially as battery voltage drops during discharge.
  3. "Emergency stop is just installing a switch" — Wrong. Emergency stop (E-stop) must comply with ISO 13850 or IEC 60947-5-5, requiring direct interruption of the power circuit rather than merely sending a software signal, and must consider the arc-extinguishing capability of contactors.
  4. "Hot-swap is just unplugging and plugging" — Wrong. Hot-swap without a pre-charge circuit generates inrush current that can burn out connectors or trigger protection.
