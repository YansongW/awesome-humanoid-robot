Summary

Humanoid control systems have made significant progress in recent years, yet modeling fluent interaction-rich behavior between a robot, its surrounding environment, and task-relevant objects remains a fundamental challenge. Institutions per source list: ** Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI).

Overview

ExoActor proposes a three-stage pipeline (video generation → motion estimation → motion execution) that leverages the generalization capabilities of large-scale video generation models, using third-person video generation as a unified interface for modeling humanoid robot interaction dynamics. It achieves interactive control on the Unitree G1 without task-specific data collection. The core contribution lies in combining robot-to-human embodiment transfer with task-to-action decomposition, making generated videos an executable intermediate representation of behavior, and validating end-to-end feasibility on B/A/S-level tasks.

What It Changes

Humanoid robot control has long been constrained by a fundamental contradiction: real-world interaction behaviors require joint modeling of spatial context, temporal dynamics, and task intent, while policies trained in controlled environments quickly fail when faced with new scenarios. Traditional solutions either rely on expensive data collection or require domain-specific tuning, essentially "patching" the task distribution. What ExoActor truly changes is the problem framing—it no longer attempts to make robots directly learn "state-to-action" mappings, but instead uses third-person video generation as a unified interaction modeling interface, allowing the implicit world knowledge and generalization capabilities of video generation models to bear the burden of modeling interaction dynamics. This shifts the bottleneck of task generalization from "robot data" to "video generation quality," which is precisely a capability current large-scale generative models already possess. Another subtle but important change: through robot-to-human embodiment transfer, it maps robot observations to human-like representations, thereby directly leveraging the priors of human-centric video generation models—essentially arguing that rather than training specialized generation models for robots, it is better to "disguise" the robot as a human and borrow the prior distribution of human actions.

Method Breakdown

Three-Stage Pipeline

  1. Video Generation Stage
  2. Robot-to-Human Embodiment Transfer: Uses Nano Banana Pro (Gemini 3.1 Pro) to convert robot observations into human-like representations via prompt-based image editing. Key constraints include: 1:1 degree-accurate replication of spatial posture, orientation, and kinematic configuration; front/back consistency as the highest priority; "Hobbit-style" body proportions (broad shoulders, elongated limbs, short torso, extremely thin waistline); tight-fitting dark unbranded clothing; hands naturally open.
  3. Task-to-Action Decomposition: Uses GPT-5.4 Thinking to decompose high-level instruction G into a temporally ordered action chain C = {a₁, a₂, …, a_T}, where each action is atomic, visually observable, and physically executable. Example: "Pick up the brown box in front of you and stand up" → approach → bend down → grasp → lift → stand upright.
  4. Prompt Template: Structured fields include Shot (10-second fixed static camera), Scene (environment completely unchanged from first frame), Subject (male in tight clothing), Task (execute action chain), Motion (natural, continuous, physically realistic), Execution (humanoid robot style: short, cautious, smooth, deliberate), Consistency (no scale drift, perspective distortion), End State (stable final pose). A/S-level tasks additionally include manipulation and long-horizon execution constraints.

  5. Video Generation: Uses off-the-shelf APIs, primarily Kling 3 (outperforming Veo 3.1 and Wan 2.6 in ablations).

  6. Motion Estimation Stage

  7. Whole-Body Motion: GENMO (diffusion model), formulating motion estimation as a constrained generation process conditioned on video features and 2D keypoints, outputting SMPL parameters M = {q_t, p_t}_{t=1}^T.
  8. Hand Motion: WiLoR estimates two-hand poses frame-by-frame H = {h_t^l, h_t^r}{t=1}^T, with each hand assigned a discrete interaction state S = {s_t^l, s_t^r} corresponds to open, half-open, and closed.}^T, where s ∈ {0, 1, 2

  9. Interaction-Aware Motion Representation: M̃ = {q_t, p_t, h_t^l, h_t^r, s_t^l, s_t^r}_{t=1}^T.

  10. Motion Execution Stage

  11. Uses SONIC as the motion tracking controller, taking current state s_t and reference motion time window q̂_{t:t+k} as input, without task-specific reward engineering.
  12. Hand states are mapped to Unitree Dex3-1 compatible 7-DoF joint targets, streamed via an event queue alongside SMPL body trajectories.
  13. Current implementation does not require retargeting.

Key Innovations

  1. Video Generation as a Unified Interaction Modeling Interface: Rather than treating video generation as a "visualization tool," it serves as the core representation for modeling robot-environment-object three-way interaction dynamics. This bypasses the bottleneck of "designing reward functions for each task" or "collecting task-specific demonstrations" in traditional methods, allowing the implicit physical common sense of large-scale video generation models to assume responsibility for interaction modeling.
  2. Robot-to-Human Embodiment Transfer: Maps robot observations to the human domain through strict prompt constraints (1:1 posture replication, front/back consistency, Hobbit-style body proportions), thereby borrowing priors from human-centric video generation models. This is an elegant "domain adaptation" operation—it acknowledges that current video generation models are inherently human-centric, and rather than fighting this, it exploits it.
  3. Explicit Task-to-Action Decomposition: Decomposes high-level instructions into temporally ordered, visually observable, physically executable action chains, combined with initial state information (extracted by Gemini 3.1 from the first frame) to construct spatially and task-aware prompts. This resolves the semantic gap between "high-level instructions → pixel-level video," giving generated videos task-structural interpretability.

Boundaries and Limitations

The authors explicitly acknowledge boundaries including: video generation exhibits object hallucination (e.g., a small umbrella hallucinated as a larger one), incorrect action sequences, and physically implausible end poses; motion estimation reliability decreases under occlusion, fast motion, and back-facing viewpoints; wrist motion estimation in vertical grasping scenarios often predicts horizontal orientations, indicating that fine-grained rotation is weakly constrained by visual cues. The current framework is an offline pipeline, with video generation being the primary bottleneck (13.2 s/s of video); generated videos provide open-loop motion references, the robot has no explicit environmental perception, and robustness to perceptual noise and dynamic obstacles is limited. It relies on external surveillance cameras for third-person observations, and no model has been developed to generate third-person videos from first-person perspectives. Style transfer introduces additional domain shifts that may weaken the consistency between generated motion and executable behavior. Finer-grained hand/finger states are left for future work. The paper does not report specific success rates, quantitative error metrics, or quantitative comparisons with end-to-end baselines.

Engineering Insights

When reproducing ExoActor, the first thing worth verifying is the constraint strength of the prompt template—in embodiment transfer, "front/back consistency" and "Hobbit-style body proportions" are not stylistic preferences but critical design choices ensuring the reliability of subsequent motion estimation; any deviation will cause mismatches between the human kinematics in generated videos and the actual robot configuration. The most likely pitfall lies in wrist motion estimation: in vertical grasping scenarios, the model often outputs horizontal wrist orientations, meaning that if downstream tasks involve fine manipulation, explicit validation or post-processing of wrist orientation is needed after motion estimation. Another engineering trap is retargeting—although retargeting reduces high-frequency jitter, it introduces spatial bias, which is actually harmful for tasks requiring precise spatial alignment (e.g., placing a bottle into a basket); it is recommended to keep it disabled by default. In terms of latency, video generation (13.2 s/s of video) and hand motion estimation (16.4 s/s of video) are the primary bottlenecks; parallelization or downsampling is needed if real-time performance is desired. Finally, for S-level tasks, the authors sometimes place small support bases under target objects to compensate for height errors, indicating that height calibration in the execution stage is a weak link in the current framework; a height compensation mechanism should be reserved during reproduction.
