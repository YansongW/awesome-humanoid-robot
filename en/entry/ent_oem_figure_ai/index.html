Overview

Figure AI is a US startup focused on general-purpose humanoid robots, headquartered in Sunnyvale, California, and founded in 2022. It established its industry position through the Figure 02 completing an 11-month real-world industrial validation at BMW's Spartanburg plant, handling over 90,000 parts, and is exploring the extension from industrial scenarios to home service scenarios with its self-developed Helix multimodal AI model. What it truly changes is not the mechanical performance of the robot itself, but the answer to the industrialization question of "whether humanoid robots can work stably on real production lines over the long term."

Content

What It Is: Precise Definition

Figure AI (Figure AI, Inc.) is a general-purpose humanoid robot complete machine manufacturer, belonging to the OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer) segment of the humanoid robot supply chain. Its core product lines include the Figure 02 for industrial manufacturing and the next-generation Figure 03 for home services. The company also develops its own Helix vision-language-action (VLA) model, enabling robots to learn new tasks through natural language instructions and visual demonstrations, rather than relying on pre-programmed fixed action sequences.

Figure AI's positioning differs from Boston Dynamics (known for high-dynamic movement) and Tesla (known for vertically integrated manufacturing): it chooses a gradual path of "industrial validation first, home adoption later," using real production line data to feed model iteration. The company's official website explicitly defines the Figure 03 as a "for every day" general-purpose humanoid robot, while the Helix model is positioned as the intelligence core enabling it to "navigate unpredictable, ever-changing home environments."

Why It Exists: Pain Points and Historical Positioning

The humanoid robot industry has long faced a fundamental contradiction: the gap between demonstration capability and product reliability. In 2025–2026, the global market is at a critical inflection point from "technology demonstration" to "industrial deployment," with the global market size around $2.9–3.2 billion in 2025, approximately 16,000 units installed, with China accounting for over 80%. In this context, Figure AI's significance can be understood from three dimensions:

First, solving the transition problem from "demonstration robots" to "product robots." The gap between robots that can walk and grasp in videos and robots that can work stably in factories for years manifests in four dimensions: reliability, cost, maintainability, and compliance. The 11-month deployment of Figure 02 at BMW's factory is precisely an empirical test of this transition.

Second, filling the gap between "AI algorithms" and "hardware deployment" in the US. The US leads in AI algorithms, large models, and capital markets, but manufacturing costs are high and supply chains partially depend on Asia. Figure AI chose to partner with manufacturing giants like BMW, replacing pure laboratory iteration with real-world scenario validation—a correction to America's "heavy on algorithms, light on manufacturing" weakness.

Third, exploring the commercialization path for VLA models. Unlike Tesla's reliance on FSD vision systems and NVIDIA's GR00T N1 foundation model, Figure AI develops its own Helix model, attempting to achieve end-to-end integration of "natural language understanding—visual perception—action generation." Its historical positioning is: the first humanoid robot company to prove VLA models can be commercialized using real factory data.

Principle Breakdown

① Helix Model: End-to-End High-Speed Humanoid Robot Control

Helix belongs to the vision-language-action (VLA) model family, alongside RT-2 (Google DeepMind), OpenVLA, GR00T N1 (NVIDIA), and π0 (Physical Intelligence). Its core idea is to unify vision, language, and action into a single neural network, enabling robots to understand natural language instructions and generalize to new tasks. Unlike π0's flow model architecture, Helix emphasizes "end-to-end high-speed control"—meaning sufficiently low latency from perception input to joint commands to meet real-time response requirements in real environments.

Mathematically, the policy optimization objective of a VLA model can be expressed as:

\[ J = \mathbb{E}_{p(\xi)}\left[R(\pi, \xi)\right] \]

where \(\xi\) represents environment parameters (including perturbations introduced by domain randomization), \(\pi\) is the policy, and \(R\) is the cumulative reward. Domain Randomization intentionally randomizes physical parameters such as mass, friction, damping, terrain, lighting, and texture in simulation, making the policy robust to the discrepancy between simulation and reality—this is the key technical guarantee for Helix's transition from simulation to real factories.

② Industrial Validation Closed Loop: Data Flywheel

The deployment of Figure 02 at BMW's Spartanburg plant was not simply "robots replacing humans," but a closed loop of data collection—model iteration—redeployment. Over 11 months, the robot handled over 90,000 parts and participated in producing over 30,000 BMW X3 vehicles. Every handling action and every anomaly response became training data for the Helix model. This "real-world data flywheel" is the core moat distinguishing Figure AI from purely simulation-based manufacturers.

③ Product Generational Logic: From Industrial to Home

Figure 02 targets structured scenarios such as automotive manufacturing, logistics, and warehousing, while Figure 03 is positioned as an "every day" general-purpose home robot. The logic behind this generational leap: the reliability validation in industrial scenarios (11 months, over 90,000 handling operations) provides the technical foundation for the complex unstructured environments of home scenarios. The capabilities learned by the Helix model in industrial settings—"understanding instructions, planning actions, adapting to changes"—are transferred to handle "unpredictable, ever-changing" tasks in home environments.

Who Uses It · Application Cases

① BMW Group—Automotive Manufacturing

Figure 02 completed an 11-month pilot deployment at BMW's Spartanburg plant, handling over 90,000 parts, participating in the production of over 30,000 BMW X3 vehicles, and accumulating 1,250 hours of operation. This is one of the longest-duration, highest-data-volume real deployments of humanoid robots in global automotive factories. BMW Group announced in 2026 that it will deploy humanoid robots at its German factories for the first time, with Figure AI as a key partner.

② Logistics and Warehousing

The Figure 02 product line explicitly covers logistics and warehousing scenarios. Its 168 cm height and multi-finger hand design enable it to adapt to shelving, conveyor belts, and handling tools designed for humans, without requiring modifications to existing warehousing facilities—this is the core advantage of the humanoid form factor compared to specialized robots.

③ Home Services (Figure 03, Future Scenario)

Figure 03 is positioned as a "general purpose humanoid robot for every day," with the Helix model enabling it to understand natural language instructions in home environments and handle unstructured tasks. The official website explicitly emphasizes its ability to "navigate unpredictable, ever-changing home environments," but large-scale deployment in home scenarios has not yet begun; specific timelines need to be confirmed independently.

Limitations and Boundaries

① Capacity Bottleneck

The BotQ factory's initial annual capacity is 12,000 units, with plans to expand to 100,000 units. However, compared to Tesla Optimus's million-unit capacity planning, Figure AI's capacity ramp-up speed remains conservative. Humanoid robot mass production involves production line design, process stabilization, BOM optimization, supplier locking, testing process standardization, and quality system development. As a startup, Figure AI has weaker supply chain bargaining power than Tesla.

② Scenario Boundaries

Figure 02's validation is concentrated in the structured scenario of automotive manufacturing. Parts handling in automotive factories features "fixed paths, controlled environments, and repetitive tasks," while the "unpredictable, ever-changing" nature of home scenarios demands an order of magnitude higher generalization capability from VLA models. The leap from industrial to home is not simply model transfer, but a comprehensive reconstruction of reliability, safety, and cost structure.

③ Cost and Reliability

From 2023 to 2024, humanoid robot manufacturing costs dropped 40%, with Chinese manufacturers' BOM reduced to approximately $35,000, and Unitree's R1 priced as low as $5,900. Figure AI has not disclosed its BOM cost, but as a US company, its manufacturing costs are likely higher than Chinese manufacturers. In terms of reliability, the reference MTBF for industrial robots is 60,000 hours, while the system MTBF for humanoid robots under serial architecture is far lower (engineering judgment). Figure 02's 1,250 hours of operation data, while a milestone, still falls short of the product-level requirement of "stable operation over years."

Common Misconceptions

  1. "Figure AI is Tesla's competitor"—Inaccurate. The two have different paths: Tesla bets on vertical integration and million-unit capacity, while Figure AI bets on the VLA data flywheel and scenario validation. In the short term, Figure AI is closer to "an AI company doing hardware," while Tesla is "a hardware company doing AI." Real competition may emerge in 2027–2028 when home scenarios take off.

  2. "The Helix model is just ChatGPT plus a robotic arm"—Wrong. Helix is an end-to-end VLA model where vision, language, and action are uniformly represented in the same network, rather than a simple "large model outputs commands + traditional control executes." Its technical approach is closer to π0 and GR00T N1 than to a mechanical extension of LLMs.

  3. "The 11-month BMW deployment proves humanoid robots are mature"—Overinterpretation. The validation of over 90,000 parts and over 30,000 X3 vehicles is a milestone, but the 1,250 hours of operation still lags the industrial robot reference MTBF of 60,000 hours by an order of magnitude. This is more of a "feasibility proof" than a "reliability proof."

  4. "Figure 03 will achieve home adoption upon release"—Needs caution. Figure 03 is positioned as "for every day," but reliability in home scenarios, safety certifications (such as ISO 13482), and cost control have not been disclosed. From industrial to home, there are three thresholds: compliance, cost, and user trust.
